CHAPTER 1
Systemic Stance and the Therapeutic Relationship
BIG IDEA A systemic therapist tracks reciprocal influence, context, meaning, strengths, and the therapist’s participation rather than searching for one person to blame.

Systemic practice begins with a stance, not a technique. Behavior develops within relationships, identities, developmental stages, and larger systems. This does not mean every symptom is caused by the family or that biology, trauma, and individual responsibility are ignored.

Linear and Systemic Formulation

Linear FrameSystemic Frame
“The adolescent is oppositional.”Monitoring increases when parents fear failure; secrecy increases when monitoring feels controlling.
“The partner is too needy.”Reassurance-seeking increases with distance; withdrawal increases when reassurance feels like pressure.
“The parent causes the anxiety.”Anxiety, accommodation, conflict avoidance, school stress, and family beliefs may reinforce one another.
“The therapist must convince them.”The therapist joins multiple perspectives, clarifies goals, notices power, and tests change collaboratively.
COMMON TRAP
Systemic does not mean refusing to identify danger, coercion, abuse, medical risk, or individual responsibility. Reciprocal formulation should never erase a controlling safety or ethical issue.

Theory-Practice Consistency

The therapist’s language, questions, goals, and interventions should fit the stated formulation. A reciprocal formulation followed by one-person blame is internally inconsistent. Collaboration followed by repeated persuasion is also inconsistent.

Systemic IntentionConsistent ActionInconsistent Action
Understand reciprocityAsk what happens before and after the recurring interaction.Ask only who started it.
Increase flexibilityIdentify exceptions and small shifts.Demand permanent commitment before exploring readiness.
Support shared allianceAcknowledge multiple perspectives and clarify the task.Privately side with one participant and present that view as objective.
Respect contextAsk how identity, access, and outside systems affect meaning.Assume the therapist’s preferred family pattern is universal.

The CIRCLE Systemic Formulation Grid

StepQuestion to Ask
C - Clarify the client systemWho is participating, who is affected, and what treatment unit is being considered?
I - Identify reciprocal influenceWhat sequence repeats, and how does each response influence the next?
R - Recognize context and powerWhat culture, hierarchy, privilege, oppression, development, access, or external system matters?
C - Connect symptom and relationshipHow does the presenting problem function within the system without assuming the system caused every symptom?
L - Locate leverage and strengthsWhere are flexibility, exceptions, resilience, motivation, and safer points of intervention?
E - Evaluate therapist participationHow might the therapist’s stance, language, values, or emotional response influence the system?

Case Lab 1: From Blame to Pattern

CASE LAB
The Martins seek therapy because 15-year-old Noah has stopped completing homework. His parents describe him as lazy and disrespectful. Noah says they check the grade portal several times a day, lecture him nightly, and remove privileges before asking what happened. He hides assignments and says he feels watched.
  • Write a linear formulation and replace it with a reciprocal systemic formulation.
  • Who is the current client system and treatment unit?
  • What strengths or exceptions could become leverage points?
  • What therapist response would reinforce blame?
MODEL REASONING
A systemic formulation might describe a monitoring-withdrawal cycle: the parents increase surveillance when they fear failure; Noah hides and disengages when monitoring feels controlling; secrecy then confirms the parents’ fear. The therapist can validate concern and autonomy, clarify shared goals, and test one small change in the cycle rather than deciding who is the problem.

  • Describe sequences rather than character defects.
  • Match the intervention to the formulation.
  • Name the treatment unit before deciding who participates.
  • Include context, strengths, and therapist influence.
  • Do not use circularity to minimize coercion, abuse, or acute risk.
CHAPTER 2
Joining, Safety, Inclusion, and Client-System Participation
BIG IDEA
Alliance is an active, co-created process. Attend to safety, voluntariness, access, multiple perspectives, and role clarity before pushing disclosure or technique.

Joining is more than being warm. It is the ongoing work of understanding how each participant experiences treatment, the therapist, and the other members of the client system. Strong alliance with one person can coexist with mistrust from another.

Alliance Controls

ControlWhat the Therapist Monitors
SafetyCan each person participate without retaliation, intimidation, humiliation, or pressured disclosure?
VoluntarinessWho chose treatment, who is pressured to attend, and what choices remain?
Role clarityWho is the client, what is the task, and what confidentiality and communication expectations apply?
AccessAre language, disability, technology, transportation, literacy, scheduling, or financial barriers limiting participation?
Multiple alliancesHow does each participant experience the therapist’s fairness, understanding, and responsiveness?
Pace and readinessIs the therapist moving faster than the system can tolerate or slower than safety and treatment needs require?
FALSE NEUTRALITY
Balanced alliance does not require treating all behavior as equally safe or ethical. The therapist may need to name coercion, protect a vulnerable participant, or set a boundary while remaining respectful and transparent.

The ALLIANCE Participation Map

StepAlliance Control
A - AttendAttend to safety, voluntariness, access, and readiness before demanding disclosure.
L - LocateLocate each participant’s goals, concerns, relationship to treatment, and perception of the therapist.
L - ListenListen for verbal, nonverbal, cultural, and power-based signals of trust, exclusion, fear, or disengagement.
I - InviteInvite balanced participation without assuming equal speaking time is always safe or appropriate.
A - AlignAlign roles, expectations, information boundaries, and the purpose of the current session.
N - NoticeNotice the therapist’s pull to rescue, side, confront, avoid, persuade, or control.

Case Lab 2: Adolescent and Parent Participation

CASE LAB
Twelve-year-old Maya attends an intake with both parents after school refusal. Her parents answer every question and say the therapist must “make her talk.” Maya gives one-word answers and looks toward her father before responding. When the therapist asks to meet briefly with Maya, the father says, “We are paying, so there are no secrets.”
  • What alliance and safety questions should be addressed first?
  • How can roles and information expectations be clarified?
  • What would balanced participation look like?
  • Which response would pressure Maya rather than co-create the alliance?
MODEL REASONING
The therapist should not force disclosure. The first task is to clarify treatment structure, safety, voluntariness, information expectations, and the purpose of individual and family participation. The therapist can acknowledge parental concern, explain that effective treatment requires understanding each perspective, and ask Maya what would make participation easier.

CHAPTER 3
Integrating Individual and Systemic Practice
BIG IDEA
Individual interventions can support systemic therapy when they remain connected to the treatment unit, reciprocal pattern, goals, autonomy, and cross-level effects.

Breathing practice, cognitive work, trauma stabilization, medical coordination, and skills training may fit within systemic treatment. The question is whether the therapist understands how the individual intervention affects the larger system and whether the treatment structure remains clear.

The LEVELS Integration Matrix

LevelQuestions Before Intervening
L - Learner/client needWhat immediate symptom, skill, safety, health, or functioning need is being addressed?
E - Existing relational patternHow do others respond, and how might the pattern change?
V - Values and contextWhat culture, beliefs, identity, development, access, or external system shapes meaning?
E - Engagement and treatment unitWho is the client for this work, who needs to know the plan, and who participates now?
L - Link to goalsHow does the intervention advance the agreed systemic goals?
S - Scan effectsWhat unintended alliance shift, secrecy, or role confusion could result?

When Individual Work Supports Systemic Treatment

Appropriate FitPotential Risk
Teach regulation so a couple can slow an escalation cycle.Make one partner solely responsible for managing both people.
Meet individually to assess safety or gather information that cannot be obtained safely conjointly.Create hidden alliances or unclear information expectations.
Support trauma stabilization before highly activating family dialogue.Use stabilization as indefinite avoidance of relational work.
Coordinate medical evaluation when symptoms may have biological contributors.Assume biology makes relational context irrelevant.
Build adolescent coping while parents change accommodation and conflict patterns.Define the adolescent as the only problem.
TREATMENT-UNIT CONTROL
Before changing format or adding participants, clarify purpose, role, information boundaries, and effect on the existing treatment agreement.

Case Lab 3: Individual Skill Within Couple Treatment

CASE LAB
A couple seeks therapy for escalating arguments. One partner experiences panic during conflict and asks for breathing skills. The other says, “So I am the problem again because I make them anxious.”
  • How can the therapist teach a skill without abandoning the systemic formulation?
  • What should be said about the treatment unit and shared goals?
  • What cross-level effects should be monitored?
  • Which intervention would assign all responsibility to one partner?
MODEL REASONING
The therapist can explain that regulation practice is one part of changing the escalation cycle, not proof that one partner is the problem. Both can learn to recognize activation, pause safely, and return to the conversation. The therapist should monitor whether one partner becomes the designated regulator.

CHAPTER 4
Culture, Power, Social Justice, and Relational Trust
BIG IDEA
Culturally responsive systemic practice examines power, access, identity, meaning, and external systems while asking this client system—not a stereotype—what those factors mean for treatment.

Culture shapes who is allowed to speak, what counts as a problem, how help is viewed, who carries authority, what risks disclosure creates, and whether the therapist is experienced as trustworthy. Social location includes identities and conditions such as race, ethnicity, gender, sexuality, ability, age, religion, immigration, language, class, geography, and institutional position.

CULTURAL HUMILITY
The therapist does not need every cultural answer in advance. The therapist does need to recognize limits, ask respectfully, notice power, correct assumptions, and adapt care when the client system signals poor fit.

Case Lab 4: Cultural Rupture and Therapist Authority

CASE LAB
During couple therapy, the therapist repeatedly asks Sofia whether immigration stress explains her mistrust of her spouse. Sofia says, “You keep making everything about where I was born. It feels like you already decided who I am.” The therapist intended to be culturally responsive and feels embarrassed.
  • What power and alliance signals should be recognized?
  • What would a defensive response sound like?
  • How can impact be acknowledged without asking Sofia to comfort the therapist?
  • What specific change should follow?
MODEL REASONING
The therapist should recognize a rupture and the possibility that repeated questions have stereotyped Sofia. A useful response acknowledges impact, asks what the therapist missed, and changes the inquiry rather than defending good intent. Future questions should be grounded in Sofia’s meaning.

CHAPTER 5
Self of the Therapist, Competence, Boundaries, and Consultation
BIG IDEA
Therapist reactions are clinically relevant data, not automatic truth. Competent practice requires noticing activation, limiting harm, obtaining support, and preventing therapist needs from controlling treatment.

Every therapist brings history, values, identity, beliefs, strengths, and vulnerabilities into the room. Self of the therapist becomes a problem when those factors distort assessment, narrow empathy, intensify rescue or avoidance, or shift the work toward the therapist’s emotional needs.

The SELF Therapist-Reactivity Check

StepRepair Question
S - SignalWhat emotion, bodily response, urge, judgment, or repeated interaction signals activation?
E - ExamineIs the reaction clinical data, personal history, bias, fear, values conflict, role confusion, or a combination?
L - Limit harmWhat action should pause or change so the therapist does not shame, rescue, retaliate, or over-disclose?
F - Find supportIs self-reflection sufficient, or are supervision, consultation, training, referral, or a boundary change required?

Common Therapist Pulls

PullPossible RiskCorrective Question
RescueOverfunctioning, dependency, blurred roles, or removing choice.Am I building capacity or relieving my discomfort by taking over?
Prove expertiseLecturing, defending, or dismissing feedback.What information am I avoiding by trying to be right?
Side with one personAlliance split or enactment of the client-system pattern.Whose perspective or safety have I stopped exploring?
Avoid conflictFailure to address coercion, risk, boundaries, or nonresponse.What clinically necessary conversation am I delaying?
OveridentifyAssumptions based on similarity and boundary expansion.What am I assuming because this client reminds me of myself?
Values conflictShame, persuasion, premature referral, or selective empathy.Can I provide competent, respectful care, and what support is needed?

Competence and Consultation

Consultation is not a ritual answer, and referral is not automatically safest. Identify the actual competence gap and urgency. Focused consultation may be enough for one novel issue; training or supervision may be needed for a new modality; transfer may be necessary when safe and effective care cannot be provided within a reasonable period.

SituationLikely Response
Uncommon ethical question within an otherwise familiar case.Obtain qualified consultation, verify standards, document, and continue if care remains competent.
Specialized trauma method without training.Pause the method, obtain training or supervision, and use care within competence; refer if needed.
Client values trigger strong therapist judgment.Use SELF, seek consultation, monitor behavior, and assess whether respectful care can continue.
Repeated errors despite consultation.Reassess competence, narrow scope, obtain more intensive support, or arrange transfer.
Immediate safety action is clearly required.Do not use consultation to delay the supported action.

Care Versus Boundary Drift

Caring ActionBoundary-Drift Risk
One clinically justified crisis extension, documented.Repeated extensions because the therapist feels guilty ending.
Resource information and help evaluating options.Becoming transportation, housing, financial, or personal rescue system.
Purposeful, limited self-disclosure serving the client.Disclosure for reassurance, closeness, admiration, or therapist relief.
Appropriate communication or scheduling adjustment.Private channels or secret exceptions that increase dependency.
Culturally responsive flexibility.Using flexibility to ignore power, role clarity, or exploitation risk.
BOUNDARY DECISION RULE
Ask whose need the action serves, what power difference exists, what alternatives are available, whether the action can be explained transparently, and what dependency or exploitation risk it creates.

Case Lab 5A: Therapist Values and Infidelity

CASE LAB
Jordan has strong personal beliefs about fidelity. A client is uncertain whether to remain after an affair and wants help clarifying options. Jordan becomes impatient, repeatedly emphasizes reconciliation, and feels relieved when the client agrees to another couple session.
  • What signals suggest a values conflict?
  • What should Jordan pause or change?
  • When is consultation sufficient, and when might referral be needed?
  • What would therapist-centered persuasion look like?
MODEL REASONING
Jordan should recognize impatience, relief, and persuasion as data about activation—not evidence that reconciliation is correct. Jordan should restore client decision-making, explore the client’s values and safety, and seek consultation. Referral should not simply avoid discomfort, but may be needed if balanced care cannot be maintained.

Case Lab 5B: Boundary Drift and Rescue

CASE LAB
A therapist begins driving a client to appointments, extending sessions without charge, using a personal phone for frequent late-night contact, and contacting landlords as though the therapist were the client’s case manager. The therapist says, “No one else will help.”
  • Which actions now create role, dependency, or exploitation risk?
  • What should happen next rather than abrupt withdrawal?
  • Which needs require referral or coordinated resources?
  • What consultation and documentation are appropriate?
MODEL REASONING
The therapist should recognize rescue, role expansion, and dependency risk. The next step is not sudden abandonment or continued overfunctioning. Obtain consultation, clarify role and communication, coordinate appropriate resources with consent, develop a paced boundary repair, and document the plan.

CHAPTER 6
Feedback, Rupture Repair, Alliance Evaluation, and Documentation
BIG IDEA
Alliance strain is treatment data. Effective repair requires curiosity, therapist accountability, a specific change, and follow-up—not defensiveness, blame, or an apology with no behavioral adjustment.

Ruptures may be obvious, such as anger or direct criticism, or subtle, such as repeated agreement without engagement, silence, missed sessions, sudden compliance, or an alliance split. Recognize the signal before labeling the client resistant or unmotivated.

Rupture Markers

MarkerPossible Alliance Meaning
Withdrawal or silenceFeeling unsafe, misunderstood, pressured, excluded, or uncertain about the therapist’s role.
Hostility or challengeDisagreement about goals, prior injury, stereotype, power struggle, or need for clearer limits.
Compliance without engagementFear of disagreement, coercion, low fit, or belief that feedback is unwelcome.
Missed sessionsPractical barrier, ambivalence, low relevance, financial strain, rupture, or crisis.
One participant speaks for allHierarchy, anxiety, cultural pattern, control, or uncertainty about who can safely speak.
Therapist feels defensive or helplessPossible enactment, competence limit, values conflict, countertransference, or nonresponse.

The REPAIR Alliance-Rupture Sequence

StepAction
R - RecognizeRecognize the rupture signal rather than labeling resistance.
E - ExploreExplore the client system’s experience, including safety, culture, power, and prior injury.
P - Practice accountabilityOwn the therapist’s contribution without asking the client to provide emotional care.
A - AlignAlign again on goals, roles, pace, expectations, and what would make treatment workable.
I - ImplementMake a specific change in stance, communication, participation, or plan.
R - ReviewReview whether trust, fit, and progress improved; document response and next step.

Direct Alliance Feedback

QuestionPurpose
“What felt useful or not useful today?”Invites evaluation without requiring personal criticism of the therapist.
“Did I understand the problem as you experience it?”Checks formulation fit and meaning.
“Who felt included or left out?”Checks multiple alliances and participation.
“Was the pace too fast, too slow, or unsafe?”Checks readiness and relational safety.
“What should I do differently next session?”Turns feedback into an observable adjustment.

Symptom Progress and Alliance Feedback

Data PatternClinical Interpretation
Symptoms improve; alliance strongContinue while monitoring maintenance and client-defined goals.
Symptoms improve; alliance strainedDo not assume outcome erases relational harm; explore impact and sustainability.
Symptoms unchanged; alliance strongRevisit formulation, intervention fit, medical/context factors, and goals.
Symptoms worsen; alliance strainedAssess safety and level of care, repair what is possible, and consider consultation or referral.
Participants disagree about progressClarify whose goals are measured; do not let the most powerful voice define success.

Documentation Without Defensiveness

IncludeAvoid
Relevant client feedback and observable behavior.Labels such as “resistant” without facts.
Assessment of alliance, safety, participation, and fit.A long defense of therapist intent.
Relational intervention and specific adjustment.Private self-analysis that does not affect care.
Consultation obtained and how it informed the plan.Writing as if responsibility transferred to the consultant.
Client response, revised goals, and follow-up.“Rupture resolved” without evidence or next steps.

Case Lab 6: Alliance Split in Couple Therapy

CASE LAB
Alex says the therapist always protects Sam from accountability. Sam says the therapist pushes too hard and “joins Alex against me.” The therapist feels accused and wants to explain that equal time has been given to both.
  • What rupture signals are present?
  • Why is proving equal speaking time incomplete?
  • What should be explored before another intervention?
  • What specific change could be tested and reviewed?
MODEL REASONING
The therapist should recognize a multiple-alliance rupture and resist defending fairness through timekeeping. Explore each partner’s experience, assess power and safety, acknowledge any therapist contribution, revise participation or accountability language, and ask for feedback after the change. Repair is evaluated by response and fit, not intention.

  • Review outcome and document feedback, action, response, and next step.
