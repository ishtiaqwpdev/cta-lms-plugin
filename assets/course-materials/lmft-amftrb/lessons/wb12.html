How to Use This Workbook

Workbook 12 is the program's primary Domain 6 workbook and closes the 12-workbook instructional sequence. It trains ordered judgment when ethics, law, consent, confidentiality, privilege, records, billing, business practice, jurisdiction, teletherapy, digital systems, recording, transcription, or artificial intelligence changes the clinical decision. Begin by identifying the client and treatment unit, immediate safety, therapist role, location and jurisdiction, and the specific decision. Then separate governing authorities, clarify information rights and consent, select the least-disclosing protective action, preserve human judgment, and document the rationale and follow-through.

LEARN -> ORGANIZE -> APPLY -> ANALYZE -> REPAIR Study the six chapters in order. Use COMPASS, CONSENT, RECORD, TRUST, TELESAFE, and HUMAN to organize decisions. Complete all eight case labs and the reusable decision record. Answer all ten formative questions before reading the rationales, then remediate every miss or low-confidence response. Mnemonics organize judgment; they do not replace direct safety assessment, current jurisdictional law, enforceable ethics, licensing-board guidance, organizational policy, payer rules, legal consultation, security analysis, vendor review, or independent clinical judgment.
CLIENT-PROTECTION, LAW, AND HUMAN-JUDGMENT OVERRIDE
Immediate danger, abuse, acute impairment, unlawful practice, privacy or security failure, active breach, corrupted documentation, or unsafe automated output requires direct assessment, protection, correction, consultation, escalation, and continuity before routine business, technology, teletherapy, or AI workflow. Technology never replaces the therapist's treatment decision or professional accountability.

Workbook Learning Objectives

  • Apply a documented ethical decision process that integrates client welfare, systemic context, applicable law and regulation, enforceable ethics, professional role, competence, consultation, alternatives, consequences, and accountability.
  • Identify the client and treatment unit, clarify third-party and legal-system roles, and obtain understandable, voluntary, appropriately authorized, and ongoing informed consent for services and material changes.
  • Distinguish confidentiality, privilege, authorization, permitted and required disclosures, minimum-necessary practice, and systemic-record access while protecting each person's information and current safety.
  • Create, correct, safeguard, release, retain, transfer, and dispose of accurate clinical and financial records using current law, ethics, organizational policy, access rights, breach duties, and continuity planning.
  • Manage fees, billing, payer representations, collections, business arrangements, advertising, testimonials, public statements, conflicts, boundaries, and professional roles with transparency and integrity.
  • Determine whether teletherapy is legally authorized, clinically appropriate, accessible, private, secure, and operationally safe for the client's present location, needs, technology, and emergency context.
  • Evaluate electronic records, cloud services, communication tools, portals, social media, recordings, transcription, tracking technology, and vendors for data flow, access, security, consent, documentation, and breach implications.
  • Use AI-assisted tools only for a defined purpose and within competence, with data minimization, privacy and security controls, appropriate transparency, bias and accuracy testing, human review, correction, documentation, and retained clinical responsibility.

High-Yield Professional-Practice Distinctions

DistinctionDecision rule
Law vs. ethics vs. policySeparate each source. Current law and regulation govern legal duties; enforceable ethics govern professional membership or conduct; policy and contracts organize a setting but do not erase higher duties.
Confidentiality vs. privilegeConfidentiality governs professional handling of information; privilege concerns whether protected communications may be compelled in a legal proceeding and is defined by current jurisdictional law.
Consent vs. authorizationConsent permits services after understandable disclosure and voluntary agreement; authorization permits a specified use or disclosure of information and must satisfy current requirements.
Clinical record vs. psychotherapy notesDo not assume every note category has identical content, access, disclosure, storage, or payer rules. Verify the actual record type and governing authority.
Subpoena vs. court orderDo not disclose automatically. Verify validity, authority, privilege, notice, objections, protective options, and the least information lawfully required.
Consultation vs. legal adviceClinical or ethics consultation can organize judgment; qualified legal counsel interprets law. The therapist still identifies the decision and documents responsibility.
Teletherapy platform fit vs. licensure authorityA secure platform does not establish permission to practice in a location, and legal authority does not prove clinical, privacy, access, or emergency fit.
AI assistance vs. professional judgmentAutomation may support a bounded task; the therapist verifies inputs and outputs, manages bias and data risk, corrects errors, and remains responsible for every decision and record.

CHAPTER 1

Ethical Decision-Making, Jurisdiction, Role, Competence, and Boundaries
BIG IDEA A defensible professional-practice answer identifies the client system, safety, role, current jurisdiction, governing authorities, competence, conflicts, options, consequences, consultation needs, selected action, and documented follow-through.

The COMPASS Sequence

StepProfessional task
C - Clarify client, treatment unit, decision, role, and affected partiesIdentify who receives the service, who requested it, who holds rights or authority, the therapist's role, the exact decision, and whose welfare or information may be affected.
O - Observe immediate safety and rightsAssess danger, abuse, coercion, acute impairment, unlawful practice, privacy failure, access barriers, emergencies, and other conditions that change the order of action.
M - Map law, regulation, ethics, policy, contracts, and jurisdictionSeparate authorities; identify the client and therapist locations, governing board or court, organizational rules, payer requirements, and the current source that controls each issue.
P - Probe competence, conflicts, options, and consequencesExamine skill, impairment, role incompatibility, multiple relationships, exploitation risk, least-restrictive alternatives, foreseeable harm, and effects on every person in the client system.
A - Ask for consultation when neededSeek appropriately qualified clinical, ethics, risk-management, security, supervisory, or legal consultation without treating consultation as a transfer of responsibility.
S - Select the most protective lawful actionChoose the option that best protects client welfare, rights, autonomy, privacy, continuity, and professional integrity at the tested decision point.
S - Support and document follow-throughRecord facts, sources, reasoning, client participation, information used or disclosed, actions, responsible parties, continuity, outcome checks, and the next review point.

Authority, Role, and Competence Checks

  • Jurisdiction is a fact question, not a guess. Verify where the client and therapist are located and which authority governs the service, record, disclosure, emergency, or legal proceeding.
  • Organizational instructions and payer contracts matter, but they do not automatically override client welfare, law, regulation, enforceable ethics, or professional judgment.
  • Clarify therapy, evaluation, consultation, supervision, expert, fact-witness, mandated-service, and third-party-request roles before accepting incompatible expectations.
  • Competence includes the clinical problem, systemic population, modality, technology, documentation method, legal context, and the ability to use the specific tool safely.
  • A multiple relationship is not resolved by naming it. Assess power, exploitation, impaired judgment, foreseeable conflict, alternatives, safeguards, and documentation.

Case Lab 1: Organizational Pressure and Ethical Conflict

An agency administrator directs every therapist to send full progress notes to a funding partner whenever the partner asks, even when the request lacks a clear authorization or other verified basis. The administrator says the agency contract requires cooperation and warns that refusal could threaten the program. A therapist serves a couple whose record contains sensitive information from both partners.

DECISION POINT
What should the therapist do before disclosing or refusing outright?
MODEL REASONING
Use COMPASS. Clarify the client system, the request, the therapist's role, and immediate consequences; identify the current jurisdiction and separate law, regulation, ethics, organizational policy, and contract terms; verify authority and information limits; consult qualified resources; identify the least-disclosing lawful option; communicate the conflict responsibly; protect continuity; and document the sources, decision, and follow-through. Neither blanket compliance nor unsupported refusal completes the analysis.

CHAPTER 2

Informed Consent, Confidentiality, Privilege, and Information Decisions
BIG IDEA
Consent is an understandable, voluntary, documented, and continuing process. Information decisions require separate analysis of confidentiality, privilege, authorization, legal permission or mandate, systemic rights, safety, purpose, recipient, and minimum necessary disclosure.

The CONSENT Sequence

StepProfessional task
C - Confirm client, treatment unit, capacity, and legal authorityIdentify each participant, the client or clients, decision-making capacity, legally authorized persons, third-party roles, and who can consent to services or authorize information use.
O - Outline service, roles, fees, risks, benefits, alternatives, and technologyExplain what will occur, who performs each role, expected benefits and burdens, financial arrangements, records, communication, technology, and feasible alternatives.
N - Name confidentiality and privilege limitsExplain systemic policies, legal requests, mandated or permitted disclosures, emergencies, consultation, records, payer or third-party access, and how privilege may be asserted or waived under current law.
S - Secure voluntary understandable agreement and required authorizationUse language and supports the person can understand; assess undue influence; obtain the form of consent or authorization required for the specific act.
E - Explain information use and disclosureIdentify information, purpose, recipient, legal or authorized basis, scope, expiration or revocation rules, and the least amount necessary for the task.
N - Note the processDocument participants, capacity and authority, disclosures, questions, preferences, agreements, refusals, authorizations, and unresolved issues.
T - Track changes and renew consentRevisit consent when the treatment unit, goals, roles, fees, technology, recording, data use, risks, location, or legal circumstances materially change.

Information-Decision Questions

QuestionWhat it prevents
Who is the client and who owns the decision?Assuming the requester, payer, parent, partner, court, or organization automatically controls services or information.
What information is at issue?Treating all records, psychotherapy notes, billing data, messages, recordings, and third-party information as identical.
What is the purpose and recipient?Overdisclosure and reuse of information for a purpose that was not explained or authorized.
What current authority permits or requires action?Confusing consent, authorization, privilege, subpoena, court order, emergency exception, and organizational preference.
What is the least-disclosing effective response?Releasing an entire record when a limited response, objection, protective process, or focused disclosure may satisfy the lawful purpose.
What must be documented and revisited?Losing the reasoning chain when circumstances, relationships, location, safety, or legal authority changes.

Case Lab 2: Conjoint Consent and Private Information

At the start of couple therapy, the therapist used a written policy stating that the treatment unit is the couple and that private communications may affect what can be kept from the other partner. After several sessions, one partner privately discloses a financial account and demands absolute secrecy. The other partner later asks whether the therapist is withholding information relevant to shared financial goals.

DECISION POINT
How should the therapist respond before revealing, promising secrecy, or continuing unchanged?
MODEL REASONING
Use CONSENT. Recheck who the client is, the existing policy, each person's understanding, current law and ethics, the meaning and safety context of the disclosure, and whether any prior commitment can lawfully and ethically be honored. Do not disclose automatically or invent a new policy after the fact. Clarify options, limits, foreseeable consequences, and the conditions for continuing treatment; seek consultation when needed; preserve each person's information rights; and document the process.

CHAPTER 3

Documentation, Records, Access, Disclosure, Retention, and Practice Transition
BIG IDEA A record must be accurate, adequate, attributable, protected, correctable, retrievable, and governed across its lifecycle. Systemic records require special attention to each person's information, access authority, third-party material, release limits, and continuity.

The RECORD Sequence

StepProfessional task
R - Record relevant facts, sources, observations, and participationDistinguish direct observation, client report, collateral information, measure results, automated output, and therapist inference. Identify who participated and the context.
E - Explain clinical reasoning and decisionsConnect assessment, systemic hypotheses, risk, intervention, response, plan, consultation, information decisions, and continuity without copying irrelevant material.
C - Correct errors transparently without obscuring the recordPreserve chronology and the original entry as required, identify the correction and source, notify affected workflows when needed, and assess downstream harm.
O - Organize clinical and financial content accuratelyKeep diagnoses, codes, services, dates, fees, payer representations, authorizations, and treatment documentation truthful and internally consistent.
R - Respect access, authorization, privilege, and third-party informationVerify who requests access, the record type, authority, systemic permissions, legal process, harm standard, and information belonging to or identifying others.
D - Control storage, disclosure, retention, disposal, breach, and continuityApply current safeguards, retention and destruction rules, incident response, transfer procedures, and plans for incapacity, death, sale, relocation, or closure.

The Record Lifecycle

StageHigh-yield questions
CreateIs the entry timely, accurate, adequate, clinically relevant, attributed to its source, and consistent with the service actually delivered?
UseWho needs the information for what purpose, and is the use clinically necessary, authorized, permitted, secure, and within role?
CorrectWhat is wrong, what is the reliable source, how is chronology preserved, who may rely on the error, and what downstream repair is required?
Access / releaseWho requests what record, with what authority, under which current rule, and how will third-party and systemic information be protected?
Store / retainWhich current law, board rule, contract, and policy govern format, access controls, backup, auditability, retention period, and recovery?
Transition / disposeHow will clients receive notice, continuity, access, transfer, secure disposal, and a responsible records custodian during practice change?

Protect information belonging to or identifying others, preserve corrected-record history, avoid reflexive full release or blanket denial, consult when needed, respond within governing timelines, and document the request, analysis, action, and continuity.

CHAPTER 4

Financial Integrity, Billing, Business Practice, Advertising, and Public Roles
BIG IDEA
Trust in professional practice depends on truthful services, records, fees, coding, claims, collections, referrals, credentials, specialties, testimonials, public statements, role boundaries, and prompt correction of misinformation.

The TRUST Sequence

StepProfessional task
T - Tell clients financial and role terms clearlyExplain fees, missed-session charges, payer limits, collection procedures, role, third-party relationships, changes, records implications, and feasible choices in understandable writing.
R - Represent credentials, services, specialties, and outcomes accuratelyUse truthful titles, licensure and supervision status, education, competence, evidence, limitations, affiliations, expected results, and public claims.
U - Use truthful billing, coding, and payer communicationMatch codes, dates, participants, diagnoses, duration, modality, and service descriptions to what actually occurred and correct errors promptly.
S - Safeguard boundaries, conflicts, referrals, collections, testimonials, and public rolesAssess power, undue influence, exploitation, confidentiality, remuneration, incompatible roles, business incentives, and the least harmful alternative.
T - Track changes, corrections, and continuityDocument disclosures, agreements, rationale, consultation, corrections, client notice, responsible parties, and what protects ongoing access to records and care.

Business and Public-Practice Decision Rules

  • The clinical record and the claim must tell the same truthful story. A payer preference does not justify a diagnosis, code, duration, participant list, or service description that did not occur.
  • Financial policies belong in informed participation before a dispute. Changes require reasonable notice and current-law review; collection does not authorize unnecessary clinical disclosure.
  • Referral relationships require scrutiny for remuneration, conflicts, steering, quality, access, client choice, and accurate representation.
  • Current clients and other people vulnerable to undue influence should not be used to create promotional credibility. Protect confidentiality even when the client volunteers praise.
  • A specialty label must follow education, training, supervised experience, or other defensible competence development - not marketing demand.
  • Therapy and forensic or evaluative roles carry different purposes, information uses, consent, neutrality expectations, and conflicts. Clarify and separate them before acting.

Case Lab 4: Billing, Collections, and Truthful Representation

What should the therapist do first and what must follow?

Limit collection disclosures to what is permitted and necessary, protect confidentiality, seek qualified consultation, communicate the concern and corrective path, preserve records, correct any affected claims, protect clients from retaliation or abandonment, and document the decision and follow-through.

CHAPTER 5

Technology, Teletherapy, Electronic Records, Digital Privacy, and Security
BIG IDEA
Safe teletherapy requires more than a functioning video link. Verify current locations and authority, identity, clinical fit, access, privacy, consent, emergency resources, communication and backup plans, platform and vendor safeguards, documentation, incident response, and ongoing fit.

The TELESAFE Sequence

StepProfessional task
T - Track client and therapist location and jurisdictionConfirm physical locations at each relevant contact and identify the current authority governing practice, consent, records, emergency response, and required reporting.
E - Evaluate authority, clinical fit, access, privacy, and technologyDetermine whether the service is lawful and appropriate for risk, symptoms, relationships, communication, disability, language, digital literacy, device, bandwidth, and environment.
L - Locate identity, emergency contacts, and local resourcesVerify who is present, the service location, emergency contact permissions, local crisis and emergency resources, and the response pathway if connection or safety deteriorates.
E - Explain risks, benefits, alternatives, communication, and backup plansUse written and verbal consent that addresses technology risks, privacy, interruptions, asynchronous communication, recording, fees, alternatives, and what happens if teletherapy is not workable.
S - Secure platform, devices, records, and vendorsMap information flow, access, encryption, authentication, storage, retention, deletion, business-associate or vendor obligations, tracking technology, updates, and staff practices.
A - Address barriersModify access, privacy, sensory, cognitive, language, financial, location, device, scheduling, or support barriers and offer feasible alternatives.
F - Follow emergency and breach proceduresAct on risk, disconnection, unauthorized access, lost devices, misdirected messages, corrupted records, tracking disclosures, and other incidents under current procedures.
E - Evaluate and document ongoing fitReassess benefit, risk, privacy, location, access, technology, client preference, continuity, and needed changes over time.

Technology Is a Data-Flow Decision

LayerQuestions before use
Client settingWho can see or hear? Who controls the device or account? Is coercion, surveillance, shared access, or lack of privacy affecting participation?
Therapist settingAre screen, audio, paper, device, network, authentication, updates, storage, backups, and staff access protected?
Platform / vendorWhat data is created, received, maintained, transmitted, retained, trained on, sold, or shared? What contracts, safeguards, subprocessors, and incident duties apply?
CommunicationWhich channels are approved for scheduling, clinical content, emergencies, files, and after-hours messages? What is the backup when a channel fails?
Record systemWhere does information become the clinical or financial record, who can amend it, what audit trail exists, and how are access, retention, export, and deletion handled?
Incident responseWho contains the problem, preserves evidence, assesses affected information and people, makes required notifications, corrects records, and protects continuity?

Case Lab 6: Client Travels During Teletherapy

A client joins a scheduled session from a hotel in an unexpected jurisdiction. The client is whispering because a partner is nearby, reports escalating suicidal thoughts, and the video connection repeatedly fails. MODEL REASONING
Use TELESAFE. Verify the client's exact current location and identity, assess immediate risk and privacy, determine whether the therapist is authorized to practice there, identify local emergency resources and a safe communication method, explain the limits and options, activate emergency or continuity procedures as indicated, and document. Do not let jurisdiction verification delay urgent protection, and do not let urgency erase the need for location, privacy, and lawful continuity planning.

CHAPTER 6

Artificial Intelligence, Recording, Transcription, Data Governance, and Human Accountability
BIG IDEA AI, recording, transcription, and automation may support a defined task, but they also create information, vendor, consent, bias, accuracy, security, and downstream-action risks. The therapist remains responsible for what enters the record and what affects treatment.

The HUMAN Sequence

StepProfessional task
H - Hold the therapist responsible for every decision and recordDo not delegate diagnosis, risk judgment, treatment choice, documentation accuracy, disclosure, correction, or continuity accountability to a tool or vendor.
U - Understand purpose, data flow, vendor terms, retention, and affected peopleDefine the exact task, users, inputs, outputs, locations, access, subprocessors, training use, ownership, retention, deletion, security, incident process, and people who may be affected.
M - Minimize sensitive data and accessUse the smallest amount of information, fewest permissions, shortest justified retention, safest configuration, and least risky workflow that can accomplish the legitimate purpose.
A - Ask whether notice, consent, recording authorization, or alternative choice is requiredVerify current law and ethics, explain risks and choices understandably, protect voluntariness, and provide a feasible non-AI or non-recorded pathway when required or clinically appropriate.
N - Notice bias, inaccuracy, fabrication, and unequal impactTest outputs against source data, clinical context, systemic hypotheses, identity and access effects, false certainty, omissions, hallucinations, and performance across affected groups.
R - Review, correct, document, and monitor before useHuman-review every output that may affect care or records, correct the full downstream chain, document the review and rationale, monitor impact, and stop use when safeguards fail.

AI and Automation Risk Ladder

UseMinimum decision controls
Administrative support with no client informationVerify accuracy, scope, vendor terms, and whether generated material could be mistaken for clinical advice or a professional representation.
Drafting with de-identified or limited informationConfirm that de-identification is defensible, purpose is bounded, access is limited, outputs are reviewed, and no data is reused unexpectedly.
Transcription or ambient documentationTreat as recording or third-party data processing where applicable; verify consent and authorization, data flow, retention, vendor access, accuracy, correction, and client choice.
Clinical summarization or decision supportUse source verification, bias and accuracy testing, uncertainty, competence, independent assessment, human judgment, documentation, and correction before clinical reliance.
Automated client interaction or risk responseAssess whether the use is appropriate at all; establish transparent limits, human availability, emergency routing, monitoring, incident response, and a safe alternative.

Case Lab 7: Cloud Transcription and Vendor Data Flow

A clinic activates an ambient transcription service for all sessions. The consent form says only that technology may be used. Staff cannot explain whether audio is retained, whether data trains the vendor's model, which subcontractors have access, how deletion works, or whether the service is covered by required privacy and security agreements. Clients are not offered a practical alternative.

DECISION POINT
What should the clinic and therapist do before continued use?
MODEL REASONING
Use HUMAN and RECORD. Pause or limit use until the purpose, recording and transcription requirements, client notice and consent, alternative choice, data flow, vendor status, contracts, access, retention, training use, security, deletion, and incident duties are verified. Minimize data, protect records, assess any prior exposure, correct consent and workflow, notify or remediate as current authority requires, and document human accountability. A broad technology clause is not a substitute for informed participation in a materially different data practice.

Case Lab 8: AI-Generated Clinical Error

An AI assistant drafts a progress note stating that a client has firearm access and a psychotic disorder, although neither appears in the source session. The same output labels one partner manipulative based on culturally unfamiliar communication and recommends excluding that partner from treatment. The therapist notices the error after the draft has been copied into a team handoff but before signing the chart.

DECISION POINT
What is the best immediate and follow-through response?
MODEL REASONING
Use HUMAN. Stop reliance on the output, verify source data directly, reassess any actual safety question, correct every affected draft and handoff, notify relevant recipients through the appropriate process, protect the client from downstream decisions, examine bias and workflow failure, document the error and human review, and determine whether the tool should be restricted or discontinued. Do not preserve a false risk statement merely because it was generated automatically, and do not let correction of the record replace direct assessment when a genuine safety concern remains possible.

Formative Answer Key and Learning Rationales

1. Correct Answer: A

Why A: Correct. COMPASS begins by defining the decision and client-protection context, then separates the current authorities before action.

Why B: Policy-collapse error. Employment rules matter, but they do not automatically displace law, regulation, enforceable ethics, client welfare, or professional responsibility.

Why C: Undocumented-avoidance error. Secret noncompliance leaves the conflict, continuity, organizational risk, and accountability unresolved.

Why D: Premature termination. Ending every service before analysis can abandon clients and bypass safer corrective options.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When authorities appear to conflict, separate them before choosing the action; do not let the loudest stakeholder become the law.
TRANSFER RULE
Clarify the client, safety, role, jurisdiction, governing sources, options, consultation, consequences, and documentation. Remediation: R1

2. Correct Answer: B

Why A: Automatic-disclosure error. Identifying a conjoint treatment unit does not by itself resolve each person's confidentiality rights or the existing policy.

Why B: Correct. The response returns to the informed agreement, safety, governing duties, options, consultation, and continuing-treatment decision.

Why C: Automatic-secrecy error. A therapist should not promise a rule that conflicts with the agreed treatment structure or current authority.

Why D: Record-integrity error. Deleting a clinically relevant communication to avoid a decision can corrupt the record and does not resolve the ethical problem.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
In multi-person treatment, identify the client and the information policy before deciding what can be shared or withheld.
TRANSFER RULE
Do not invent confidentiality rules after a conflict emerges; use ongoing consent, current authority, safety, and continuity. Remediation: R2/R3

3. Correct Answer: C

Why A: Compulsion shortcut. A subpoena is not automatically the same as a court order or a complete resolution of privilege and disclosure procedure.

Why B: Requester-control error. The requesting attorney does not determine the therapist's confidentiality, privilege, authorization, or legal duties.

Why C: Correct. The response preserves confidentiality while checking privilege, authority, procedure, protective options, and the lawfully required scope.

Why D: Destruction error. Altering or destroying records in response to legal process can violate law, ethics, preservation duties, and record integrity.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When legal process appears, verify what it is, who holds privilege, and what procedure applies before releasing information.
TRANSFER RULE
Confidentiality remains the starting duty; privilege and compelled disclosure require current jurisdictional analysis. Remediation: R3

4. Correct Answer: D

Why A: Attendance-is-ownership error. Participation alone does not answer record type, client identity, access authority, privilege, or third-party protection.

Why B: Blanket-denial error. Systemic complexity requires analysis, not a universal refusal.

Why C: Majority-rule error. Legal and information rights are not determined by an informal vote.

Why D: Correct. RECORD identifies the requester, treatment structure, record components, current authority, permissions, third-party information, and response process.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY A record request is a classification problem before it is a release problem.
TRANSFER RULE
Verify requester, client, record type, authority, privilege, permissions, third-party information, process, and documentation. Remediation: R4

5. Correct Answer: A

Why A: Correct. Truthful service representation, preserved records, qualified consultation, correction, continuity, and documentation protect both client and payer integrity.

Why B: Delegated-liability error. A supervisor's instruction does not make a knowingly inaccurate claim truthful or erase the therapist's responsibility.

Why C: Record-falsification error. Documentation must reflect the service; it cannot be rewritten to manufacture billing support.

Why D: Financial-consent error. A new client charge requires current authority and the applicable financial agreement, not an improvised transfer of payer loss.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Match the claim to the service and the record; never repair billing pressure by changing clinical facts.
TRANSFER RULE
Use truthful codes and representations, correct errors, protect continuity, and document the business decision. Remediation: R5

6. Correct Answer: B

Why A: Undue-influence error. A current client's apparent willingness does not eliminate the power, exploitation, privacy, or ethics concerns in solicitation.

Why B: Correct. TRUST stops the risky act, corrects inaccurate representation, protects confidentiality, addresses the vendor, and adds monitoring.

Why C: Disclaimer shortcut. A caveat about outcomes does not make an unsupported specialty claim accurate.

Why D: De-identification failure. A story and image may identify the client, and removing the therapist's name does not resolve solicitation or undue influence.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Advertising questions test truth, competence, confidentiality, power, and correction - not just persuasive wording.
TRANSFER RULE
Represent credentials and services accurately; do not use current-client vulnerability as marketing capital. Remediation: R7

7. Correct Answer: C

Why A: Routine-plan error. New location, privacy limits, and escalating risk change the immediate decision.

Why B: Abrupt-disconnection error. Ending contact without assessing and arranging protection can worsen danger and continuity.

Why C: Correct. TELESAFE sequences location, safe communication, risk, privacy, authority, local resources, protection, and continuity.

Why D: Promise substitute. Future travel plans do not address current location, risk, privacy, or lawful emergency action.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
In teletherapy crises, location is clinical and legal information; obtain it early and pair it with direct risk assessment.
TRANSFER RULE
Protect the client now while verifying authority and arranging a lawful local continuity path. Remediation: R8

8. Correct Answer: D

Why A: Single-safeguard error. Encryption is important but does not resolve vendor status, contracts, access, retention, configuration, risk analysis, or incident duties.

Why B: Age-of-data error. Historical records remain sensitive and governed by current retention, security, access, and disposal requirements.

Why C: Marketing-reliance error. Security claims require due diligence and applicable contractual and organizational controls.

Why D: Correct. The response maps the vendor and data lifecycle before exposing client information.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY A technology product is not a safeguard merely because it is marketed to healthcare.
TRANSFER RULE
Map data, access, contracts, safeguards, retention, deletion, incidents, and organizational responsibility before use. Remediation: R9

9. Correct Answer: A

Why A: Correct. HUMAN requires defined purpose, lawful and ethical consent, vendor and data-flow review, minimization, choice, human review, and correction.

Why B: Blanket-consent error. Materially different recording, transcription, retention, or model-training practices require specific current analysis.

Why C: Risk-amplification error. High complexity increases the need for safeguards; it does not justify unreviewed automation.

Why D: Responsibility-transfer error. A vendor cannot decide the therapist's record, consent, or professional-accountability duties.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When a tool listens, records, transcribes, summarizes, or trains, trace the information before deciding whether to use it.
TRANSFER RULE
Define purpose, consent, data flow, vendor use, minimization, alternatives, human review, correction, and monitoring. Remediation: R10

10. Correct Answer: B

Why A: False-record preservation error. Record integrity requires transparent correction, not retention of known fabrication as clinical fact.

Why B: Correct. The therapist halts reliance, verifies actual risk, repairs every downstream use, assesses bias and harm, documents review, and changes the unsafe workflow.

Why C: False-balance error. Averaging a fabricated output with valid judgment does not create a reliable clinical conclusion.

Why D: Accountability-shift error. The therapist and organization remain responsible for the clinical workflow and record, even when a vendor produced the output.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
If an automated output changes risk, diagnosis, treatment, or the record, verify source data before relying on it.
TRANSFER RULE
Human accountability includes stopping use, direct assessment, complete correction, bias review, documentation, and prevention of recurrence. Remediation: R10
