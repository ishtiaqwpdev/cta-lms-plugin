Workbook Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish symptoms, syndromes, diagnoses, relational/contextual conditions, and systemic hypotheses and explain how each contributes to case formulation.
  • Apply pattern, timeline, threshold, impairment, exclusions, alternatives, and diagnostic-status reasoning before selecting a working diagnosis.
  • Compare closely competing presentations using chronology, mechanism, baseline, context, cross-setting evidence, and co-occurrence rather than one memorable symptom.
  • Select and interpret standardized instruments within purpose, psychometric limits, language/cultural fit, accessibility, professional competence, and scope.
  • Integrate medical, neurological, medication, substance, sleep, nutrition, trauma, sexual-functioning, developmental, and contextual factors into differential diagnosis and referral decisions.
  • Use working, provisional, rule-out, other specified, unspecified, deferred, and contextual language only at the level of certainty supported by the evidence.
  • Write a concise systemic case formulation that integrates diagnostic impression, reciprocal patterns, development, culture/context, strengths, risk, treatment unit, missing data, and next step.
  • Select consultation, specialty evaluation, referral, medical/prescriber coordination, or higher-level assessment when diagnostic uncertainty, urgency, scope, or treatment fit requires it, and document the rationale.

High-Yield Distinctions

Do Not CollapseDistinction
Symptom and syndromeA symptom is one reported or observed feature. A syndrome is a recognizable cluster; neither automatically establishes a specific disorder.
Diagnosis and systemic formulationA diagnosis names a clinical pattern. A formulation explains how that pattern interacts with relationships, development, context, strengths, risk, and treatment needs.
Screening and diagnosisA screening result estimates concern or need for further evaluation. It does not replace interview, observation, history, exclusions, and professional judgment.
Provisional and rule-outProvisional is a supported but not fully confirmed diagnosis. Rule-out is a clinical alternative under consideration, not a final diagnosis.
Other specified and unspecifiedOther specified states why full criteria are not met; unspecified does not state a reason. Use only when the diagnostic system and evidence support the choice.
Trait and stateTraits are enduring patterns; states are time-limited or episode-related. One intense session does not establish a pervasive personality pattern.
Culture/context and pathologyCulture, spirituality, discrimination, development, and relational conditions change meaning and access; they are not diagnostic proof by themselves.
CORE DECISION SEQUENCE
What pattern is supported? What is the timeline and baseline? Is threshold and impairment established? What exclusions or alternatives remain? What does the current evidence allow me to call this? How does the pattern operate in the client system? What consultation, referral, or next step follows?
CHAPTER 1 Diagnosis as a Working Systemic Formulation
BIG IDEA
Diagnosis is useful when it organizes evidence and guides care, but it becomes unsafe when it replaces systemic thinking, exceeds the evidence, or closes inquiry too early.

Diagnosis is one part of assessment. It identifies a clinically meaningful pattern using a recognized diagnostic system, but it does not explain the entire person or relationship. A systemic formulation must also address reciprocal interaction, development, context, culture, power, strengths, risk, treatment unit, and the evidence still missing.

Diagnostic Language: What Each Term Does

TermFunctionControl
Sign or symptomA reported experience or observed feature.Record source and context; do not infer a disorder from one feature.
SyndromeA cluster that commonly appears together.Compare duration, impairment, exclusions, and alternative explanations.
DiagnosisA classification supported by current criteria and evidence.Use the least overstated status and revise when evidence changes.
Relational/contextual conditionA relationship, role, environmental, or psychosocial focus relevant to care.Do not confuse a treatment focus with a mental disorder.
Systemic hypothesisA tentative explanation of reciprocal patterns and contextual influences.Keep it evidence-linked, nonblaming, and revisable.
Treatment hypothesisA prediction about what intervention focus or treatment unit may help.Do not treat it as proof before outcomes are observed.

The DIAGNOSE Diagnostic Reasoning Sequence

StepQuestion the Learner Asks
D - Define the patternWhat symptom cluster, relational pattern, or functional change is actually supported?
I - Identify timeline and impactWhen did it begin, what is baseline, and what impairment or risk is present?
A - Assess alternatives and exclusionsWhat medical, medication, substance, sleep, trauma, developmental, cultural, or situational explanation remains?
G - Gather decision-changing dataWhich question, collateral source, record, measure, consultation, or evaluation would change the conclusion?
N - Name the current statusIs the impression working, provisional, deferred, unspecified, other specified, historical, or sufficiently established?
O - Organize systemic meaningHow do diagnosis, reciprocal patterns, development, culture, power, access, strengths, and treatment unit influence one another?
S - Select the next actionIs the next step further assessment, feedback, treatment design, consultation, referral, coordination, or escalation?
E - Evaluate and reviseWhat new evidence would strengthen, weaken, replace, or expand the formulation?
COMMON TRAP A diagnosis that fits some symptoms can still be the weaker answer when it ignores chronology, exclusions, co-occurring conditions, relational effects, or a medical and safety issue that must be addressed first.

Case Lab 1: Label-Only Formulation

CASE LAB
Jordan reports low mood, irritability, poor concentration, and withdrawal after losing a job. A partner increases checking and advice; Jordan then withdraws further. Sleep has shifted because of late-night gig work, alcohol use has increased, and the couple recently moved away from family support. An intake screener is elevated for depression.
  • Which facts support a depressive presentation, and which facts remain nonspecific?
  • What alternative or contributing explanations must remain open?
  • How does the checking-withdrawal cycle affect functioning without proving diagnosis?
  • What would a label-only formulation omit?
MODEL REASONING
Use DIAGNOSE. The elevated screener and symptoms support further depressive assessment but do not establish the entire case. Clarify onset, duration, baseline, impairment, alcohol pattern, sleep disruption, medical factors, and any history of activation. Formulate the reciprocal cycle and loss of support as maintaining conditions, not diagnostic proof. State the current impression at the level supported and identify what information would change it.

CHAPTER 2 Pattern, Timeline, Threshold, and Functional Impact
BIG IDEA
Before choosing a diagnosis, organize what clusters together, when it occurs, whether threshold is supported, and how functioning has changed.

Many distractors are plausible labels built from one memorable symptom. The stronger response asks whether the full pattern is present, whether the timeline fits, whether the presentation is episodic or persistent, whether functioning is impaired, and whether substances, medication, sleep, health, development, or context better explain the change.

The PTI Grid: Pattern, Timeline, and Impact

ElementDiagnostic Question
PatternWhich features cluster together, and which are observed, reported, measured, or inferred?
TimelineWhat are onset, duration, frequency, sequence, baseline, episodicity, persistence, remission, and temporal links?
ThresholdAre required features, distress, severity, duration, impairment, and exclusions sufficiently supported?
ImpactHow are individual, relational, occupational, educational, caregiving, health, and daily functioning affected?
StatusWhat level of certainty and diagnostic language does the evidence support now?

Diagnostic Status Language

LanguageUseDo Not Use It To
Working impressionCommunicate the leading clinical understanding while assessment continues.Pretend the conclusion is final or fully coded.
ProvisionalIndicate substantial support while required information or expected course is still incomplete.Label a vague possibility with little evidence.
Rule-out / considerTrack an alternative that remains clinically plausible and needs a discriminator.List every imaginable disorder or code it as confirmed.
Other specifiedName the reason a presentation does not meet full criteria when the diagnostic system permits.Avoid gathering missing information.
UnspecifiedUse when the category is relevant but the reason is not stated or information is insufficient.Create false precision or bypass differential reasoning.
DeferredPause diagnostic assignment when evidence, safety, medical status, or scope makes a conclusion premature.Ignore urgent evaluation or fail to document the plan.
STATUS CONTROL “Rule out” and “deferred” are communication choices, not substitutes for a documented assessment plan. State what remains uncertain, what information is needed, how urgency is managed, and when the formulation will be reviewed.

Case Lab 2: Distress, Threshold, and Certainty

CASE LAB
Maya reports crying, poor sleep, guilt, and reduced concentration for ten days after a relationship ended. She continues work, maintains parenting routines, denies self-harm thoughts, and reports brief periods of relief with friends. A parent had bipolar disorder, but Maya denies past periods of decreased need for sleep, elevated mood, increased goal-directed activity, or risky behavior.
  • Which features are present, and which threshold elements remain uncertain?
  • How do timeline, functioning, context, and family history affect the differential?
  • What diagnostic language is appropriately cautious at this stage?
  • What follow-up evidence would strengthen or weaken the leading impression?
MODEL REASONING
Use PTI. The presentation is clinically important but brief and closely linked to a major stressor. Family history increases the need to ask about past activation, but it does not establish bipolar disorder. Assess severity, impairment, prior episodes, substances, medication, sleep, and course over time. Use a working or deferred impression rather than a definitive label when threshold is not yet established.

Chronology converts a symptom list into diagnostic reasoning. Threshold and impairment determine how much the current evidence can support.

  • Separate episode from trait and change from baseline.
  • Do not equate distress with a full disorder.
  • Match status language to the current evidence.
  • Document what will be reassessed and when.
CHAPTER 3 Differential Diagnosis and Exclusion Logic
BIG IDEA
The best differential is not the longest list. It compares plausible explanations, identifies the missing discriminator, and routes urgent medical, substance-related, or safety conditions before routine diagnosis.

Differential diagnosis asks which explanation best accounts for the pattern, course, mechanism, context, and impairment while remaining consistent with exclusions. The therapist does not need to diagnose medical conditions, prescribe medication, or conduct specialty testing to recognize when another evaluation controls the next step.

The DIFF Competing-Explanation Grid

Grid RowLearner Action
Candidate explanationName a plausible diagnosis, medical/substance factor, developmental/contextual explanation, or relational condition.
Evidence forList facts that directly support the explanation.
Evidence againstList facts that conflict with or weaken the explanation.
Missing discriminatorIdentify the next fact, timeline detail, observation, instrument, consultation, or referral result that would distinguish it.
Urgency / care levelState whether safety, medical instability, intoxication, withdrawal, psychosis, delirium, or inability to function changes the sequence.
Systemic effectsDescribe how the presentation and client-system responses influence one another without treating context as proof.

High-Yield Exclusion Questions

CategoryQuestions Before Routine Diagnostic Closure
Medical / neurologicalIs there sudden change, confusion, head injury, infection, endocrine or neurological concern, pain, pregnancy/postpartum complication, or nutritional compromise?
Medication / substanceDid symptoms begin or change after starting, stopping, changing, misusing, combining, or withdrawing from a substance or medication?
Sleep / physiologyCould severe sleep loss, circadian disruption, dehydration, restriction, overexercise, or other physiological instability explain or intensify the presentation?
Trauma / dissociationAre the experiences linked to reminders, flashbacks, depersonalization, derealization, or altered awareness rather than a primary psychotic process?
Development / learningIs the pattern longstanding, cross-setting, developmentally anchored, or better explained by learning, language, sensory, or environmental factors?
Culture / spiritualityWhat meaning does the client give the experience, and is it shared or understandable within the person’s cultural or spiritual context without impairment or loss of reality testing?
URGENCY RULE
When acute confusion, medical instability, severe intoxication or withdrawal, inability to care for self or dependents, imminent danger, or rapidly worsening psychosis is present, routing and safety assessment come before ordinary outpatient diagnostic refinement.

Case Lab 3A: Postpartum Intrusive Thoughts

CASE LAB
Four weeks after childbirth, Elena reports depressed mood, severe sleep loss, repeated unwanted images of dropping the baby, and fear that having the images means she is dangerous. She finds the thoughts horrifying, avoids being alone with the baby, seeks reassurance, and retains insight. A relative had postpartum psychosis.
  • Which features suggest ego-dystonic intrusive thoughts rather than intent or fixed psychotic belief?
  • What safety, mood, activation, psychosis, sleep, medication, and medical questions are still required?
  • How should family history affect assessment without determining the diagnosis?
  • What level of coordination or urgent evaluation may be needed if insight, organization, or safety changes?
MODEL REASONING
Assess the content, meaning, distress, insight, intent, planning, reality testing, organization, sleep, functioning, support, and ability to care for self and infant. Intrusive thoughts that are unwanted and resisted differ from intent or delusional conviction, but they still require direct safety assessment. Preserve diagnostic uncertainty, coordinate medical or psychiatric evaluation when indicated, and monitor for rapid change.

Case Lab 3B: Activation After Medication and Stimulants

CASE LAB
After an antidepressant dose increase and several weeks of heavy energy-drink use, Devon sleeps two to three hours without feeling tired, speaks rapidly, starts multiple projects, spends excessively, becomes irritable, and insists performance is better than ever. A partner reports no similar history before the medication change.
  • Which facts suggest activation, and which facts are needed to determine baseline and prior episodes?
  • How should medication, stimulants, sleep loss, substance use, bipolar-spectrum illness, psychosis, and medical causes be compared?
  • What is the first appropriate coordination step?
  • Why is routine couple communication work insufficient as the initial response?
MODEL REASONING
Treat the marked change from baseline and reduced need for sleep as decision-critical. Clarify chronology, prior episodes, substances, medication changes, psychotic features, safety, and functioning. Coordinate prompt psychiatric or medical evaluation rather than advising a medication change. A substance- or medication-related explanation and a bipolar-spectrum episode may both remain open until the timeline and clinical evaluation clarify the presentation.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY
Differential diagnosis is an active comparison. The strongest answer identifies the discriminator and does not delay evaluation when medical, substance-related, psychotic, nutritional, or safety conditions may control the sequence.
  • Compare mechanism and course, not only symptom overlap.
  • Assess medical, medication, substance, sleep, and nutritional factors.
  • Separate intrusive thoughts from intent and fixed belief.
  • Coordinate rather than prescribe or exceed scope.
  • Keep continuity while specialty evaluation occurs.
CHAPTER 4 Standardized Instruments, Measurement, and Diagnostic Confidence
BIG IDEA
An instrument answers a defined question under specific conditions. It improves reasoning when used within purpose and limits; it becomes unsafe when a score is treated as a diagnosis.

Assessment instruments can structure symptom inquiry, estimate severity, monitor change, or support a specialty evaluation. The therapist must still consider fit, administration conditions, norms, reliability, validity, measurement error, language, culture, accessibility, competence, and the consequences of false positives and false negatives.

The MEASURE Instrument-Use Check

StepInstrument Control
M - MatchMatch the tool to the unresolved clinical question; do not administer it merely because it is available.
E - Evaluate evidenceReview norms, reliability, validity, measurement error, and limits for the population and purpose.
A - Assess accessConsider language, culture, literacy, disability, technology access, and accommodation needs.
S - Stay within competenceUse only within training, scope, scoring authority, licensing rules, and permitted use.
U - Use multiple dataCombine results with interview, observation, collateral, records, context, and judgment.
R - Review collaborativelyExplain what the result supports, what it does not establish, and how the client system understands it.
E - Explain and documentRecord purpose, consent, conditions, limitations, interpretation, consultation, and next step.

Psychometric Concepts in Plain Language

ConceptPractical MeaningExam Control
ReliabilityHow consistently a measure performs under similar conditions.A consistent tool can still measure the wrong construct.
ValidityHow well evidence supports the interpretation and use of scores for the intended purpose.Validity belongs to the interpretation and use, not the test name alone.
NormsReference data used to compare a score with a defined group.Ask whether the comparison group fits the client and context.
SensitivityHow often the tool identifies people who have the condition or concern.Higher sensitivity may increase false positives.
SpecificityHow often the tool correctly excludes people who do not have the condition or concern.Higher specificity may still miss cases if sensitivity is limited.
Cut scoreA decision threshold chosen for a specific purpose.A cut score is not a biological boundary or stand-alone diagnosis.
Measurement errorThe expected uncertainty around a score.Small score changes may not reflect meaningful clinical change.
Repeated measurementUsing the same or comparable measure over time.Interpret trends with treatment, context, administration conditions, and client report.
SCREENING CONTROL A positive screen means “assess further,” not “diagnosis confirmed.” A negative screen does not override direct clinical evidence, acute change, or a tool-population mismatch.
AI AND TECHNOLOGY CONTROL
An AI-generated intake summary, score interpretation, or diagnostic suggestion must be verified by a qualified human. Review source data, omissions, bias, privacy, authorization, and clinical fit. Never delegate diagnosis or professional accountability to a tool.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY
Use instruments to sharpen a defined question. The instrument does not replace the therapist’s responsibility to integrate the full evidence and explain uncertainty.
  • Match the measure to the unresolved decision.
  • Evaluate fit, evidence, access, and competence.
  • Interpret scores with other data and context.
  • Explain false-positive, false-negative, and measurement limits.
  • Preserve human review and accountability in technology-assisted assessment.
CHAPTER 5 High-Yield Diagnostic Families and Complex Presentations
BIG IDEA
High-yield diagnosis depends on discriminators: timeline, mechanism, insight, reality testing, baseline, cross-setting pattern, functional impact, and relation to substances, medication, sleep, health, development, and context.

The AMFTRB examination does not require memorizing every criterion in isolation. It requires selecting the next supported assessment or professional action when several explanations fit part of the presentation. Use the following comparisons as reasoning prompts, not as one-to-one diagnostic rules.

COMPARE Matrix

Presentation AreaDecision-Critical Comparisons
Mood and bipolarEpisode versus trait; depressed mood versus grief or adjustment; decreased need for sleep versus insomnia; activation versus anxiety, ADHD baseline, substances, medication, or sleep loss.
Anxiety and OCDGeneralized worry, panic, obsessions, compulsions, intrusive thoughts, trauma cues, health concerns, and avoidance; assess insight, function, intent, and reinforcement.
Trauma, dissociation, and psychosisIntrusion, flashback, hypervigilance, depersonalization, derealization, fixed belief, hallucination, disorganization, delirium, substance effects, and cultural or spiritual meaning.
Substance, medication, and sleepIntoxication, withdrawal, side effects, misuse, interactions, adherence, periods of abstinence, circadian disruption, and change from baseline.
Neurodevelopmental and learningDevelopmental onset, cross-setting pattern, educational history, language, sensory factors, sleep, anxiety, trauma, mood, medical issues, and collateral consistency.
Personality and enduring patternsLongitudinal, pervasive, inflexible, cross-context pattern versus trauma adaptation, attachment response, mood episode, neurodevelopment, substance effect, or situational rupture.
Eating, somatic, sleep, and sexualMedical stability, restriction, exercise, body image, pain, health anxiety, sleep disorders, medication effects, trauma, consent, culture, identity, diversity, and specialty referral.

Case Lab 5A: School Refusal and Cross-Setting Inattention

CASE LAB
Ten-year-old Sam has morning stomachaches, school refusal, distractibility, and declining grades. A teacher reports inattention mainly during reading. A caregiver reports no problems during preferred activities. Sam describes bullying and difficulty understanding written assignments. Sleep is irregular because of late-night gaming.
  • Which data support anxiety, ADHD, learning, sleep, trauma, medical, or contextual explanations?
  • What developmental, school, medical, and cross-setting information would discriminate among them?
  • Why would one informant or one behavior be insufficient?
  • What referral questions may be appropriate?
MODEL REASONING
Require developmental history, cross-setting evidence, functional impact, academic skill data, sleep assessment, bullying and safety inquiry, medical review, and collaboration with school and caregivers. Inattention limited to specific tasks may reflect learning difficulty, anxiety, or context rather than a pervasive attention disorder. Keep multiple explanations open and write precise referral questions.

Case Lab 5B: Trauma, Possible TBI, and Cannabis

CASE LAB A veteran reports nightmares, irritability, headaches, memory lapses, “zoning out” while driving, heavy cannabis use, and access to firearms. A partner describes a recent increase in confusion after a fall. The client attributes all symptoms to PTSD and wants trauma processing immediately.
  • Which conditions require urgent routing before routine trauma treatment?
  • How should PTSD, TBI, substance effects, depression, sleep disorder, dissociation, and bipolar-spectrum explanations be compared?
  • What safety and medical questions are decision-critical?
  • How can the therapist maintain continuity while coordinating evaluation?
MODEL REASONING
New confusion after a fall, driving episodes, heavy substance use, sleep disruption, and firearm access require direct safety assessment and prompt medical or neurological evaluation. PTSD may remain relevant, but it cannot explain every feature without comparison. Do not begin intensive trauma processing before clarifying physiological stability, cognitive change, substance effects, and current safety.

Case Lab 5C: Restrictive Eating and Athlete Identity

CASE LAB A collegiate athlete reports severe restriction, dizziness, missed menstrual periods, compulsive exercise, a stress fracture, and fear that reduced training will cost a scholarship. The client’s weight is not visibly low and a coach praises discipline.
  • Which red flags indicate that medical evaluation controls the sequence?
  • What eating, anxiety/OCD, mood, medical, sport-energy, identity, and cultural factors require assessment?
  • Why are appearance and weight alone inadequate indicators?
  • What multidisciplinary coordination and continuity are needed?
MODEL REASONING
Dizziness, menstrual disruption, restriction, compulsive exercise, and stress fracture indicate possible physiological compromise regardless of visible weight. Arrange prompt medical and specialty evaluation while assessing safety, eating-disorder features, mood, anxiety, exercise function, scholarship pressure, identity, and support. Maintain therapeutic continuity and avoid reinforcing performance-based minimization.

Case Lab 5D: NSSI, Relational Instability, and Diagnostic Closure

CASE LAB
Taylor has a history of nonsuicidal self-injury, intense fear of abandonment, episodic crisis messages, alcohol misuse, rapid shifts in closeness and anger, and strong provider reactions. A recent breakup triggered renewed cutting, but Taylor denies current suicidal intent and has never had a sustained period of elevated mood.
  • How should NSSI and current suicide risk be assessed separately?
  • What longitudinal evidence is needed before a personality diagnosis?
  • How should trauma, bipolar disorder, substance use, ADHD, attachment responses, and situational rupture remain in the differential?
  • How do provider reactions and treatment-frame problems affect formulation without becoming diagnostic proof?
MODEL REASONING
Assess current suicide risk directly while recognizing that NSSI and suicidal intent are not identical. Use longitudinal, pervasive, cross-context evidence before assigning an enduring personality diagnosis. Compare trauma adaptations, attachment responses, substance effects, neurodevelopment, mood episodes, and situational rupture. Include the treatment relationship and provider response in systemic formulation while maintaining boundaries, consultation, and a clear treatment frame.

CHAPTER 6 Integrated Systemic Case Formulation, Collaboration, and Referral
BIG IDEA A complete formulation states the current diagnostic impression, competing explanations, reciprocal pattern, context, strengths, treatment unit, missing data, referral needs, and conditions for revision.

The formulation is the bridge from assessment to treatment design. It should be brief enough to guide action and specific enough to show how the therapist reached the conclusion. It must also be collaborative: the client system should be able to understand, question, and refine the therapist’s explanation.

The FORMULATE Integrated Case Statement

StepRequired Content
F - Facts and impressionState the supported pattern, timeline, functional impact, and current diagnostic certainty.
O - Other explanationsName the most important competing explanations and missing discriminators.
R - Relational cycle and treatment unitDescribe reciprocal responses, roles, boundaries, meanings, and who should participate now.
M - Medical, medication, substance, sleep, and safetyState what must be coordinated, referred, monitored, or escalated.
U - Unique context and strengthsIntegrate culture, identity, development, spirituality, power, access, resources, and protective factors.
L - Likely prioritiesIdentify immediate treatment priorities without prematurely choosing a detailed treatment model.
A - Actions, collaboration, and referralName the next action, referral question, continuity plan, and documentation need.
T - Track and reviseIdentify outcomes or evidence that would change diagnosis, treatment unit, care level, or plan.
E - Explain collaborativelyUse clear, nonstigmatizing language and invite correction or refinement from the client system.

Referral Questions That Improve Continuity

Weak ReferralStronger Referral Question
“Rule out medical causes.”Evaluate whether recent cognitive change, headaches, and sleep disruption may reflect neurological or other medical conditions that alter psychiatric formulation and treatment safety.
“Assess for ADHD.”Clarify developmental onset, cross-setting attention and executive-function pattern, learning factors, sleep, anxiety, and functional impairment.
“Medication evaluation.”Review whether medication changes, side effects, adherence, interactions, or activation could account for the recent change from baseline.
“Eating disorder referral.”Assess medical stability, restrictive intake, exercise pattern, bone and reproductive health, and appropriate level of specialty care while coordinating ongoing psychotherapy.

Integrated Formulation Lab

CASE LAB
Alex, age 36, seeks help for panic, irritability, poor concentration, and escalating conflict with a spouse. Symptoms intensified after rotating-shift work began. Alex sleeps four hours, uses increasing alcohol to fall asleep, had one prior episode of increased energy after a medication change, and recently stopped attending a faith community. The spouse responds to panic by repeatedly checking, arranging tasks, and discouraging independent activity; Alex then becomes more avoidant and ashamed. A brief anxiety screener is elevated.
  • Use DIAGNOSE and PTI to state the current pattern, timeline, impact, and status.
  • Use DIFF to compare panic, sleep/circadian disruption, alcohol effects, medication-related activation, mood disorder, and medical factors.
  • Use MEASURE to state what the screening result does and does not establish.
  • Use FORMULATE to describe the reciprocal cycle, context, strengths, treatment unit, referral questions, and revision plan.
MODEL REASONING A working formulation may include panic symptoms, severe sleep disruption, alcohol-related effects, occupational strain, loss of support, and a checking-avoidance cycle. The elevated screener supports further assessment but not a final diagnosis. Clarify prior activation, medication history, substance pattern, medical factors, safety, and functional impairment. Coordinate medical or prescriber evaluation as needed, preserve couple or individual treatment only if safe and appropriate, and review the formulation after sleep, substance, and medical information become clearer.

Answer Key and Detailed Rationales

Review the reasoning, not only the correct letter. For each miss, identify the diagnostic task, the missing evidence or discriminator, the level of certainty supported, and the earliest professional correction.

1. Correct Answer: B

Why B is correct: Diagnosis classifies a supported clinical pattern; a systemic hypothesis tentatively explains reciprocal, developmental, cultural, and contextual influences and remains open to revision.

Why A is not best: A diagnosis does not explain every contextual or relational factor, and a systemic hypothesis is more than a symptom list.

Why C is not best: Including family information does not automatically make a hypothesis more certain.

Why D is not best: The terms serve different functions and should not be used interchangeably.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Keep classification and formulation separate. The strongest answer can use both without allowing either to replace the other.

2. Correct Answer: C

Why C is correct: A screening result estimates concern or severity and guides further evaluation. It must be integrated with interview, history, impairment, exclusions, context, and professional judgment.

Why A is not best: A cut score does not establish full diagnostic criteria.

Why B is not best: Treatment selection requires broader formulation and client-system information.

Why D is not best: Screeners can contribute useful information when used within purpose and limits.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Screen to assess further. Do not convert a score into a diagnosis or dismiss a useful measure because it is not diagnostic by itself.

3. Correct Answer: A

Why A is correct: A disorder-level conclusion requires a supported pattern, appropriate duration or course, clinically meaningful distress or impairment, and adequate attention to exclusions.

Why B is not best: One symptom may be important but does not establish the full pattern or threshold.

Why C is not best: Family certainty is collateral information, not diagnostic proof.

Why D is not best: One score without administration and context review is incomplete evidence.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Use PTI: pattern, timeline, threshold, impact, and status before diagnostic closure.

4. Correct Answer: D

Why D is correct: Cautious working or provisional language communicates the leading impression while making missing longitudinal evidence and the review plan explicit.

Why A is not best: Definitive language overstates the evidence.

Why B is not best: Listing multiple diagnoses as confirmed creates inaccurate certainty rather than preserving a differential.

Why C is not best: A clinician can document a responsible current impression without pretending all uncertainty is resolved.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Match status language to evidence. State what is known, what is missing, and when the formulation will be reviewed.

5. Correct Answer: B

Why B is correct: The marked change after medication and stimulant exposure requires chronology, prior-episode assessment, substance and medical review, safety evaluation, and prompt coordination with a prescriber or psychiatric provider.

Why A is not best: Grandiosity is important but does not by itself distinguish primary bipolar illness from medication- or substance-related activation.

Why C is not best: Behavioral activation may worsen risk and does not address the diagnostic urgency.

Why D is not best: Partner supervision is not a substitute for professional assessment and prescriber coordination.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When activation follows medication, substances, or severe sleep loss, assess timeline and route before choosing a definitive diagnosis or routine intervention.

6. Correct Answer: C

Why C is correct: The therapist must compare trauma-linked intrusion and dissociation with psychosis while assessing reality testing, organization, substances, medical factors, cultural meaning, and impairment.

Why A is not best: Hearing a voice-like experience does not automatically establish a primary psychotic disorder.

Why B is not best: Cultural meaning must be explored, not assumed.

Why D is not best: Avoidance prevents the assessment needed for safety and differential diagnosis.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Clarify form, context, insight, reality testing, and baseline before labeling an unusual experience.

7. Correct Answer: A

Why A is correct: The measure is matched to a defined question and client population, its limits are explained, and results are integrated with other evidence.

Why B is not best: Routine use without fit or purpose may create burden and misleading interpretation.

Why C is not best: Agreement and a cut score do not transform screening into diagnosis.

Why D is not best: An unverified translation may reduce accuracy and cannot be justified merely as better than nothing.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Use MEASURE: match, evaluate, assess access, stay within competence, use multiple data, review collaboratively, and explain.

8. Correct Answer: D

Why D is correct: It integrates a possible diagnostic pattern with sleep, occupation, substance or medical clarification, and a reciprocal relational cycle while preserving uncertainty.

Why A is not best: It uses one-person causation and makes the spouse the cause.

Why B is not best: Systemic practice does not eliminate the legitimate role of diagnosis.

Why C is not best: Relational assessment is part of the formulation rather than a later optional step.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY A systemic formulation includes diagnosis without reducing the client system to the diagnosis.

9. Correct Answer: B

Why B is correct: The physiological red flags require prompt medical and specialty evaluation regardless of visible weight or the exact diagnostic category. Psychotherapy continuity and safety assessment should be maintained.

Why A is not best: Waiting for a perfect category may delay care for medical instability.

Why C is not best: Weight alone does not determine physiological risk.

Why D is not best: Performance anxiety may be relevant but does not control the initial sequence when medical compromise is possible.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Medical stability before diagnostic precision. Route urgent concerns while preserving continuity and systemic assessment.

10. Correct Answer: C

Why C is correct: It preserves uncertainty accurately, uses consultation and specialty evaluation within competence, defines the referral question, coordinates appropriately, and avoids abandonment.

Why A is not best: Choosing one source creates a credibility contest and ignores the value and limits of conflicting data.

Why B is not best: Referral may be needed, but immediate termination can disrupt care and does not define the clinical question.

Why D is not best: Treatment and support do not always need to stop while diagnostic clarification continues.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Consult and refer with a question, authorization, documentation, and continuity. Do not outsource the case or wait for impossible certainty.
