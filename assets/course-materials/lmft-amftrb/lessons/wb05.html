Workbook Learning Objectives

  • Translate assessment findings, diagnostic impressions, systemic hypotheses, strengths, risks, and client priorities into an organized contextual treatment plan.
  • Establish a clear therapeutic contract that identifies the client system, treatment roles, goals, participation expectations, communication structure, and relevant limits.
  • Write short- and long-term goals with observable objectives, meaningful measures, review intervals, and criteria for successful completion.
  • Select the treatment unit, participants, delivery modality, intensity, and level of care based on safety, appropriateness, access, readiness, preference, and formulation.
  • Order treatment priorities and interventions according to urgency, dependencies, prior attempts, readiness, homeostasis, and competing client-system needs.
  • Explain an intervention rationale grounded in systemic theory, current evidence, context, culture, strengths, client autonomy, and therapist competence.
  • Integrate safety planning, referral, medication coordination, collateral involvement, and professional roles without exceeding scope or obscuring responsibility.
  • Specify documentation, progress measures, feedback processes, and plan-revision triggers that support ongoing evaluation, transition, and continuity.

Do Not CollapseDistinction
Treatment plan and technique listA plan defines the clinical logic, roles, goals, structure, measures, and decision points. Techniques are selected only after the plan explains why they fit.
Goal and interventionA goal describes the desired change. An intervention describes what the therapist and client system will do to support that change.

CHAPTER 1From Formulation to Treatment Priorities
BIG IDEA Planning begins with the best-supported systemic formulation and the most important decision—not with the therapist's favorite technique.

Treatment planning converts assessment into action. A useful plan is specific enough to guide care and flexible enough to change when new evidence appears. It identifies the client system, the target pattern, the priority, the strengths and constraints, the service structure, and the decision that comes next. A plan that merely restates symptoms or lists techniques does not show clinical reasoning.

The DESIGN Treatment-Planning Sequence

StepQuestion the Learner Asks
D - Define the client system and planning taskWho is receiving treatment, what stage of care applies, and what decision must the plan make?
E - Establish priorities and constraintsWhat safety, health, ethical, access, role, or level-of-care issue controls the sequence?
S - State the systemic formulationWhat reciprocal pattern, function, diagnosis, context, strength, and missing information explain the target?
I - Identify goals, unit, modality, and participantsWhat change is sought, who must participate, and what service structure best contains the maintaining pattern?
G - Ground interventions in evidence and collaborationWhat theory, research, client preference, culture, readiness, and competence support the approach?
N - Name measures, review points, and next decisionsHow will progress, safety, alliance, implementation, and completion be evaluated and documented?

Prioritizing Competing Concerns

Not every important concern becomes the first treatment target. The therapist distinguishes urgent from important, prerequisite from later-stage work, and client-defined priorities from therapist preferences. A high-quality answer asks which issue controls safety or level of care, which change makes other work possible, and which target the client system is prepared to address.

Priority TypePlanning Question
Safety or stabilityDoes acute danger, medical instability, coercion, intoxication, severe impairment, or environmental threat require immediate action or a different setting?
PrerequisiteWhat must improve before another intervention can be effective or safe?
Maintaining patternWhich reciprocal sequence, avoidance pattern, role, boundary, or contextual barrier keeps the problem active?
Client priorityWhich change is meaningful enough to support engagement and informed participation?
FeasibilityWhat can be implemented with the available time, resources, access, and competence?
MeasurementWhich target can be observed and reviewed without oversimplifying the case?
COMMON TRAPChoosing the most severe-sounding problem or most advanced intervention before identifying the controlling priority. Severity matters, but the first plan decision depends on safety, prerequisites, readiness, treatment fit, and the client system's goals.

Case Lab 1: Couple Planning After Assessment

Maya and Chris report repeated conflict about household responsibilities. The assessment supports a pursue-withdraw cycle: Maya escalates reminders when she feels alone; Chris withdraws when he expects criticism; withdrawal increases Maya's urgency. There is no current violence or coercion. Both want less conflict, but their stated goal is only “communicate better.” Childcare makes weekly evening sessions difficult.

  • What is the planning task and client system?
  • Which reciprocal pattern should organize the plan?
  • What should be prioritized before selecting a technique?
  • How should childcare and scheduling affect the treatment structure?
  • What initial measure or indicator could show movement?
MODEL REASONINGDefine the couple as the treatment unit and use the pursue-withdraw cycle as the working systemic target. Convert “communicate better” into a shared functional goal and observable objectives. Choose a feasible structure before assuming missed sessions mean low motivation. Select interventions only after the goal, sequence, and measure are clear.

CHAPTER 2Therapeutic Contracts, Roles, and the Treatment Frame
BIG IDEA A treatment plan cannot function when the client system, roles, expectations, participation, and communication structure remain ambiguous.

The therapeutic contract is the shared frame for treatment. It clarifies who the client is, why services are being provided, who participates, what each person can expect, how communication and emergencies are handled, and how the agreement will be revisited. In multi-client work, role clarity protects alliance, autonomy, confidentiality structure, and coordination.

The CONTRACT Treatment-Frame Check

ElementRequired Planning Question
C - Client systemWho is the client, who participates, and whose goals and rights must be represented?
O - Objectives and purposeWhy are services being provided, and what do the parties expect treatment to address?
N - Nature and limitsWhat modality, roles, information-sharing structure, collateral contact, recording/technology, or limits affect the plan?
T - Time and structureWhat frequency, session format, communication channels, attendance expectations, emergency procedures, and review intervals apply?
R - ResponsibilitiesWhat is the therapist, client, caregiver, collateral, prescriber, agency, or community system responsible for?
A - Access and accommodationHow will language, disability, finances, transportation, caregiving, technology, scheduling, and cultural fit be addressed?
C - Collaboration and consentHow will questions, preferences, disagreement, and voluntary informed participation be invited and documented?
T - Track and repairWhen roles, risk, goals, participation, or setting change, how will the agreement be reviewed and repaired?

Contract Problems That Change the Plan

ProblemBest Initial Response
Unclear client identityClarify who is receiving treatment, whose goals guide care, and how each participant is involved before proceeding.
Third-party payment or referralClarify the service purpose, roles, reporting expectations, and client rights; payment does not automatically make the payer the client.
Involuntary or pressured participationAssess voluntariness, safety, power, and realistic participation; do not treat attendance as agreement.
Conflicting goalsMake the disagreement explicit, identify shared and incompatible goals, and determine whether the treatment unit or structure remains workable.
Changing treatment unitRevisit roles, expectations, information-sharing structure, goals, and consent when new participants are added or removed.
Alliance rupture around the frameAcknowledge the impact, clarify the misunderstanding, invite the client system's perspective, repair the agreement, and document material changes.
COMMON TRAPAssuming that a signed intake form permanently resolves the treatment contract. Informed participation and role clarity continue throughout care and must be revisited when participants, risks, modalities, goals, or expectations change.

Case Lab 2: Family Contract and Role Confusion

A 15-year-old is referred by a school counselor after falling grades and panic symptoms. The parents expect the therapist to report everything discussed. The adolescent believes sessions are private. The school requests progress updates, and a prescriber asks for symptom information. No one has clarified who the client is, what information may be coordinated, or how family sessions will be used.

  • What must be clarified before treatment proceeds?
  • Whose goals and rights must be represented?
  • Which roles belong to the parents, adolescent, school, prescriber, and therapist?
  • What expectations should be documented?
  • What would be premature?
MODEL REASONINGUse CONTRACT before relying on technique or compliance. Clarify the client system, treatment purpose, roles, participation, communication structure, coordination needs, and applicable limits. Invite questions and disagreement. Do not promise blanket secrecy or blanket disclosure, and do not let the referral source define the clinical relationship without clarification.

CHAPTER 3Goals, Objectives, Measures, and Completion Criteria
BIG IDEA A strong plan converts shared priorities into observable change without reducing treatment to a checklist or promising perfection.

Goals describe the direction of change. Objectives identify observable indicators that show whether the client system is moving in that direction. Measures provide consistent information for review. Completion criteria define what adequate improvement, maintenance, self-management, step-down, transfer, or transition would look like.

The TARGET Goal and Objective Builder

ElementGoal-Control Question
T - Target pattern or functionWhat problem process or functional impairment should change without assigning blame?
A - Agreed directionWhat language does the client system understand and support?
R - Relational and contextual fitHow does the objective connect to relationships, development, culture, setting, resources, and treatment unit?
G - GaugeWhat observable, reportable, or measurable indicator can be reviewed consistently?
E - Expected time and conditionsWhat realistic review period, supports, barriers, and dependencies apply?
T - Transition or completion criterionWhat would successful completion, step-down, transfer, or plan revision look like?

From Vague Goal to Measurable Plan

Vague or WeakStronger Planning Language
“Communicate better.”During the next four weeks, each partner will identify the beginning of the pursue-withdraw sequence and use the agreed pause-and-return process in at least three documented conflicts per week.
“Stop being anxious.”Over six weeks, the client will report and track panic frequency, avoidance, sleep, and work functioning while practicing the agreed regulation and exposure steps.
“Be compliant with treatment.”The client and therapist will identify two barriers to attendance, select feasible accommodations, and review participation and burden at each visit for four weeks.
“Improve the family.”Within eight weeks, the family will complete one weekly planning meeting using defined roles and will reduce unresolved household-task disputes from five to two per week.
“Resolve all trauma.”The client will first establish agreed stabilization indicators and demonstrate sufficient sleep, substance stability, grounding capacity, and crisis support before trauma processing is reconsidered.

Choosing Measures

Measures should match the planning question. They may include client report, relational frequency or intensity, functional indicators, symptom measures, safety status, attendance burden, alliance feedback, skill use, collateral information, or therapist observation. A score is one source of evidence, not the whole case.

Measure TypeExampleControl
RelationalFrequency of escalation, repair attempts, time to return, or ability to name a cycle.Avoid assigning a single person as the sole cause.
FunctionalWork attendance, caregiving tasks, sleep routine, school participation, or daily living.Connect change to client priorities.
SymptomFrequency, intensity, duration, and interference.Do not treat score change as sufficient without context.
SafetyIdeation, intent, access, warning signs, protective supports, or crisis-plan use.Escalate level of care when data require it.
Alliance and fitClient report of collaboration, understanding, burden, and cultural fit.Use feedback to repair, not to pressure agreement.
ImplementationWhether agreed interventions were delivered and feasible.Do not label ineffective implementation as treatment resistance.
COMMON TRAPWriting an objective that merely names a therapist activity, such as “provide psychoeducation,” or that demands an absolute outcome, such as “never argue again.” The objective should describe observable client-system change and a realistic review point.

Case Lab 3: Rewriting the Goal

A couple agrees that jealousy and repeated phone checking are damaging trust. The therapist writes: “Goal: eliminate jealousy. Objective: therapist will teach communication skills.” Both partners say the plan does not reflect what they want.

  • What is wrong with the goal and objective?
  • How can the target be stated without blaming one partner?
  • What observable relational and functional indicators could be used?
  • What review interval and completion criteria would be reasonable?
MODEL REASONINGUse TARGET to describe the reciprocal process and the client system's agreed direction. The therapist activity may be part of the intervention, but the objective should state the observable change: reduced checking, increased direct requests, agreed privacy boundaries, repair after triggers, or improved trust-related functioning. Set a review point and state what evidence would support continuation or revision.

CHAPTER 4Treatment Unit, Modality, Level of Care, and Sequence
BIG IDEA The plan must place the right people in the right structure at the right time; more participants, more intensity, or faster technique is not automatically better.

Treatment structure includes who participates, where and how services occur, how often contact happens, what level of care can contain the risk and impairment, and which steps must occur before later-stage work. The therapist selects the least restrictive supported setting that can safely and feasibly meet the need.

The SEQUENCE Treatment-Order Map

StepPlanning Rule
1. Safety and stabilityAddress acute danger, medical instability, coercion, intoxication, severe impairment, or environmental threat before routine change work.
2. Engagement and contractEnsure participation is voluntary, roles are clear, and the therapeutic frame can support the plan.
3. Assessment and formulation sufficiencyConfirm enough information exists to plan without demanding impossible certainty.
4. Priority and dependencyIdentify what must occur before another goal or intervention can work.
5. Treatment unit and modalitySelect who participates, where, how often, and at what level of care.
6. Intervention and rationaleChoose the least premature evidence- and theory-consistent action that fits readiness and context.
7. Coordination and accessArrange referrals, prescriber or community collaboration, accommodations, and feasible follow-through.
8. Evaluation and next branchDefine the data and time point supporting continuation, revision, step-up, step-down, or transition.

Treatment-Unit Decision Controls

QuestionPlanning Control
Where is the maintaining pattern?Include the person or system needed to observe and change the target, but do not include everyone automatically.
Is participation safe and voluntary?Fear, coercion, retaliation, active violence, or major power imbalance may make conjoint work unsafe or misleading.
Whose rights and goals are involved?Clarify client status, consent, roles, and competing goals before changing participants.
What information is needed?Collateral consultation may support care without making the collateral participant a psychotherapy client.
What burden does participation create?Consider disability, language, culture, caregiving, work, transportation, technology, and finances.
Can the setting contain the need?Match risk, impairment, monitoring capacity, medical needs, and support to level of care.

Case Lab 4: Coercive Control and Conjoint Fit

A couple requests communication therapy. During an individual screening, one partner reports fear of retaliation, recent sexual coercion, monitoring of finances and messages, and threats if the relationship ends. The other partner describes the problem as “equal conflict” and insists both attend conjoint sessions immediately.

  • What controls the sequence?
  • Is symmetrical communication framing appropriate?
  • What treatment unit and modality concerns must be addressed?
  • What specialty, safety, or higher-level resources may be needed?
  • What would be premature?
MODEL REASONINGSafety and coercion control the plan. Do not frame the pattern as symmetrical or begin routine conjoint communication work simply because both requested it. Reassess treatment fit, create or coordinate appropriate safety and specialty supports, clarify roles and confidentiality structure, and determine whether separate or alternative services are indicated.

Case Lab 5: Premature Trauma Processing

A client requests intensive trauma processing. Current information shows severe sleep disruption, frequent intoxication, episodes of dissociation, recent medical symptoms, and no reliable crisis supports. The therapist is trained in a trauma model and wants to begin immediately to maintain engagement.

  • Which prerequisites are missing?
  • What level-of-care and coordination questions come first?
  • How should the goal be staged?
  • Why is therapist competence in a model not enough to justify immediate use?
MODEL REASONINGUse SEQUENCE. Stabilization, medical and substance-related assessment, risk review, engagement, support, and feasibility precede intensive processing. A model may be evidence-based and within general competence yet still be premature for the current stage, setting, or monitoring capacity.

CHAPTER 5Intervention Rationale, Evidence, Context, and Autonomy
BIG IDEA The strongest intervention choice explains why the mechanism, theory, evidence, context, preference, competence, and feasibility fit this client system now.

A treatment plan should explain why an intervention is expected to influence the target pattern. “Evidence-based,” “systemic,” or “client-centered” is not enough. The rationale links the mechanism of change to the formulation, goal, treatment stage, client preferences, culture and context, therapist competence, and practical feasibility.

The MATCH Intervention-Rationale Grid

ComponentRequired Content
M - MechanismWhat maintaining pattern, skill deficit, avoidance, attachment injury, boundary problem, belief, trauma response, or contextual barrier is the intervention intended to change?
A - AlignmentDoes it align with the systemic hypothesis, goal, stage, treatment unit, and client-system priorities?
T - Theory and current evidenceWhat model, research, clinical guidance, or practice-based evidence supports the choice?
C - Context, culture, and client preferenceHow do identity, values, spirituality, language, disability, power, access, autonomy, and prior experience affect fit?
H - Human feasibility and harm controlsIs the plan within competence, acceptable, practical, monitorable, and unlikely to create avoidable harm or burden?

Evidence-Informed Does Not Mean Evidence-Only

Evidence-informed planning integrates the best available research with clinical expertise, case formulation, client characteristics, culture, preferences, values, resources, and ongoing outcome information. Evidence quality and relevance matter. A study from a different population or setting may still inform care, but the therapist should state the limits and monitor fit rather than present certainty.

QuestionWhat the Stronger Answer Does
Does the intervention address the target mechanism?Links the method to the actual maintaining process rather than the diagnosis alone.
Is the evidence relevant?Considers population, setting, treatment unit, stage, outcome, and study limitations.
Is the client system willing and able to participate?Uses shared decision-making and assesses burden, access, language, disability, culture, and prior experience.
Is the therapist competent?Uses training, supervision, consultation, referral, or transition rather than practicing beyond competence.
How will fit be monitored?Names outcome, alliance, safety, implementation, and access indicators and a review point.

Case Lab 6: Access and Disability Adaptation

A Deaf client and hearing partner request couple therapy. The therapist proposes an uncaptioned teletherapy platform and a fast-paced verbal exercise. The interpreter available to the couple can attend only every other week. The couple misses two sessions, and the therapist documents “low motivation.”

  • What planning error occurred before the missed sessions?
  • How should modality, communication supports, schedule, and measures be adapted?
  • How can the therapist preserve the couple's autonomy and treatment goals?
  • What evidence would distinguish access burden from low engagement?
MODEL REASONINGUse MATCH and CONTRACT. The proposed structure was not accessible, so the therapist should revise the modality, communication supports, pacing, schedule, and outcome indicators before attributing missed sessions to motivation. Collaboration should preserve the clients' goals and language access while remaining clinically feasible and within competence.

Case Lab 7: Recovery-Oriented Planning

A client has escalating alcohol use, a partner who no longer trusts promises, medication treatment from a prescriber, childcare limits, and recent relapse risk. The therapist proposes weekly insight-oriented couple sessions as the only service and asks the partner to monitor all medication and alcohol use.

  • What problems should be prioritized and sequenced?
  • What role should the partner have—and not have?
  • What recovery, safety, specialty, and prescriber coordination may be needed?
  • What measurable goals could avoid moralizing or surveillance?
MODEL REASONINGBuild a staged, recovery-oriented plan that addresses safety and substance-related needs, coordinates within role, protects the partner from being assigned clinical policing responsibility, and integrates feasible family involvement. Goals should focus on agreed recovery behaviors, relational repair, functioning, support use, and review data rather than blame or promises.

CHAPTER 6Integrated Planning, Coordination, Documentation, and Review
BIG IDEA The plan remains clinically useful only when responsibilities, safety, coordination, documentation, feedback, and revision triggers are explicit.

Treatment planning continues after the initial plan is written. The therapist coordinates responsibly, documents the basis and agreed structure, monitors progress and fit, and revises when evidence changes. Review is not a ritual. It is the decision point that asks whether to continue, adapt, intensify, step down, refer, transfer, or begin transition.

The REVIEW Planning Feedback Loop

ComponentRequired Content
R - ResultsWhat changed in symptoms, functioning, relationships, safety, participation, and client-defined priorities?
E - ExperienceWhat do client and collateral systems report about alliance, burden, access, culture, preference, and fit?
V - Validity of the hypothesisDoes the formulation still explain the pattern, or did new evidence weaken it?
I - ImplementationWas the plan delivered as intended, feasible, accessible, and within role?
E - Exceptions and emerging risksWhat gains, setbacks, side effects, medication changes, crises, or contextual events affect the plan?
W - Way forwardContinue, adapt, intensify, step down, refer, transfer, or begin transition; document the rationale and next review.

Coordination Without Losing Role Clarity

Planning NeedTherapist Responsibility
Medication or health concernObserve and document relevant effects within competence; encourage or coordinate prescriber/medical evaluation with appropriate authorization; do not direct dose changes.
Specialty referralDefine the referral question, urgency, role, handoff, follow-up, and continuity rather than giving a name and assuming completion.
Collateral or community involvementClarify purpose, client status, information needed, responsibilities, and how the input affects the plan.
Safety planningIdentify warning signs, protective supports, responsibilities, emergency steps, monitoring, and level-of-care triggers.
Access barrierModify structure, communication, burden, or resources and track whether the adaptation improves participation.
Transition or transferPlan communication, referrals, records procedures, crisis information, and follow-up appropriate to the setting and client needs.

Documentation That Shows Planning Reasoning

  • Assessment and formulation evidence supporting the priority.
  • Client system, roles, participants, modality, frequency, and responsibilities.
  • Collaborative goals, objectives, measures, review points, and completion criteria.
  • Intervention rationale, competence, consultation, coordination, and referrals.
  • Safety responsibilities and level-of-care decisions when relevant.
  • Client preferences, disagreement, cultural/contextual factors, access adaptations, and material contract changes.
  • Progress data, implementation, alliance and fit feedback, plan revisions, and next steps.

AI-Assisted Treatment Planning

An AI-generated plan is a draft, not a clinical decision. The therapist remains responsible for privacy, consent when applicable, accuracy, source verification, bias review, competence, client collaboration, documentation, and the final plan. A polished format can conceal unsupported assumptions, fabricated data, generic goals, or interventions outside competence.

AI CONTROLDo not paste protected information into an unapproved system. Do not accept generated goals, diagnoses, measures, or interventions without verification. Do not let the tool replace collaboration, professional judgment, or documentation of who made the decision.

Case Lab 8: AI-Generated Plan

A documentation system generates a treatment plan after a brief intake. It adds “improve compliance,” assumes one partner is manipulative, recommends a model the therapist has never been trained to provide, and states that symptoms will resolve in eight sessions. The client system has not reviewed the plan.

  • Which statements are unsupported or biased?
  • What privacy and source-verification questions must be answered?
  • How should competence and client collaboration change the plan?
  • What should be documented about human review and correction?
MODEL REASONINGReject the output as a finished plan. Verify whether the tool was approved and how data were handled; remove unsupported and stigmatizing assumptions; rebuild goals collaboratively; select only interventions supported by formulation, evidence, competence, and feasibility; avoid guarantees; and document the therapist's review, corrections, client participation, and final decision.
