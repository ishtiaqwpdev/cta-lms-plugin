How to Use This Workbook

Workbook 3 teaches assessment as a disciplined, collaborative, and revisable process. The strongest AMFTRB answer is rarely the one that gathers the most facts or reaches the fastest conclusion. It is the response that identifies the client system and assessment task, notices urgency, tracks timeline and baseline, distinguishes evidence from inference, integrates relational and contextual information, and selects the next supported step.

Workbook Learning Objectives

  • Identify the client, treatment unit, assessment purpose, participation expectations, and immediate safety priorities before gathering detailed history.
  • Distinguish direct observation, client report, collateral report, record data, screening results, and clinical inference when organizing assessment information.
  • Apply a structured mental-status and baseline-change scan to determine when acute medical, substance-related, psychiatric, or safety evaluation is needed.
  • Map communication, interactional sequences, roles, rules, boundaries, alliances, hierarchies, and multigenerational patterns within the client system.
  • Evaluate how lifespan development, family-life-cycle transitions, loss, caregiving, and occupational demands affect problem formation and functioning.
  • Integrate culture, identity, spirituality, power, discrimination, language, disability, economic stress, environment, access, and community systems without stereotyping.
  • Screen substance use, trauma, childhood experiences, process-use concerns, sexual functioning, health factors, strengths, and resources and select the appropriate next assessment or referral step.
  • Form a provisional systemic hypothesis, identify missing decision-critical data, select the safest appropriate treatment unit or modality, and document the rationale for follow-up.

Observation vs. inference“Client looked at the floor and spoke softly” is observed. “Client is deceptive” is an inference requiring evidence.

CORE DECISION SEQUENCE
Who is the client system? What decision must the assessment support? Is there anything urgent? What changed from baseline? What is observed, reported, documented, measured, inferred, or unknown? What relational and contextual patterns matter? What must be assessed, referred, or clarified next?
CHAPTER 1
The Intake Frame: Client, Treatment Unit, Purpose, and Participation
BIG IDEA
Assessment begins by defining the question, the client system, and the conditions for safe and meaningful participation before collecting detailed history.

A referral may say “anxiety,” “family conflict,” “school refusal,” or “communication problems,” but those labels do not yet identify the clinical decision. The therapist must clarify who is being served, who is participating, who is affected, what the assessment is expected to determine, and what immediate conditions may change the sequence.

The INTAKE Systemic Assessment Sequence

StepQuestion the Learner Asks
I - Identify purpose and client systemWhat is the referral question, who is the client, who is participating, and who is affected?
N - Notice urgency and accessIs there immediate danger, acute change, coercion, medical concern, intoxication, communication barrier, or another condition that changes the sequence?
T - Track timeline and baselineWhen did the concern begin, what changed, what was typical before, and where does the pattern occur or not occur?
A - Assess across domainsWhat individual, relational, developmental, cultural, medical, substance, trauma, sexual, environmental, and strength information is needed?
K - Keep evidence and hypotheses distinctWhat is observed, reported, documented, measured, inferred, contradictory, or still unknown?
E - Engage and establish the next stepHow will the client system participate in priorities, collateral decisions, referrals, follow-up, and revision of the assessment?

Information Sources and Levels of Certainty

SourceExampleDocumentation Control
Direct observationOne partner interrupts three times and answers questions directed to the other.Describe what occurred without assigning motive.
Client reportThe client says panic began after a workplace transfer.Attribute the statement to the client and clarify timeline.
Collateral reportA parent reports that the adolescent has stopped attending school.Identify the source, purpose, and limits of the report.
Record dataAttendance records show a decline over six weeks.Name the record and avoid treating it as a complete explanation.
Screening resultA brief screen indicates further substance-use assessment is warranted.State what the screen supports and what it does not establish.
Clinical inferenceWithdrawal may increase when monitoring intensifies.Use tentative language and identify evidence that would test the hypothesis.
COMMON TRAP
The person who speaks most, pays the fee, makes the referral, or appears most distressed is not automatically the sole client or the most reliable source. Clarify the treatment unit and the clinical purpose before deciding whose account controls.

Case Lab 1: Couple Intake and Hidden Safety Concern

CASE LAB
Rina and Mark attend a couple intake after escalating arguments. Mark answers nearly every question. When conflict is discussed, Rina becomes quiet, looks toward the door, and says, “Whatever he says is fine.” Mark insists they should remain together for the entire assessment because “couples should not keep secrets.”
  • What is the current assessment purpose and treatment unit?
  • Which observations require clarification without assuming a cause?
  • What safety, voluntariness, and privacy questions should be addressed before routine conjoint assessment continues?
  • Which response would be premature: insisting on equal speaking time, diagnosing coercive control, or creating a safe opportunity for separate assessment?
MODEL REASONING
Clarify the couple-assessment purpose while recognizing that each participant’s safety and voluntary participation must be assessed. Document the observable interaction, create a safe opportunity for separate inquiry, and determine whether conjoint assessment remains appropriate. Do not force disclosure, promise secrecy beyond the assessment frame, or reach a fixed conclusion from one interaction.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY
Define the assessment frame before gathering detail. A clear frame protects participation, keeps information sources organized, and prevents the therapist from solving the wrong problem.

  • Clarify the referral question and requested decision.
  • Assess urgency and access before routine history.
  • Label observation, report, record, measure, and inference.
  • Engage the client system in priorities and next steps.
CHAPTER 2
Interviewing, Observation, Mental Status, and Change from Baseline
BIG IDEA
Mental-status findings become clinically useful when they are described accurately, compared with baseline, connected to functioning, and routed according to urgency.

Interviewing is not a race to complete a checklist. The therapist uses open and focused questions, observation, timeline, cross-setting comparison, and mental-status data to understand current functioning. The same behavior may have different meaning depending on the person’s baseline, development, culture, setting, medical status, substances, sleep, and recent change.

Observation Before Interpretation

Less DefensibleMore Defensible
“Client was manipulative.”“Client gave different timelines after the therapist asked about the discrepancy; the reason remains unclear.”
“Client had flat affect and did not care.”“Client’s facial expression and vocal range were limited while the client reported intense sadness.”
“Client was psychotic.”“Client reported hearing a voice others could not hear and had difficulty considering alternative explanations.”
“Partner is controlling.”“Partner answered questions directed to the client, interrupted repeatedly, and objected to separate assessment.”

The BASELINE Mental Status and Change Scan

A - Appearance and behaviorGrooming, motor activity, cooperation, eye contact, psychomotor change, and observable distress without pejorative labels.
S - Sensorium and cognitionOrientation, attention, memory, level of consciousness, language, and capacity to participate.

L - Language and thoughtSpeech, thought process, thought content, perception, coherence, and reality testing.

Acute Change Overrides Routine Technique

A sudden decline in orientation, attention, memory, consciousness, motor behavior, judgment, or functioning requires a different sequence than a longstanding relational pattern. The therapist should not assume avoidance, resistance, personality, or relationship conflict explains a new change before medical, substance-related, neurological, medication, sleep, or psychiatric causes are considered.

Case Lab 2: Acute Change in an Older Adult
During a couple session, 72-year-old Glenn repeats the same question several times, loses track of the conversation, and is unsure of the date. His spouse says, “He does this whenever he wants to avoid responsibility,” but reports that the confusion began yesterday after a medication change.
  • Which BASELINE findings are decision-critical?
  • What should the therapist do before continuing routine couple work?
  • How should the spouse’s interpretation be documented?
  • What continuity steps are needed while urgent evaluation is arranged?
MODEL REASONING
Treat the new cognitive change as urgent. Pause routine therapy, assess immediate safety and ability to participate, clarify medication and health information, and arrange prompt medical evaluation. Document observable findings, the spouse’s report and interpretation as separate sources, the action taken, and the continuity plan.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY
Mental-status assessment is not a diagnosis. It is an organized description of current functioning that helps determine what must be clarified, protected, or evaluated next.
  • Describe behavior before interpreting it.
  • Compare current findings with baseline and development.
  • Distinguish reported mood from observed affect.
  • Treat sudden cognitive or functional change as a routing problem.
  • Document source, uncertainty, action, and follow-up.
CHAPTER 3
Relational Patterns, Genograms, Family Life Cycle, and Development
BIG IDEA
Systemic assessment maps reciprocal sequences, relational organization, multigenerational patterns, and developmental transitions without turning the map into proof of causation.

Relational assessment asks what happens before, during, and after the presenting concern; how each response influences the next; and how roles, rules, boundaries, alliances, hierarchy, and larger systems maintain or interrupt the pattern. The goal is to organize interaction—not to assign equal blame or assume that every symptom originates in the family.

The MAPS Relational Assessment Map

StepRelational Question
M - MembersWho is in the client system, who is absent, and how are subsystems, generations, and collateral systems organized?
A - ActionsWhat sequence repeats before, during, and after the concern, and how does each response influence the next?
P - Positions and powerWhat roles, rules, boundaries, alliances, coalitions, hierarchy, loyalties, and decision rights shape the pattern?
S - Strengths and shiftsWhere are exceptions, resilience, supportive relationships, flexible responses, and safer leverage points?

From Content to Sequence

Content StatementSequence Description
“They fight about homework.”Caregiver anxiety increases monitoring; the adolescent delays or hides work; monitoring intensifies; both become more certain the other is the problem.
“One partner is emotionally unavailable.”Requests for reassurance increase when distance is sensed; the other partner withdraws when requests feel critical; distance and pursuit both intensify.
“The child controls the family.”Adults disagree about limits; the child escalates during the disagreement; adults temporarily unite around the child; the original adult conflict remains unresolved.

Genograms and Family Maps: Use and Limits

A family map can organize relationships, transitions, losses, health and substance history, immigration, caregiving, conflict, cutoffs, alliances, resources, and recurring themes across generations. The diagram is a question generator—not a verdict. Similar events may have different meanings, and apparent repetition does not establish a single cause.

DEVELOPMENTAL CONTROL
Assess actual capacity, expectations, and context rather than using age or family stage as an automatic explanation. A transition such as childbirth, launching, remarriage, caregiving, migration, or retirement may create strain, but the therapist must assess how this client system is experiencing it.

Case Lab 3: Adolescent, Family, and School Reports

CASE LAB
Fifteen-year-old Devin says nothing is wrong and that adults are overreacting. Caregivers report withdrawal and irritability. School records show a sharp attendance decline beginning after repeated peer harassment. At home, caregivers respond with tighter monitoring, and Devin becomes more secretive.
  • What does each source contribute, and what remains unknown?
  • How would MAPS describe the monitoring-secrecy sequence?
  • What developmental and safety questions are needed?
  • What provisional hypothesis fits the current evidence without choosing a “most credible” informant?
MODEL REASONING
Integrate the adolescent report, caregiver observations, and school data as different sources and settings. Assess bullying impact, mood, safety, development, peer support, and cross-setting functioning. Describe the reciprocal monitoring-secrecy pattern while keeping contextual and individual explanations open.

  • Use genograms to generate questions, not fixed conclusions.

CHAPTER 4
Culture, Context, Environment, Strengths, and Access
BIG IDEA
Context is not background information. It shapes what the client system experiences, what participation costs, which explanations fit, and which assessment methods are accessible and trustworthy.

Culturally responsive assessment asks how identity, migration, spirituality, disability, language, privilege, discrimination, economic stress, community systems, and institutional conditions affect functioning and participation. The therapist remains curious about within-group variation and avoids using cultural information as a stereotype or a substitute for direct inquiry.

The LENSES Context and Hypothesis Grid

StepContext and Hypothesis Action
L - Life stageAssess developmental capacity, family-life-cycle transitions, caregiving, loss, and age-related expectations without stereotyping.
E - EnvironmentAssess housing, work, school, military or first-responder demands, neighborhood safety, migration, discrimination, and economic stress.
N - NetworksIdentify family, peers, faith communities, schools, health systems, agencies, online resources, and other supports or pressures.
S - Social location and meaningAsk how identity, culture, spirituality, language, disability, privilege, oppression, and power shape the client system’s experience.
E - Embodied factorsScreen health, medications, sleep, substances, trauma, sexual functioning, sensory needs, and physical or cognitive change.
S - Systemic hypothesisIntegrate patterns, context, strengths, and missing information into a tentative explanation that can be tested and revised.

Contextual Inquiry Without Stereotyping

Risky ShortcutResponsive Alternative
Assume a cultural value explains the conflict.Ask which identities, values, traditions, and social conditions matter to each participant in this situation.
Use a child or partner as interpreter for complex clinical material.Arrange qualified language access and assess how interpretation affects privacy, hierarchy, and participation.
Describe poverty, disability, migration, or discrimination as an individual deficit.Assess structural barriers, strengths, resource limits, community supports, and the client system’s meaning.
Treat spirituality as either pathology or protection.Ask how spiritual beliefs, practices, communities, conflict, and support affect functioning and decisions.

Case Lab 4: Migration, Language, and Family Transition

CASE LAB
A recently immigrated family attends because of escalating parent-adolescent conflict. The family has been using the 13-year-old sibling as interpreter. A parent emphasizes family obligation and safety; the adolescent emphasizes privacy and independence. Housing is unstable, one parent works nights, and the family has limited transportation.
  • What access correction should occur before deeper assessment?
  • Which migration, loss, developmental, work, housing, and power factors require inquiry?
  • How can the therapist explore meaning without choosing a cultural side?
  • What strengths and networks may support participation and continuity?
MODEL REASONING
Arrange qualified language access, clarify roles and privacy, and assess each participant’s meaning. Map migration and loss, developmental transition, work schedule, housing, transportation, family obligation, safety concerns, discrimination, and available supports. Keep multiple hypotheses open and avoid framing either autonomy or obligation as inherently healthier.

Strengths Are Required Assessment Data

Strengths include coping, exceptions, prior successful change, motivation, supportive relationships, cultural and spiritual resources, practical skills, community ties, and client-defined protective factors. Strengths should shape hypotheses and feasible next steps, but they must not be used to minimize risk, oppression, or unmet needs.

Ask what context means to this client system and how it changes assessment accuracy, participation, safety, and feasible options. Contextual humility is more reliable than cultural certainty.
  • Ask rather than assume.
  • Treat language and disability access as clinical conditions.
  • Assess structural pressures and community systems.
  • Include strengths and feasible supports in every hypothesis.
  • Revise the formulation when context changes the meaning of the facts.
CHAPTER 5
Integrated Screening: Safety, Health, Substances, Trauma, Sexuality, and Collateral Data
BIG IDEA
Screen broadly enough to identify urgent, medical, substance-related, trauma-related, sexual, and specialty concerns, then route the next step without diagnosing beyond the evidence.

Integrated assessment does not mean conducting every full specialty evaluation. The therapist asks enough respectful, direct, and context-sensitive questions to determine whether routine psychotherapy can continue, whether safety or medical needs control the sequence, and whether consultation, collateral information, or specialty referral is required.

The SCREEN and ROUTE Matrix

SCREENING CONTROL
A positive screen is not a complete diagnosis. It indicates what must be assessed next, how urgently, by whom, and with what continuity plan.

Case Lab 5A: Postpartum Distress and Family Reorganization

CASE LAB
Six weeks after childbirth, Noor reports tearfulness, detachment, severe sleep loss, and thoughts that the family would be better without her. Her partner says this is “normal new-parent stress” and is working extended shifts. Infant care is inconsistent, and Noor recently changed medication.
  • What safety, mood, sleep, medical, medication, and infant-care questions must be addressed first?
  • How does the family transition and partner response affect the assessment?
  • Which referrals or coordination steps may be needed?
  • Why would immediate communication-skills training be premature?
MODEL REASONING
Directly assess suicide and infant-safety concerns, current functioning, sleep, medication and health factors, support, and ability to care for self and infant. Treat minimization by the partner as one source, not the conclusion. Arrange the needed urgent clinical or medical evaluation and coordinate continuity before routine couple intervention.

Case Lab 5B: Collateral Conflict and Referral

CASE LAB
A client denies any alcohol problem. The spouse reports impaired driving, missed work, and arguments after drinking. The client permits only limited coordination with a primary-care clinician and does not want the spouse contacted outside sessions.
  • What can be assessed directly without turning the session into a credibility contest?
  • What safety and substance-use questions are decision-critical?
  • How should the limits of collateral coordination be clarified?
  • What referral question and documentation are appropriate?
MODEL REASONING
Assess use pattern, impairment, driving risk, withdrawal history, health and medication factors, consequences, and readiness. Treat the spouse’s report as collateral data while documenting the client’s report separately. Clarify the permitted coordination, identify whether specialty or medical assessment is needed, and preserve alliance and continuity.

CHAPTER 6
Provisional Systemic Hypotheses, Treatment-Unit Selection, and Next-Step Decisions
BIG IDEA A strong assessment ends with a tentative, evidence-linked hypothesis, explicit missing data, an appropriate treatment unit, and a supported next step that can be reviewed and revised.

A provisional systemic hypothesis organizes what is known while preserving uncertainty. It should connect presenting concerns, interactional patterns, development, context, strengths, and screening findings. It should also state what evidence is missing and what would strengthen, weaken, or replace the current explanation.

The Provisional Hypothesis Formula

WORKING FORMULA
Current concern + timeline and baseline + reciprocal pattern + developmental and contextual conditions + embodied and screening factors + strengths/resources + missing data = tentative explanation and next assessment step.

Keep Competing Hypotheses Open

EvidencePossible HypothesesWhat Would Clarify
Adolescent withdrawal after school decline and tighter monitoringBullying-related distress; depressive symptoms; family conflict; substance use; developmental privacy struggle; more than one factor.Direct safety and mood assessment, peer context, substance screen, timeline, school data, exceptions, and functioning across settings.
New confusion during couple therapy after medication changeMedication or medical effect; delirium; substance effect; neurological change; psychiatric process; relational avoidance is not excluded but is not sufficient.Urgent medical evaluation, medication review, onset and fluctuation, cognition, safety, and collateral history.
Sexual avoidance and escalating couple conflictPain or medical factor; medication effect; trauma response; identity or relationship meaning; desire discrepancy; coercion; cultural shame; relational cycle.Consent-sensitive separate and conjoint inquiry, medical history, safety, timeline, meaning, and referral need.

Treatment-Unit and Modality Fit

OptionUse WhenContraindications / Questions
IndividualThe individual is the current client, privacy or safety requires separate work, or focused assessment supports the larger plan.Does individual work create a hidden alliance or conflict with the agreed treatment unit?
CoupleBoth partners can participate safely and voluntarily, goals are relational, and conjoint assessment improves understanding.Is there coercion, retaliation risk, undisclosed safety concern, or inability to participate freely?
FamilyFamily interaction, development, caregiving, or multigenerational patterns are central and members can participate appropriately.Who is missing, overpowered, developmentally unable, or placed in an inappropriate role?
Teletherapy or hybridJurisdiction, access, privacy, technology, clinical fit, and emergency planning support the task.Can identity, location, privacy, accessibility, safety, and participation be maintained?

COMMON TRAP
The largest treatment unit is not automatically the most systemic, and referral is not automatically the safest answer. Select the smallest effective safe structure that fits the assessment purpose, client participation, competence, and continuity needs.

Integrated Assessment and Hypothesis Lab

CASE LAB
Elena, age 38, seeks help for panic, irritability, and conflict with her spouse. Symptoms intensified after a promotion requiring rotating shifts. She sleeps four hours, uses increasing amounts of alcohol to unwind, and recently stopped attending her faith community. Her spouse increases checking and reassurance; Elena withdraws and hides drinking. Both want couple therapy, but Elena is unsure whether alcohol is relevant.
  • Use INTAKE: identify the client system, purpose, urgency, timeline, and information sources.
  • Use BASELINE: identify current functioning and decision-critical change.
  • Use MAPS: describe the reciprocal checking-withdrawal sequence.
  • Use LENSES: integrate work schedule, sleep, alcohol, faith/community loss, and strengths.
  • Write two competing hypotheses and identify what evidence would clarify them.
  • Select the current treatment unit, referral or coordination needs, and next step.
MODEL REASONING
The current presentation may involve panic, sleep disruption, alcohol-related impairment, occupational stress, relational accommodation and withdrawal, and loss of support. Assess immediate safety, use pattern and withdrawal risk, health and medication factors, timeline, cross-setting functioning, and treatment goals. Couple assessment may remain appropriate if participation is safe, while individual or specialty substance assessment and medical coordination may also be needed. Keep the formulation provisional and document the rationale for each next step.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY
A defensible assessment shows how the therapist moved from evidence to a tentative explanation and next step. It names uncertainty, protects safety, includes context and strengths, and remains open to revision.
