How to Use This Workbook

Workbook 9 teaches complex-presentation reasoning when several explanations and service needs compete for attention. Identify the client and treatment unit, separate urgent from nonurgent concerns, build a provisional multidimensional systemic hypothesis, select an appropriately scoped response, coordinate with informed permission, and revise from safety, functioning, alliance, access, and outcome data.

LEARN -> ORGANIZE -> APPLY -> ANALYZE -> REPAIR Study the six chapters in order. Use the six reasoning tools to organize each case, complete all eight case labs and the reusable decision record, answer all ten formative questions before reading the rationales, and remediate every miss or low-confidence response. Mnemonics do not replace direct assessment, medical evaluation, current law, ethics, specialty competence, consultation, supervision, or individualized care.
SAFETY AND STABILITY OVERRIDE
Immediate danger, intoxication or withdrawal, overdose risk, acute medical or psychiatric instability, abuse, coercion, exploitation, neglect, or inability to participate voluntarily requires direct assessment, stabilization, and an appropriate level-of-care response before routine outpatient technique.

Workbook Learning Objectives

  • Integrate biological, psychological, relational, developmental, cultural, spiritual, environmental, and structural information into a provisional systemic hypothesis without diagnostic overshadowing.
  • Assess substance-use patterns, intoxication or withdrawal concerns, co-occurring conditions, motivation, recovery supports, family impact, and level-of-care indicators within professional scope.
  • Conduct inclusive sexuality and sexual-health inquiry using permission, client-defined language, consent and coercion screening, trauma sensitivity, reproductive context, and competence limits.
  • Evaluate chronic illness, disability, pain, sleep, medication effects, cognitive or functional change, access needs, caregiving patterns, and bidirectional effects on the client system.
  • Distinguish developmentally and culturally expectable transitions and grief from clinically significant disorder, trauma response, acute risk, exploitation, neglect, or functional decline.
  • Select and adapt treatment goals, modality, pacing, family involvement, recovery supports, referrals, and integrated-care roles to the presentation, preferences, access, and evidence.
  • Recognize safety and stabilization priorities, determine when a higher or different level of care is indicated, and coordinate emergency, medical, addiction, or specialty resources without abandoning the client system.
  • Use alliance, functioning, safety, adherence, quality-of-life, and outcome information to continue, adapt, step up, step down, transfer, or end a complex-care plan with clear documentation and continuity.

High-Yield Complex-Presentation Distinctions

DistinctionDecision rule
Multidimensional formulation vs. list makingExplain how the most relevant factors interact, identify what is provisional, and state what new evidence would change the hypothesis.
Urgent vs. importantAddress danger, instability, withdrawal, overdose, abuse, coercion, neglect, and participation capacity before routine outpatient goals.
Recovery support vs. pathway impositionClarify goals, readiness, evidence, resources, and risks without making one identity, support model, or endpoint universally correct.
Inclusive inquiry vs. assumptionAsk permission and distinguish anatomy, identity, behavior, desire, function, consent, relationship structure, and context.
Health context vs. medical practiceAssess relational and functional effects, coordinate with permission, and refer for medical evaluation without diagnosing or prescribing beyond role.

Referral vs. abandonmentExplain rationale, coordinate a warm handoff when possible, clarify continuing roles, and preserve continuity.
More services vs. better careChange intensity because current need and response data support it, not because complexity automatically requires more.

CHAPTER 1

Complex Presentation Integration, Scope, and Treatment Unit
BIG IDEA
Complexity requires disciplined narrowing, not a single-cause story. Map interacting conditions and relational sequences, identify the decision point, test acuity and scope, then keep the hypothesis revisable.

The MOSAIC Formulation Sequence

StepClinical task
M - Map interacting conditions and relational sequencesGather biological, psychological, relational, developmental, cultural, spiritual, environmental, and structural information; describe observable feedback loops.
O - Observe function, acuity, strengths, and contextTrack changes in daily function, participation, safety, resources, protective factors, access, and the conditions under which the concern changes.
S - Select client, treatment unit, and decision pointClarify who is the client, who participates, what collateral roles exist, what decision must be made now, and whose consent is required.
A - Assess safety, readiness, access, and scopeSeparate urgent from nonurgent needs and determine whether the therapist has the competence, setting, information, and support to proceed.
I - Integrate provisional hypothesesState the leading interactional explanation, plausible alternatives, evidence for and against each, and what information would change the plan.
C - Check response and reviseUse alliance, function, safety, adherence, barriers, quality of life, and outcome indicators to continue, adapt, coordinate, step up/down, transfer, or end.

Build a Provisional Hypothesis, Not a Verdict

QuestionComplex-presentation answer
What changed?Specify timing, course, severity, function, relationships, environment, and what preceded the change.
What interacts?Connect symptoms, health, substances, sleep, development, trauma, medication, caregiving, culture, resources, and relational responses.
What is urgent?Directly assess danger, instability, intoxication or withdrawal, overdose, abuse, coercion, neglect, and inability to participate.
What is the treatment unit?Name the identified client, participants, collateral sources, confidentiality boundaries, and target of intervention.
What is outside role?Identify medical, prescribing, withdrawal-management, specialty, legal, protective, or higher-level services that require coordination or referral.
What will change the hypothesis?Predefine new findings, nonresponse, adverse effects, functional change, or consultation results that require revision.

Prevent Diagnostic Overshadowing

  • Do not attribute every new symptom or conflict to an existing psychiatric diagnosis, disability, substance pattern, trauma history, age, identity, or relationship problem.
  • Ask what is new, what is baseline, what is worsening, and what information comes from the client versus collateral sources.
  • Consider medication effects, sleep, pain, acute illness, cognitive or functional change, environmental stress, oppression, and access barriers before narrowing.
  • Preserve strengths and client meaning while documenting impairment and risk precisely.
  • Use consultation and referral when the differential or level-of-care decision exceeds competence; maintain appropriate continuity and follow-up.

Case Lab 1: When One Diagnosis Explains Everything

DeShawn, who has a history of depression, attends with his spouse after three weeks of new confusion, irritability, missed medication doses, poor sleep, and falls. The spouse assumes the depression has returned. The therapist is considering increasing session frequency without asking about recent medical changes or clarifying who prescribed the medication.

Decision point: What should the therapist assess and coordinate before treating this as routine depressive relapse?

MODEL REASONING
Use MOSAIC. Clarify baseline and timing, directly assess safety and function, ask about medication and health changes within scope, identify the client and collateral roles, obtain permission for appropriate medical coordination, and preserve several provisional hypotheses. New confusion and falls require timely medical evaluation rather than diagnostic overshadowing.

CHAPTER 2

Substance Use, Co-Occurring Conditions, and Recovery Systems
BIG IDEA
Assess pattern, function, acuity, co-occurring conditions, readiness, recovery capital, family effects, and level of care. Preserve multiple recovery pathways while acting directly on withdrawal, overdose, and acute instability.

The RECOVER Sequence

StepClinical task
R - Recognize pattern, function, and recovery contextAsk about substance, route, amount, frequency, timing, context, perceived benefits, consequences, past change, and current recovery supports.
E - Evaluate intoxication, withdrawal, overdose, and co-occurring riskAssess reported and observed signs, medical or psychiatric instability, self-harm/violence risk, pregnancy context when relevant, and need for emergency or specialty evaluation.
C - Clarify goals and readinessElicit the client's concerns, priorities, confidence, ambivalence, language, and preferred outcomes without covertly imposing a pathway.
O - Organize level of care and supportsMatch needs to appropriate care using qualified addiction/medical consultation and current criteria when beyond MFT scope.
V - Verify systemic effects and resourcesMap family responses, reinforcement, caregiving, children/dependents, housing, work, culture, spirituality, peer support, and recovery capital.
E - Engage appropriate careClarify roles, obtain permission, coordinate medical/addiction/community resources, and create a warm handoff and continuity plan.
R - Review responseTrack safety, use, craving, function, relationships, attendance, barriers, quality of life, and unintended effects; revise level and plan as indicated.

Assessment Domains Without Practicing Medicine

DomainAsk / observeBoundary
Pattern and functionWhat is used, how, when, with whom, for what effect, and with what short- and long-term consequences?Do not infer severity from one label, substance, or family report.
AcuityCurrent intoxication, withdrawal concerns, overdose indicators, medical/psychiatric instability, driving, caregiving, and immediate safety.Emergencies and withdrawal management require appropriate medical care.
Co-occurrenceMood, anxiety, trauma, psychosis, cognition, sleep, pain, medication, and health symptoms across time.Keep causal conclusions provisional and coordinate differential evaluation.
Readiness and recoveryGoals, ambivalence, prior attempts, strengths, recovery capital, treatment/support preferences, and barriers.Do not make one recovery identity or pathway mandatory.
Family systemAccommodation, conflict, secrecy, protection, reinforcement, burden, children/dependents, and safety.Family involvement requires rationale, safety assessment, and appropriate consent.
OVERDOSE AND WITHDRAWAL BOUNDARY
Suspected overdose, severe withdrawal, altered consciousness, respiratory compromise, seizure, delirium, or acute medical instability requires emergency activation and medical care according to current local protocols. Relationship treatment does not substitute for stabilization.

Case Lab 2: Morning Tremor and Couple Conflict

Luis reports escalating alcohol use and morning tremors. His partner says arguments are worse and wants communication training today. Luis is sweating, appears unsteady, and says he plans to stop drinking abruptly after the session. The therapist has not assessed withdrawal history, recent use, medical status, or transportation.

Decision point: What takes priority before routine couple work?

MODEL REASONING
Use RECOVER and STEADY. Assess current instability and immediate danger, obtain urgent medical evaluation or emergency care as indicated, and avoid directing unsupervised withdrawal. Clarify transportation, dependents, and the partner's safety. Couple work can resume only after the immediate level-of-care need is addressed and roles are clear.

CHAPTER 3

Sexuality, Sexual Health, Reproductive Context, and Relational Care
BIG IDEA
Use permission, privacy, inclusive language, and behaviorally specific inquiry. Distinguish identity, anatomy, behavior, desire, function, relationship structure, consent, safety, health, trauma, culture, and reproductive context without assumption.

The OPENS Sequence

StepClinical task
O - Offer permission, privacy, and inclusive languageExplain why the topic may matter, ask permission, clarify confidentiality and participation, and use the client's terms for body, identity, partners, and relationships.
P - Pinpoint the concern and client-defined goalClarify who experiences the concern, what has changed, how it affects function and relationships, and what useful care would look like.
E - Evaluate consent, coercion, safety, trauma, medical, and relational factorsAsk behaviorally specific questions without assuming that desire, agreement, silence, identity, or relationship status establishes consent.
N - Normalize variation without assumptionUse accurate information and a nonpathologizing stance while remaining alert to distress, impairment, exploitation, trauma, pain, and health needs.
S - Scope treatment, adapt, coordinate, or referDetermine what fits MFT competence; coordinate medical, sexual-health, reproductive, pelvic-health, trauma, or specialty services with permission and continuity.

Separate the Dimensions

DimensionDo not assume
IdentityIdentity predicts anatomy, partners, behavior, desire, fertility goals, or relationship structure.
Anatomy and healthThe therapist knows which terms, organs, procedures, or reproductive possibilities apply without asking.
Behavior and practicesRelationship status, orientation, or appearance describes actual sexual behavior or exposure.
Desire and functionDiscrepant desire means dysfunction, lack of love, infidelity, coercion, or a single medical cause.
Consent and coercionPrior consent, marriage, silence, compliance, or absence of physical force establishes current voluntary agreement.
Culture and spiritualityA group identity determines the client's values, language, disclosure preferences, or meaning.

When to Coordinate or Refer

  • New pain, bleeding, sudden functional change, medication-related concerns, pregnancy/reproductive needs, or other health questions require appropriate medical or sexual-health evaluation.
  • Coercion, violence, exploitation, inability to consent, or immediate danger changes the task to safety and jurisdiction-specific protective action.
  • Specialized sex therapy, trauma treatment, reproductive counseling, pelvic-health care, or medical treatment requires relevant competence and role clarity.
  • Do not make disclosure to a partner a universal condition of care; assess law, ethics, safety, identified client, consent, and clinical context.
  • Document permission, language preferences, boundaries, referrals, coordination, and the basis for the chosen treatment unit.

Case Lab 4: Desire Discrepancy and Pressure

A couple reports different levels of sexual desire. One partner says, “If you loved me, you would agree,” while the other looks down and says they usually consent to avoid conflict. The requesting partner wants techniques to increase frequency. The therapist has not met with either partner privately or assessed retaliation, pain, trauma, or medical factors.

Decision point: What should the therapist do before routine desire-focused intervention?

MODEL REASONING
Use OPENS. Establish privacy and client-defined language, assess voluntariness, coercion, retaliation, safety, trauma, pain, and health factors, and clarify whether conjoint work is safe and appropriate. Normalize desire variation without normalizing pressure. Coordinate or refer within competence and current jurisdictional duties.

CHAPTER 4

Health, Disability, Medication, and Integrated Care
BIG IDEA
Health and disability affect and are affected by relationships, function, access, identity, and resources. Assess the systemic impact, avoid diagnostic overshadowing and ableism, adapt access, and coordinate medical information with permission and role clarity.

The BRIDGE Coordination Sequence

StepClinical task
B - Build the shared care questionState the specific clinical question, the identified client, why coordination may help, and what the client wants from it.
R - Review health, medication, disability, function, and accessClarify client-reported diagnoses, symptoms, sleep, pain, medication experience, cognition, daily function, accommodations, and barriers without practicing medicine.
I - Identify bidirectional relational effectsMap how health affects roles and relationships and how relational stress, resources, caregiving, and access affect health management.
D - Define consent, roles, and information limitsObtain informed permission, identify recipients, specify the minimum necessary information, communication method, and each professional's role.
G - Generate an adapted planAdjust format, pacing, communication, goals, caregiving expectations, referrals, and supports to improve equitable participation.
E - Evaluate and communicate responseTrack function, access, burden, safety, adherence barriers, alliance, and outcomes; update collaborators and the client within permission and need.

Anti-Ableist and Access-Responsive Practice

PracticeApplication
Ask, do not presumeAsk what access, communication, pacing, sensory, mobility, cognitive, fatigue, pain, or support adaptations are useful.
Separate impairment from environmentDistinguish body or cognitive limitations from inaccessible systems, stigma, role expectations, transportation, cost, and communication barriers.
Protect autonomyDo not default to a caregiver as decision-maker or interpreter; clarify the client's preferences, capacity, privacy, and legal authority.
Adapt measurementUse accessible language, format, response mode, timing, and interpretation; document meaningful functional outcomes.
Coordinate preciselyShare only necessary information with permission, clarify uncertainty, and avoid presenting therapist impressions as medical conclusions.

Medication and Medical Questions

  • Ask what the client notices, when it began, what changed, and how symptoms affect function and relationships.
  • Do not recommend starting, stopping, increasing, decreasing, or substituting medication beyond professional authority and competence.
  • New confusion, falls, severe sedation, agitation, chest pain, respiratory symptoms, seizures, or other acute changes require appropriate medical evaluation.
  • With informed permission, communicate concise observations, the client's questions, relevant function, and the purpose of coordination.
  • Continue to address relational responses, access, adherence barriers, caregiving strain, and meaning while medical professionals address medical care.

Case Lab 5: Fatigue After a Medication Change

After a medication change by a prescriber, Mei reports severe fatigue, irritability, and sleeping through family commitments. Her family labels the behavior “lazy” and wants the therapist to persuade her to stop the medication. Mei wants help communicating with the prescriber and reducing conflict.

Decision point: How should the therapist respond within role?

MODEL REASONING
Use BRIDGE. Assess timing, function, safety, health and sleep context, and the relational feedback loop. Reject the character label, clarify that medication decisions belong with the qualified prescriber, obtain Mei's permission for focused coordination, and adapt family expectations while the medical question is evaluated.

CHAPTER 5

Developmental Transitions, Aging, Caregiving, Grief, and Loss
BIG IDEA
Locate concerns in development, family life cycle, culture, spirituality, and material context. Honor grief and variation while directly assessing impairment, trauma, depression, suicide risk, capacity, neglect, exploitation, and caregiver-system needs.

The SEASONS Sequence

StepClinical task
S - Situate the concern in development and family life cycleClarify expected tasks, transitions, role changes, timing, history, and the meanings each participant assigns.
E - Elicit meaning, culture, spirituality, and ritualAsk how the client and community understand the transition or loss; avoid prescribing a universal timeline or expression.
A - Assess grief, disorder, trauma, function, and safetyEvaluate course, severity, distress, impairment, trauma, depression, substance use, suicide risk, cognition, capacity, abuse, neglect, and exploitation as indicated.
S - Support adaptive tasks and relationshipsHelp the system revise roles, communicate needs, tolerate differing rhythms, preserve connection, and engage chosen rituals or meaning-making.
O - Organize caregiver and system resourcesAssess burden, respite, practical supports, care coordination, finances, housing, legal authority, transportation, and community resources within scope.
N - Notice nonresponse and unintended effectsTrack function, isolation, risk, caregiver strain, alliance, and whether the plan is inaccessible, culturally mismatched, or insufficient.
S - Step, refer, or reviseContinue, adapt, coordinate, step up/down, refer, transfer, or terminate with continuity based on the changing need and response.

Differentiate Without Policing Grief

ConsiderClinical question
Meaning and normsHow do culture, spirituality, family, community, and the relationship to the loss shape expression and timing?
Course and functionWhat changed across home, work, school, caregiving, sleep, health, relationships, and self-care?
Differential evidenceAre depression, trauma, prolonged grief, substance use, medical factors, or another condition better supported, overlapping, or uncertain?
SafetyIs there suicidal thinking or behavior, self-neglect, abuse, exploitation, inability to meet basic needs, or acute instability?
Resources and barriersWhat support, ritual, respite, practical care, accessibility, or community connection is wanted and available?
ResponseIs the plan improving connection, function, meaning, and safety, or increasing isolation, shame, burden, or avoidance?

Aging, Capacity, and Caregiving

  • Do not treat age as diagnosis or assume cognitive decline, dependence, incapacity, or family authority.
  • New cognitive or functional change requires appropriate medical evaluation and a careful differential, especially when onset is acute.
  • Assess caregiver burden without making stress an excuse for mistreatment; evaluate safety, neglect, exploitation, and available support.
  • Capacity is decision-specific and may fluctuate; follow current jurisdictional law and qualified evaluation requirements.
  • When abuse, neglect, or exploitation is suspected, use current law, consultation, safety planning, and protective resources rather than a universal national rule.

Case Lab 7: Grief, Culture, and Functional Decline

Fourteen months after her wife's death, Amara continues daily mourning rituals valued in her faith community. She also reports intense longing, severe isolation, loss of work functioning, increased alcohol use, and thoughts that life is not worth living. A relative says the rituals prove she is “stuck,” while Amara fears therapy will disrespect her culture.

Decision point: How should the therapist differentiate and respond?

MODEL REASONING
Use SEASONS. Respect the meaning of the rituals and avoid using duration alone. Directly assess suicide risk, substance use, function, trauma, depression, and prolonged-grief features; identify cultural and spiritual supports; and coordinate the appropriate level of care. Cultural humility and diagnostic assessment are complementary.

CHAPTER 6

Trauma Complexity, Level of Care, Coordination, and Family-System Adaptation
BIG IDEA
Stabilization and participation capacity control sequence. Coordinate complex care with informed permission, precise roles, minimum necessary information, warm handoffs, response monitoring, and continuity rather than adding disconnected services.

The STEADY Sequence

StepClinical task
S - Screen immediate danger and instabilityAssess suicide, violence, abuse, coercion, exploitation, neglect, overdose, withdrawal, acute medical or psychiatric change, and inability to care for self or dependents.
T - Triage medical, psychiatric, substance, abuse, and neglect needsIdentify what requires emergency care, protective response, specialty evaluation, consultation, or a different setting now.
E - Establish stabilization and participation capacitySupport orientation, regulation, practical safety, basic needs, and voluntary ability to engage before trauma processing or routine conjoint work.
A - Align level of care and rolesSelect appropriate care and clarify the client, family, MFT, medical, addiction, protective, rehabilitation, school, and community roles.
D - Document and coordinateRecord findings, rationale, consultation, consent, information limits, handoff, client response, and follow-up responsibilities.
Y - Yield to reassessment and continuity planningReassess safety, function, access, alliance, and outcomes; revise the plan and maintain continuity across transitions.

Integrated Care Requires Role Clarity

QuestionRequired clarification
Who is the client?Name the identified client and the purpose and status of every participant and collateral source.
What permission exists?Specify what may be shared, with whom, for what purpose, by what method, for how long, and how it may be withdrawn within current law.
What is each role?Differentiate psychotherapy, medical care, prescribing, addiction treatment, case management, rehabilitation, school, protective, peer, legal, and family-support functions.
What information is necessary?Share focused, accurate information relevant to the care question rather than the entire record by default.
Who follows up?Assign responsibility for referrals, handoff confirmation, safety monitoring, record receipt, missed contacts, and next review.
What if care fails?Predefine how barriers, refusal, nonresponse, worsening, service gaps, or loss of access will trigger outreach and revision.

Trauma Complexity and Pacing

  • Assess complex trauma, dissociation, substance use, health needs, housing, family instability, coercion, and ongoing exposure before assuming trauma processing is timely.
  • Stabilization is not a permanent avoidance category; define the capacity and safety indicators needed for the next phase.
  • Do not use conjoint treatment when it increases danger, coercion, retaliation, or inability to speak freely.
  • Coordinate services because each has an explicit role in one plan; more providers without information boundaries and ownership can increase fragmentation.
  • Track outcomes by participant and setting, including adverse effects, alliance, access, burden, and what happens between sessions.

Case Lab 8: Complexity, Nonresponse, and Fragmented Care

Sam has trauma symptoms, intermittent stimulant use, uncontrolled diabetes, housing instability, and escalating family conflict. The therapist has added coping worksheets and trauma narratives while symptoms and attendance worsen. A physician and case manager are involved, but releases, roles, and follow-up responsibilities are unclear. Sam reports feeling managed rather than heard.

Decision point: What should the therapist do before adding another intervention?

MODEL REASONING
Use STEADY and return to MOSAIC. Reassess danger, medical and substance instability, participation capacity, housing and access, and the treatment unit. Repair the alliance, obtain focused permission, clarify each role and information limit, coordinate a coherent plan and level of care, and define who follows up. Revise or pause trauma processing until safety and capacity support it.

Answer Key and Detailed Rationales

Review the clinical decision, not only the letter. For every miss or low-confidence correct answer, identify the remediation code and write a transferable rule.

1. Correct Answer: C

Why A: Diagnostic overshadowing. An existing anxiety diagnosis does not explain new confusion, falls, and fatigue without further assessment.

Why B: Poorly sequenced. Family treatment may later help, but the acute change and safety question come first.

Why C: Correct. The option assesses acuity and function, preserves a differential, and coordinates medical evaluation within consent and role.

Why D: Unsafe delay. Staying within MFT scope means arranging appropriate evaluation, not ignoring a potentially urgent medical change.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
New acute function or cognition changes override the convenience of an existing diagnosis.

Transfer rule: Prevent diagnostic overshadowing by assessing change, safety, and appropriate medical coordination. Remediation: R1/R5

2. Correct Answer: A

Why A: Correct. Possible withdrawal and current instability require direct assessment and qualified medical or emergency care before routine couple intervention.

Why B: Unsafe sequencing. Relationship stress may matter, but communication training does not treat withdrawal risk.

Why C: Outside role and unsafe. A therapist should not prescribe a home withdrawal schedule.

Why D: Unsafe. Partner supervision is not a substitute for appropriate medical evaluation and could create additional risk.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Tremor, sweating, instability, and abrupt cessation cues move the decision to acuity and level of care.

Transfer rule: Address intoxication, withdrawal, overdose, and medical instability before routine therapy. Remediation: R2/R9

3. Correct Answer: B

Why A: Pathway imposition. The therapist's preferred recovery identity or group is not universally required.

Why B: Correct. The response is person-driven, evidence-informed, safety-aware, and coordinated without abandoning professional guidance.

Why C: Incomplete. Ambivalence is relevant assessment information, not a reason to avoid treatment planning.

Why D: Autonomy violation. Family input may be useful with consent, but the partner does not choose the adult client's pathway.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When several legitimate recovery options exist, choose the response that combines client goals, evidence, safety, and supports.

Transfer rule: Recovery planning is person-driven and revisable, not therapist-owned. Remediation: R3

4. Correct Answer: D

Why A: Assumptive. Identity and relationship status do not determine anatomy, behavior, partners, or exposure.

Why B: Not client-centered. Language should be accurate and adapted to the client's stated terms where clinically appropriate.

Why C: Unsafe and incomplete. Consent and coercion are routine assessment dimensions, not topics reserved for disclosed violence.

Why D: Correct. OPENS uses permission, inclusive language, behaviorally specific inquiry, safety assessment, and contextual reasoning.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Inclusive sexual-health items reward separation of dimensions and direct consent and safety assessment.

Transfer rule: Ask rather than infer identity, anatomy, behavior, desire, function, consent, and context. Remediation: R4

5. Correct Answer: A

Why A: Correct. The client's voice, equitable access, privacy, and chosen support role must be established before routine treatment proceeds.

Why B: Ableist presumption. Family knowledge may be useful, but it does not replace direct client participation and consent.

Why C: Access neglect. Environmental barriers and relational assumptions are part of the treatment context.

Why D: Overcorrective. Support persons may be appropriate when the client chooses and roles and privacy are clear.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When disability and access appear, look first for direct client voice and removal of participation barriers.

Transfer rule: Adapt the environment and roles before labeling the client nonparticipatory. Remediation: R5

6. Correct Answer: C

Why A: Culturally presumptive. Duration alone and the presence of ritual do not establish disorder.

Why B: Risk bypass. Cultural humility requires respectful inquiry, not avoidance of clinically necessary assessment.

Why C: Correct. The option honors meaning while evaluating function, safety, and plausible overlapping conditions.

Why D: Unsafe. Suicidal thoughts require direct assessment regardless of grief context.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
The best grief answer respects culture and still assesses function, differential evidence, and suicide risk.

Transfer rule: Cultural humility and diagnostic and safety assessment are complementary. Remediation: R7

7. Correct Answer: B

Why A: Overdisclosure. The entire record is rarely the minimum information necessary for a focused care question.

Why B: Correct. BRIDGE requires informed permission, a specific purpose, information limits, role clarity, and documentation.

Why C: Incorrect. MFTs can coordinate appropriately; refusing all coordination may fragment care.

Why D: Outside role and unsafe. Medication changes belong with the qualified prescriber or appropriate medical professional.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Coordination items are usually solved by client identity, permission, purpose, minimum necessary information, and role ownership.

Transfer rule: Define the care question and information boundary before sharing. Remediation: R8

8. Correct Answer: D

Why A: Unsafe escalation. Worsening dissociation and instability indicate the need to reassess readiness and level of care.

Why B: Fragmenting. Another provider without a coherent role, consent, and ownership can increase confusion.

Why C: Feedback neglect. Worsening participation and safety-related indicators require active revision now.

Why D: Correct. STEADY prioritizes safety, participation capacity, role clarity, coordinated level of care, and documented follow-up.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When trauma work destabilizes the system, choose stabilization and coordinated reassessment before intensity.

Transfer rule: Processing proceeds only when safety, regulation, capacity, and continuity support it. Remediation: R9/R10

9. Correct Answer: A

Why A: Correct. Sudden change, caregiver strain, capacity, and possible mistreatment require direct safety and medical or protective assessment using current jurisdictional requirements.

Why B: Ageist and unsupported. Acute change does not establish dementia or automatic transfer of authority.

Why C: Incorrect. Caregiver stress can coexist with neglect or exploitation and does not rule it out.

Why D: Poor sequencing. A routine intervention should not precede assessment of acute function and protection concerns.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Acute late-life change plus caregiver strain cues medical, capacity, safety, and protection assessment.

Transfer rule: Do not convert age or caregiver role into diagnosis or authority. Remediation: R6/R9

10. Correct Answer: B

Why A: Intensity bias. More services are not automatically better and may worsen fragmentation or burden.

Why B: Correct. The plan should return to the decision point, client voice, distributed response, role clarity, and continuity-based revision.

Why C: Feedback neglect. Worsening outcomes and alliance require reassessment rather than equal-duration waiting.

Why D: Blaming and unsupported. Nonresponse may reflect formulation, fit, access, coordination, safety, or level-of-care problems.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When many services and worsening feedback appear together, choose coherent reassessment rather than addition.

Transfer rule: Complex care is coordinated, measured, and revisable. Remediation: R10
