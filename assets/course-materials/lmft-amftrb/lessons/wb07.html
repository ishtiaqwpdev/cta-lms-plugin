How to Use This Workbook

Workbook 7 teaches relational intervention as a decision sequence: identify the client and treatment unit, formulate the observable pattern maintaining the problem, verify safety and voluntary participation, select an intervention that fits the hypothesis and mechanism of change, observe the system’s response, and revise when the intervention is ineffective, poorly timed, or unsafe.

LEARN → ORGANIZE → APPLY → ANALYZE → REPAIR Study the six chapters in order. Use the reasoning tools to organize each case, complete the application worksheets, answer all ten formative questions before reading the rationales, and remediate every miss or low-confidence response. Mnemonics organize judgment; they do not replace individualized assessment, competence, current law, ethical standards, consultation, or supervision.
SAFETY AND VOLUNTARINESS OVERRIDE When coercive control, fear, violence, abuse, retaliation risk, acute impairment, or inability to participate freely is present, pause routine conjoint or evocative relational techniques. Direct safety assessment, role clarification, and an appropriate level-of-care or referral response take priority.

Workbook Learning Objectives

  • Identify the client and treatment unit, construct a systemic hypothesis, and select an intervention target that fits the presenting relational sequence.
  • Establish alliance across members while maintaining balanced accountability, cultural humility, role clarity, and attention to power.
  • Determine when conjoint, individual, subgroup, collateral, hybrid, or referral work is clinically indicated and safe.
  • Use attachment-informed interventions to identify cycles, access underlying emotion and needs, and support responsive engagement without forcing vulnerability.
  • Apply structural methods to clarify boundaries, hierarchy, coalitions, subsystems, and enactments without imposing one family norm.
  • Apply strategic methods through pattern-focused tasks, reframing, directives, and interruption of attempted solutions while monitoring consent and unintended effects.
  • Use intergenerational concepts, genograms, differentiation, triangles, legacies, and relational transmission without treating history as destiny.
  • Integrate model-consistent methods, evaluate system response, repair alliance strain, revise the plan, and recognize safety, competence, ethical, or referral limits.

High-Yield Relational-Intervention Distinctions

DistinctionDecision rule
Identified client vs. treatment unitThe person named in the referral may not be the only person or relationship that must change for the treatment goal to move.
Circular hypothesis vs. blameDescribe an observable reciprocal sequence and its maintaining feedback; do not assign a single person as the cause.
Multipartiality vs. false equivalenceBuild alliance with each participant while naming power, responsibility, coercion, or harm accurately.
Emotion access vs. forced disclosureDeepen only when sufficient safety, regulation, consent, and alliance are present.
Enactment vs. reenactment of dangerUse in-session interaction to observe and reshape a pattern only when the structure is safe enough and therapeutically bounded.

Family history vs. destinyUse multigenerational patterns as hypotheses that expand choice; do not present them as deterministic explanations.

CHAPTER 1

Treatment Unit, Systemic Hypothesis, and Intervention Selection
BIG IDEA Choose the treatment target before choosing a technique. The best intervention follows from who the client is, which relational sequence maintains the problem, what change is observable, and whether the proposed format is safe and feasible.

Think Systemically at the Decision Point

Systemic therapy does not deny individual symptoms, biology, trauma, development, or environmental stress. It asks how those factors interact with relationship patterns, meanings, resources, and feedback. A useful hypothesis is tentative, behaviorally anchored, culturally responsive, and open to revision. It should guide the next intervention without pretending to prove causation.

The identified client is the person named as having the problem. The treatment unit is the individual, couple, family, caregiver system, subgroup, or coordinated network whose participation is clinically relevant to the agreed change target. These may be the same, but they often are not.

The FIT Intervention-Selection Sequence

StepOperational question
F — FormulateWhat observable interactional sequence, meaning, emotion, attempted solution, or contextual pressure appears to maintain the problem?
I — IdentifyWho is the client? What is the treatment unit? What specific relational or individual change would show movement?
T — Test fitIs the proposed modality and intervention safe, voluntary, culturally responsive, accessible, within competence, and linked to the hypothesized mechanism?
EXAM LENS If an item presents a symptom and several plausible techniques, look first for the option that clarifies the treatment unit and links the intervention to the maintaining sequence. Technique-before-formulation is a common distractor.

Build a Testable Systemic Hypothesis

ComponentExample of useful formulation language
Observation“When uncertainty rises, Sam asks repeated questions and Lee becomes quieter.”
Meaning / emotion“Sam reads quiet as abandonment; Lee reads repeated questions as criticism and failure.”
Response“Sam intensifies pursuit; Lee withdraws further.”
Feedback“Each response confirms the other person’s fear and strengthens the cycle.”
Change target“Both partners will notice the cycle earlier and replace pursuit/withdrawal with a brief signal, regulated pause, and responsive return.”
Revision test“If the intervention does not reduce escalation or increase responsive return, reassess the hypothesis, readiness, barriers, and safety.”

Match Modality to the Target

Possible formatWhen it may fitQuestions before choosing it
Conjoint couple/familyThe maintaining pattern is observable in the room and participants can engage voluntarily and safely.Are privacy, safety, power, participation, and confidentiality policies clear?
Individual within a relational frameA participant needs focused work, another member will not attend, or conjoint work is currently unsafe or contraindicated.Can the work remain systemic without covertly conducting therapy on absent people?
Subgroup / caregiver sessionA subsystem must strengthen coordination, boundaries, or leadership before broader family work.Will the format create or reinforce a coalition? How will purpose and information boundaries be explained?
Collateral / coordinated careExternal information or collaboration is needed for the treatment goal.What authorization, minimum-necessary disclosure, role boundary, and documentation apply?
Referral / consultationThe needed model, risk response, cultural expertise, language access, or level of care exceeds current capacity.How will continuity and nonabandonment be protected?

Intervention Selection Rules

  • Name the current decision point: assessment, alliance, stabilization, restructuring, emotional engagement, task design, response evaluation, or consolidation.
  • Select the intervention because it targets the hypothesized maintaining mechanism—not because it is familiar, dramatic, or identified with a favored model.
  • Specify what the therapist will observe after the intervention. A mechanism without an observable response indicator cannot guide revision.
  • Treat client preference, culture, language, disability access, developmental stage, family structure, spirituality, resources, and practical barriers as components of fit.
  • Reassess when the treatment unit changes, new safety information emerges, or an intervention produces withdrawal, escalation, shame, or no movement.

Case Lab 1: The Referral Is Not the Treatment Unit

Rae, age 31, is referred for anxiety after repeated urgent-care visits. Rae asks for “tools to stop worrying.” During intake, Rae and spouse Devon describe a sequence in which bodily sensations lead Rae to seek repeated reassurance, Devon responds with increasingly detailed checking and then frustration, Rae interprets the frustration as abandonment, and both lose sleep. Neither reports fear, coercion, violence, or inability to participate voluntarily.

Decision point: What should the therapist clarify and target before selecting an anxiety-management or couple intervention?

MODEL REASONING Use FIT. Confirm Rae’s individual needs and medical coordination while clarifying whether the agreed client and treatment unit include the couple’s reassurance-escalation loop. Formulate the sequence without blaming either partner, define observable goals, assess preferences and feasibility, and then choose a modality and intervention that fit the mechanism.

CHAPTER 2

Alliance, Voluntariness, Power, and Conjoint Safety
BIG IDEA A multi-person alliance is not neutrality about harm. Join each participant, protect voluntary participation, clarify the treatment frame, observe power, and name the relational process without equating unequal responsibility.

The JOIN Alliance-and-Safety Sequence

StepClinical task
J — Join each participantCommunicate respect, understand each person’s goals and meanings, and avoid forming a covert coalition.
O — Observe power and participationNotice who speaks, interrupts, monitors, freezes, defers, translates, controls access, or appears unable to disagree safely.
I — Identify voluntariness and safetyAsk privately when needed about fear, coercion, violence, retaliation, privacy, capacity, and the practical freedom to participate or decline.
N — Name the shared processDescribe the observable cycle and accountability accurately without implying that all behavior has equal impact or responsibility.

Clarify the Conjoint Treatment Frame

ControlWhat the therapist clarifies
Client and goalsWho is receiving treatment, which goals are shared or individual, and how conflicting goals will be handled.
ParticipationWho will attend, whether participation is voluntary, what separate contacts may occur, and when the format may change.
Confidentiality / privacyApplicable legal and ethical limits, the practice’s policy for information shared outside conjoint sessions, records, portals, collateral contacts, and emergencies.
Decision authorityThe therapist facilitates treatment; clients retain autonomy over relationship decisions. Therapy is not mediation, custody evaluation, legal advice, or investigation.
Technology and settingLocation, privacy, identity, connection-failure planning, and whether participants in different places can engage safely.
Review pointsWhen consent, goals, safety, the treatment unit, and the information policy will be revisited.
SECRETS POLICY There is no universal “no-secrets” rule. The therapist must explain the chosen practice policy and its limits before relevant disclosures arise, apply current law and ethics, avoid promising outcomes that cannot be kept, and assess safety before deciding how information affects conjoint treatment.

Multipartiality, Accountability, and Culture

Multipartiality means working to understand and engage each participant while remaining accountable to the welfare of the client system and the treatment contract. It does not require equal validation of all conduct. The therapist can validate fear, grief, longing, or protective intent while still naming intimidation, surveillance, degradation, violence, exploitation, or boundary violations accurately.

  • Ask how family structure, gender, disability, migration, race, class, faith, sexuality, relationship agreements, caregiving roles, and community context shape meaning—without assuming they determine it.
  • Track therapist reactivity: rescuing one member, overidentifying with a role, avoiding conflict, imposing a preferred relationship outcome, or mistaking familiarity for cultural knowledge.
  • Use interpreters and access supports appropriately; do not automatically recruit a family member into a translation or accessibility role that changes power or privacy.
  • Repair alliance strain directly: name the observed impact, invite correction, clarify intent without defensiveness, and change the treatment process when warranted.

When Conjoint Work May Be Contraindicated or Delayed

SignalImmediate reasoning task
Fear or retaliation riskAssess privately and behaviorally; determine whether disclosure or conjoint discussion could increase danger.
Coercive control / surveillanceDo not frame the pattern as symmetrical communication difficulty. Address safety, privacy, access, and specialized resources.
Acute impairment or instabilityAssess capacity, safety, medical/psychiatric contributors, and level of care before evocative relational work.
Incompatible rolesClarify therapy versus evaluation, mediation, legal, disciplinary, or forensic functions; refer when roles conflict.
No meaningful consentDo not use pressure, surprise disclosure, or alliance leverage to manufacture participation.

Case Lab 2: Coercion Hidden Inside “Communication Problems”

During an individual check-in that was described in the couple’s informed-consent process, Nia reports that partner Alex checks Nia’s phone, controls transportation money, and waits outside the therapy door. Nia says, “Please do not bring this up together; I will pay for it later.” In conjoint sessions, Alex speaks for Nia and describes the problem as Nia’s unwillingness to communicate.

Decision point: What should control the next clinical step?

MODEL REASONING Pause symmetrical couple framing. Use JOIN to assess privacy, fear, coercive control, retaliation risk, access to support, dependents, and the safety of future contact. Apply the stated information policy and current jurisdictional requirements carefully. Determine whether conjoint work is contraindicated and coordinate specialized safety resources without using confrontation that could increase risk.

CHAPTER 3

Attachment-Informed Couple and Family Interventions
BIG IDEA Attachment-informed work changes the cycle by helping participants recognize protective responses, access underlying emotion and relational meaning, and create tolerable moments of accessibility, responsiveness, and engagement.

Use Attachment as a Process Lens

An attachment lens asks what happens when connection, safety, importance, or availability feels threatened. Anger, criticism, pursuit, shutdown, intellectualization, caretaking, or distance may function as protective responses. The therapist does not excuse harmful behavior, diagnose an “attachment style” from a single interaction, or assume vulnerability is safe simply because it is emotionally meaningful.

The SECURE Intervention Sequence

StepClinical task
S — Spot the negative cycleTrack cue, perception, emotion, protective move, partner impact, and reinforcing feedback.
E — Explore primary emotion and meaningMove beneath reactive emotion at a tolerable pace; identify attachment fears, needs, longings, and protective logic.
C — Create safe engagementEstablish enough regulation, consent, and alliance for a new interaction.
U — Use responsive interactionsShape a specific, present-focused expression and response rather than a debate about historical facts.
R — Review impactAsk what each participant experienced; monitor shame, flooding, withdrawal, escalation, and responsiveness.
E — Extend repairConsolidate the new interaction, plan how to recognize the cycle, and practice a reachable next step.

Map the Cycle, Not the Villain

Cycle elementQuestions that deepen without blaming
Trigger / cueWhat happened just before the shift? What did each person notice?
MeaningWhat did the cue seem to say about connection, importance, safety, failure, or rejection?
Secondary emotionWhat reactive emotion was most visible—anger, irritation, contempt, numbness, urgency?
Primary emotion / needWhat more vulnerable feeling or attachment concern may be underneath, if the client experiences that fit?
Protective actionWhat did each person do to manage the threat?
Partner impactHow did the action land, and what did it invite next?
Cycle labelCan the participants name the shared pattern in language that does not shame either person?

Stage a Tolerable Enactment

  • Prepare: verify regulation, voluntariness, safety, and the speaker’s own words. Do not surprise a participant with a high-intensity disclosure.
  • Specify: ask for a brief present-focused message about the softer feeling, need, boundary, or request.
  • Support the receiver: slow down interpretation, help the receiver summarize what was heard, and shape a responsive statement.
  • Process impact: ask both people what happened internally and relationally. A scripted response without felt contact may not be corrective.
  • Consolidate: identify what interrupted the cycle and define a small repeatable action outside the session.
PACING SAFEGUARD Do not intensify emotional access when a participant is flooded, dissociated, coerced, retaliatory, acutely impaired, or unable to decline. Regulation, safety, and choice are part of the intervention—not obstacles to it.

Case Lab 3: A Pursue–Withdraw Cycle Under the Content

Mika and Tomás argue about unfinished household tasks. Mika raises a list of examples and speaks faster; Tomás becomes quiet and looks away. Mika concludes that Tomás does not care and escalates. Tomás experiences Mika’s urgency as proof that he will fail and withdraws further. Both want more connection and report no violence, coercion, or fear.

Decision point: What attachment-informed step is most useful before problem-solving the task list?

MODEL REASONING Use SECURE to externalize the cycle, slow the sequence, and help each partner access the meaning beneath the protective move. After confirming regulation and willingness, shape a small interaction in which Mika expresses the fear underneath urgency and Tomás communicates the fear underneath withdrawal, then review whether the exchange increases responsiveness.

CHAPTER 4

Structural and Strategic Interventions
BIG IDEA Structural work changes organization and interaction in the room; strategic work interrupts problem-maintaining sequences and attempted solutions. Both require a clear hypothesis, consent, monitoring, and cultural humility.

The SHIFT Structural–Strategic Sequence

StepClinical task
S — See the patternObserve who approaches, withdraws, aligns, interrupts, rescues, decides, or repeats an attempted solution.
H — Highlight the mechanismName the boundary, hierarchy, coalition, sequence, or attempted solution linked to the target.
I — InterveneUse an enactment, boundary shift, reframe, task, or pattern interruption that fits the hypothesis.
F — Follow the responseObserve participation, affect, power, safety, feasibility, and whether the pattern changes.
T — Tune the next stepStrengthen, simplify, revise, stop, consult, or change modality according to the response.

Structural Concepts and Interventions

ConceptClinical meaningPossible intervention
SubsystemA functional grouping such as partners, coparents, siblings, or caregivers.Meet with or strengthen a relevant subgroup while preventing covert coalition.
BoundaryThe rules governing contact, information, participation, protection, and autonomy.Clarify, strengthen, or soften interactional boundaries according to function and context.
Hierarchy / leadershipHow responsibility and decision authority are organized.Support developmentally and culturally appropriate caregiver coordination without demanding rigid dominance.
Coalition / triangulationA recurring alignment that regulates conflict or excludes another member.Make the pattern observable and redirect direct communication or age-appropriate responsibility.
EnactmentAn in-session interaction that reveals and can reshape the pattern.Invite a brief live exchange, coach it, monitor impact, and process what changed.
Unbalancing / therapist positioningA deliberate temporary shift in support to change a rigid pattern.Use only with clear rationale, awareness of power, and active monitoring for rupture or harm.
CULTURAL HUMILITY “Clear boundaries” and “effective hierarchy” are functional concepts, not a license to impose one culture’s family structure, gender role, independence norm, or communication style. Ask how the family understands responsibility, respect, protection, connection, and authority.

Strategic Concepts and Interventions

ConceptDecision question
Attempted solutionWhat does the system repeatedly do to solve the problem that unintentionally strengthens it?
ReframeCan the therapist shift meaning in a way that reduces blame and opens a different response without minimizing harm?
Directive / taskIs the task specific, consensual, feasible, linked to the mechanism, and paired with a plan to review effects?
Pattern interruptionWhat small change in timing, sequence, setting, or response would test the hypothesis?
Paradoxical methodIs the therapist specifically trained, is informed consent adequate, and are power, safety, misinterpretation, and unintended effects controlled?
Outcome reviewWhat happened after the task, for whom, in what context, and what should be continued, changed, or stopped?

Structural and Strategic: Do Not Collapse the Models

LensPrimary focusIn-session clueTypical rationale
StructuralOrganization, subsystems, boundaries, hierarchy, coalitionsThe pattern is visible in how members interact and organize participation.Change the relational organization so members can function differently.
StrategicSequences, attempted solutions, rules, and feedbackThe family repeats a solution that keeps the problem active.Interrupt the maintaining sequence and learn from the response.

Case Lab 4: Parent–Adolescent Coalition and Caregiver Coordination

Jordan, age 15, argues with caregiver Mei about school attendance. During session, Jordan turns to caregiver Alana, who answers for Jordan and criticizes Mei’s strictness. Mei then addresses the therapist instead of either family member. The caregivers share concern about school but disagree about authority and cultural expectations regarding respect.

Decision point: How can a structural intervention make the pattern observable without blaming Jordan or imposing a preferred family hierarchy?

MODEL REASONING Use SHIFT to identify the cross-generational alignment and indirect caregiver communication as hypotheses. Invite a brief caregiver-to-caregiver enactment about one school decision while Jordan observes, coach direct communication and shared responsibility, and ask the family how authority and respect should function in their cultural and developmental context. Monitor Jordan’s participation and avoid making the adolescent the cause of caregiver conflict.

Case Lab 5: The Solution That Maintains the Problem

Whenever Chris worries that partner Aiden is upset, Chris asks for reassurance repeatedly. Aiden answers at first, then becomes irritated and leaves the room. Chris follows and asks more urgently. Both say they want fewer late-night conflicts. The therapist has already assessed safety and the partners can decline tasks freely.

Decision point: What strategic intervention could test the attempted-solution hypothesis?

MODEL REASONING Use SHIFT to frame repeated reassurance and withdrawal as a mutually reinforcing attempted solution. Collaboratively design a low-risk, specific interruption—such as one clear reassurance request, one responsive answer, and a mutually agreed return time if either needs a pause. Define what each person will track, review unintended effects, and revise rather than treating task completion as success by itself.

CHAPTER 5

Intergenerational and Caregiver/Parenting Work
BIG IDEA Intergenerational information expands the field of view. Use it to identify triangles, legacies, cutoffs, relational transmission, strengths, and present choices—not to declare that the past determines the future.

The ROOTS Intergenerational Sequence

StepClinical task
R — Review generations and contextMap relationships, transitions, identities, roles, stressors, resources, losses, migration, health, and social context.
O — Observe triangles and legaciesNotice recurring ways tension moves between people and the rules, stories, loyalties, or burdens that organize response.
O — Outline transmission and cutoffsDevelop tentative hypotheses about repeated emotional processes, avoidance, projection, and patterns across generations.
T — Track present activationIdentify how the historical pattern operates in current relationships and situations.
S — Support differentiation and choiceHelp clients stay connected while taking a thoughtful position, tolerating emotion, and choosing a present response.

Use the Genogram as a Clinical Conversation

MapExamplesSafeguard
StructureHouseholds, partnerships, separations, births, deaths, caregiving, chosen family, important nonfamily supportsDo not assume legal or biological ties define emotional significance.
Relationship processCloseness, conflict, cutoff, caregiving, triangles, alliances, reconciliation, estrangementUse client language; avoid turning symbols into diagnoses.
Transitions and stressorsMigration, illness, disability, deployment, discrimination, loss, economic changes, family-life-cycle shiftsPlace patterns in social and historical context.
Legacies and beliefsMessages about emotion, loyalty, authority, privacy, gender, work, safety, success, help-seekingExplore variation and resistance within generations; avoid stereotypes.
Strengths and repairsSurvival, mutual aid, advocacy, flexibility, rituals, protective adults, prior changesMap resilience with the same attention given to problems.

Core Intergenerational Concepts

ConceptWorking definitionIntervention direction
TriangleTension between two people is stabilized by involving a third person, issue, or role.Help participants observe the triangle and communicate or self-regulate more directly.
DifferentiationCapacity to remain emotionally connected while maintaining reflective, values-consistent thought and action.Support “I-position” clarity without equating differentiation with distance or individualism.
Projection processAnxiety and expectations become organized around a child or other member.Reduce blame, broaden the formulation, and return age-appropriate responsibility to the relevant subsystem.
Emotional cutoffDistance or avoidance manages unresolved relational intensity.Explore function, safety, loss, and choice; do not prescribe reunion.
Multigenerational transmissionRelational processes, meanings, resources, and vulnerabilities recur or transform across generations.Treat recurrence as a hypothesis and identify current exceptions and choices.
BOUNDARY Differentiation does not require reconciliation, disclosure, contact, or forgiveness. In cases involving abuse, coercion, discrimination, or ongoing harm, distance may be protective. Explore choice and safety rather than prescribing family connection.

Caregiver and Parenting Alliances

  • Clarify the caregiver subsystem and each person’s actual authority, responsibility, access, and constraints rather than assuming a two-parent nuclear structure.
  • Assess child development, neurodivergence, disability, trauma, school and community systems, and practical demands before interpreting behavior relationally.
  • Strengthen coordination around a small observable task; do not recruit a child to resolve adult conflict or treat the child as the family symptom without individual assessment.
  • Ask what caregiving, respect, autonomy, protection, and discipline mean in the family’s context; distinguish cultural difference from harm without minimizing either.
  • When caregivers cannot safely collaborate, adjust the treatment unit, coordinate within role, and use consultation or referral as needed.

Case Lab 6: A Multigenerational Triangle Without a Predetermined Story

Leila reports becoming intensely anxious whenever her adult son Mateo disagrees with Leila’s mother, Farah. Leila immediately mediates, then feels resentful that both “make me responsible.” A genogram shows that Leila was asked to carry messages between her parents during conflict, but it also shows several relatives who used direct, respectful problem-solving.

Decision point: How should the therapist use the intergenerational information?

MODEL REASONING Use ROOTS to explore the triangle and Leila’s inherited responsibility story as tentative hypotheses, while also identifying exceptions and strengths. Track the present sequence, help Leila define an “I-position” and tolerate others’ disagreement without automatically mediating, and do not present childhood history as proof that the current response is inevitable.

CHAPTER 6

Integration, Response Evaluation, and Plan Revision
BIG IDEA Integration is disciplined adaptation. Maintain a coherent link among hypothesis, mechanism, goal, intervention, and observed response; repair alliance strain and change course when the evidence does not support continuing.

The ADAPT Revision Sequence

StepClinical task
A — Assess response and allianceWhat changed, for whom, in which context? Who feels understood, blamed, excluded, pressured, or unsafe?
D — Detect nonresponse, harm, or driftIs the pattern unchanged, worse, displaced, hidden, or replaced by treatment compliance without meaningful benefit?
A — Align with the hypothesisDoes the intervention still fit the formulation, mechanism, goals, culture, readiness, and treatment unit?
P — Pivot, consult, refer, or reviseAdjust pace, model, modality, level of care, access, goals, or provider support according to findings.
T — Track and documentRecord data, client feedback, clinical reasoning, informed-consent updates, actions, and the next reassessment point.

Model Integration Without Drift

Integration questionCoherent answer example
What is the hypothesis?Pursuit and withdrawal mutually amplify perceived attachment threat.
What mechanism is targeted?De-escalation plus a responsive interaction changes the feedback loop.
Why this intervention now?The partners can identify the cycle and remain regulated enough for a brief enactment.
What will be observed?Earlier cycle recognition, less escalation, and a successful return after a regulated pause.
What would prompt revision?Increasing fear, shutdown, humiliation, no improvement, alliance split, or evidence that the treatment unit is wrong.

A therapist may integrate methods when competent to do so, but should be able to state the function of each method. “I use whatever works” is not a formulation. A structural enactment, attachment intervention, strategic task, or intergenerational exploration can coexist when their sequencing and purposes are explicit and their effects are monitored.

Evaluate More Than Symptom Count

Information sourceQuestions
Client-defined outcomesWhat matters to each participant? Are goals shared, compatible, or in conflict?
Interactional observationIs the maintaining sequence changing in session or only being described differently?
Alliance / participationWho feels engaged, blamed, silenced, pressured, or peripheral?
Function and contextDoes change generalize across home, work, school, caregiving, community, or teletherapy settings?
Unintended effectsHas improvement for one member increased burden, danger, exclusion, or symptom substitution elsewhere?
Standardized or structured feedbackWhat does the measure add, and where does it conflict with narrative or observed data?

When Treatment Is Not Moving

  • Reassess safety, coercion, substance/medical factors, capacity, and level of care before interpreting nonresponse as motivational.
  • Revisit the client, treatment unit, and goals. A member may be improving on a goal that another person never endorsed.
  • Check alliance across participants and repair therapist contributions, including cultural misattunement, coalition, pressure, or unclear information boundaries.
  • Examine whether the intervention targets the formulated mechanism, whether the dose and pacing are feasible, and whether access barriers prevent implementation.
  • Consult or refer when model-specific skill, language access, risk expertise, neurodevelopmental competence, medical coordination, or another resource is needed.
  • If ending or transferring care, plan continuity, consolidate gains, address relapse indicators, and avoid abrupt termination or role ambiguity.

Case Lab 7: Technique Accumulation and Model Drift

After four sessions with a couple, the therapist has assigned a communication worksheet, explored family-of-origin stories, taught grounding, challenged cognitions, and asked for a structural enactment. The therapist cannot explain which maintaining mechanism any intervention targets. The partners complete homework but report that the same escalation continues.

Decision point: What should the therapist do before adding another technique?

MODEL REASONING Use ADAPT to restate the treatment unit, goals, observed sequence, and working hypothesis; review alliance and client feedback; identify which intervention mechanism fits the current decision point; and discontinue or revise methods that lack rationale or benefit. Consultation may be indicated if model integration exceeds competence.

Case Lab 8: Nonresponse, Alliance Split, and Hybrid Participation

A caregiver joins family sessions by video from work while two family members attend in person. After six sessions, the caregiver reports better communication, but a young adult family member has become quiet and says the therapist “always sides with the parents.” Privacy at the caregiver’s worksite is inconsistent, and the young adult does not want sensitive topics discussed while the caregiver is there.

Decision point: What should the therapist reassess before continuing the plan unchanged?

MODEL REASONING Use ADAPT to assess each participant’s goals, alliance, privacy, voluntariness, and experience of benefit; clarify the client and treatment unit; review technology and information boundaries; repair the perceived coalition; and determine whether a different format, subgroup work, access support, consultation, or referral is needed. Document both improvement and the uneven or harmful effects.

Answer Key and Detailed Rationales

Review the clinical decision, not only the letter. For every miss or low-confidence correct answer, identify the remediation code and write a transferable rule.

1. Correct Answer: C

Why A: Premature and too narrow. Individual coping may help, but excluding relational and medical assessment bypasses the treatment-unit decision.

Why B: Incorrect. The stem does not establish intentional behavior, and blame is not a systemic hypothesis.

Why C: Correct. FIT begins by clarifying the client, relevant medical needs, treatment unit, observable sequence, and shared goals before selecting technique.

Why D: Premature and deterministic. A genogram may later add context, but family history does not automatically explain the symptom.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY Separate the referral label from the treatment-unit decision.

Transfer rule: Clarify who is the client, what system must change, and what assessment remains necessary before technique. Remediation: R2/R3

2. Correct Answer: A

Why A: Correct. Surveillance, speaking for a partner, and scanning the exit require private safety and voluntariness assessment before conjoint skills work.

Why B: Insufficient. Equal speaking time can conceal coercion and may increase risk if the quieter partner cannot disagree safely.

Why C: Unsafe and premature. Attachment meaning must not replace assessment of control, fear, or retaliation.

Why D: Outside the immediate therapeutic role. The therapist supports safety and autonomy rather than directing a relationship decision from limited information.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY Behavioral signs of control move the item from communication technique to conjoint-safety assessment.

Transfer rule: Safety and voluntariness control the treatment frame. Remediation: R4/R5

3. Correct Answer: D

Why A: Incorrect. The therapist should not promise an outcome that conflicts with the disclosed policy, law, ethics, or safety needs.

Why B: Potentially unsafe and inconsistent with thoughtful application of the treatment frame.

Why C: Incorrect. Deleting clinically relevant information does not resolve the policy, safety, or treatment implications.

Why D: Correct. The therapist applies the previously explained policy, assesses safety, and determines whether and how treatment can continue.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY When outside-session information appears, return to informed consent, policy, safety, and the treatment frame.

Transfer rule: Clarify information rules before the crisis; reassess them when circumstances change. Remediation: R4/R5/R10

4. Correct Answer: B

Why A: Unsafe. Flooding indicates that pacing and regulation require attention; forced intensity can deepen withdrawal or harm.

Why B: Correct. SECURE includes regulation, choice, and tolerable engagement before deeper enactment.

Why C: Model-incongruent. Debating blame strengthens content conflict rather than changing the attachment cycle.

Why D: Unsafe and poorly timed. Surprise disclosure removes consent and therapist support.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY Attachment work follows capacity; the most emotionally intense option is not necessarily the best one.

Transfer rule: Regulation and voluntariness are part of emotional engagement. Remediation: R3/R5/R9

5. Correct Answer: C

Why A: Unsupported and blaming. The pattern can be observed without assigning a covert motive to the teenager.

Why B: Not best. Reading may be educational but does not directly assess or reshape the observed structural pattern.

Why C: Correct. The enactment makes caregiver coordination and boundaries visible and allows in-session restructuring.

Why D: Culturally presumptive. Structural concepts should be applied functionally, not as a universal authority rule.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY Structural answers often change organization through a live, bounded interaction.

Transfer rule: Observe and reshape the relevant subsystem without imposing one family norm. Remediation: R3/R7

6. Correct Answer: A

Why A: Correct. The task interrupts the maintaining attempted solution, is collaborative and specific, and includes outcome review.

Why B: Incorrect. Forced equal fault creates false equivalence and does not test the maintaining sequence.

Why C: Not best. Historical exploration may become relevant, but it does not address the current repetitive attempted solution.

Why D: Unsafe and outside demonstrated competence. Paradoxical work requires specific training, safeguards, informed consent, and monitoring.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY A strategic task is keyed by its link to the attempted solution and its review plan.

Transfer rule: Interrupt the sequence collaboratively, then learn from what happens. Remediation: R4/R6/R10

7. Correct Answer: D

Why A: Deterministic. A recurring pattern is a hypothesis, not proof of a universal cause.

Why B: Unsafe and autonomy-limiting. Contact or reconciliation is not automatically indicated.

Why C: Incorrect. Intergenerational information can be clinically useful when contextualized and tested.

Why D: Correct. ROOTS uses history to understand function, present activation, exceptions, and choice while preserving agency.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY Avoid answers that turn a genogram pattern into destiny or prescribe reunion.

Transfer rule: Use history to expand present choice, not to dictate it. Remediation: R8

8. Correct Answer: B

Why A: Unsafe. Continuing after a participant says disagreement is not possible may reenact coercion or danger.

Why B: Correct. The therapist pauses the technique and reassesses safety, voluntariness, and treatment-frame impact.

Why C: Incorrect. Praising others bypasses the frozen member’s safety and alliance signal.

Why D: Incorrect. Labeling resistance substitutes judgment for assessment.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY A technique stops being therapeutic when the structure removes meaningful choice or increases danger.

Transfer rule: Pause, assess, and revise before continuing evocative conjoint work. Remediation: R5/R9

9. Correct Answer: A

Why A: Correct. ADAPT reconnects response data, alliance, hypothesis, mechanism, and intervention before further treatment.

Why B: Incorrect. More variety deepens technique accumulation without improving coherence.

Why C: Blaming and unsupported. Homework completion does not establish that nonresponse reflects motivation.

Why D: Incorrect. Continuing despite unchanged outcomes and unclear rationale neglects feedback.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY When several models appear and outcomes are flat, look for formulation and response review—not another technique.

Transfer rule: Integration requires an explicit hypothesis, mechanism, and revision rule. Remediation: R6/R9

10. Correct Answer: C

Why A: Incomplete. Improvement for one member does not erase disengagement or alliance harm for another.

Why B: Premature and potentially abandoning. The therapist first assesses the rupture, goals, safety, and appropriate format.

Why C: Correct. The response evaluates distributed outcomes, repairs alliance, and revisits the treatment unit and plan.

Why D: Incorrect. Multipartiality includes discussing alliance impact; avoidance does not create neutrality.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY In family treatment, evaluate who benefits, who bears cost, and who remains meaningfully engaged.

Transfer rule: Repair alliance and revise the unit or format when progress is uneven or harmful. Remediation: R4/R9
