How to Use This Workbook

Workbook 10 teaches the decision point after treatment has begun: Is care helping, harming, stalling, mismatched, or ready to end? Start with collaboratively defined goals and baselines, gather proportionate information from the relevant client system, interpret change with humility, and choose the next action from safety, response, alliance, context, evidence, competence, and continuity—not from therapist preference alone.

LEARN -> ORGANIZE -> APPLY -> ANALYZE -> REPAIR Study the six chapters in order. Use TRACK, LENS, SIGNAL, REPAIR, STEPS, and CLOSE to organize decisions. Complete all eight case labs and the reusable record. Answer all ten formative questions before reading the rationales, then remediate every miss or low-confidence response. The tools organize judgment; they do not replace direct assessment, current law and ethics, measure-specific training, consultation, supervision, or individualized care.
SAFETY AND CONTINUITY OVERRIDE
New danger, deterioration, acute medical or psychiatric instability, abuse, coercion, neglect, or inability to participate requires direct reassessment, stabilization, and an appropriate level-of-care response before routine outcome interpretation or termination. Administrative, financial, therapist, or setting changes do not erase reasonable continuity responsibilities.

Workbook Learning Objectives

  • Define client-system goals, baseline indicators, expected direction of change, review intervals, and decision thresholds collaboratively and in culturally responsive terms.
  • Select feasible outcome, alliance, functioning, safety, and qualitative feedback methods that fit the treatment unit, purpose, setting, language, burden, and access needs.
  • Interpret repeated information using trend, meaningful change, uncertainty, missingness, measurement limits, context, unintended effects, and client-system meaning.
  • Appraise quantitative, qualitative, review, and practice-based evidence for validity, bias, effect, harms, cultural relevance, feasibility, and applicability.
  • Recognize nonresponse, deterioration, rupture, dropout risk, access barriers, and implementation problems; reassess safety, diagnosis, hypotheses, goals, and treatment fit before revising care.
  • Collaboratively continue, adapt, sequence, intensify, reduce, consult, refer, transfer, or end treatment based on response, acuity, preferences, evidence, competence, and resources.
  • Conduct step-up, step-down, referral, and transfer decisions with informed participation, role clarity, limited information flow, interim responsibility, and confirmation of continuity.
  • Complete termination with agreed criteria, review of gains and remaining needs, maintenance supports, early-warning signs, follow-up or re-entry options, and responsible documentation.

High-Yield Evaluation Distinctions

DistinctionDecision rule
Goal vs. indicatorA goal names the desired change; an indicator shows whether and how that change is occurring.
Score vs. clinical decisionA score contributes information. Integrate it with safety, function, alliance, behavior, context, and client meaning.
Statistical vs. clinical significanceA calculated difference is not automatically noticeable, valuable, durable, or functionally meaningful to the client system.
Evidence hierarchy vs. applicabilityA rigorous study may still fit poorly when the sample, setting, burden, culture, resources, or decision question differ.
Nonresponse vs. client failureReassess formulation, alliance, access, implementation, competence, modality, sequence, and level of care before assigning blame.
Referral vs. transferA referral recommends another resource; a transfer changes clinical responsibility and requires explicit continuity planning.
Planned ending vs. abandonmentA responsible ending uses clear criteria, communication, risk review, records, options, and reasonable continuity.
Maintenance vs. indefinite treatmentConsolidate client-owned skills and supports, specify warning signs and response steps, and define follow-up or re-entry options.

CHAPTER 1

Defining Progress, Outcomes, Alliance, and the Measurement Plan
BIG IDEA
Progress becomes interpretable only when the client system and therapist agree on the target, baseline, indicators, review interval, and decision the information will support.

The TRACK Monitoring Sequence

StepClinical task
T - Target agreed goals and indicatorsName the identified client or treatment unit, preferred outcome, observable indicators, expected direction, and whose perspective matters.
R - Record baseline, method, and review pointDocument starting conditions, information sources, collection method, burden, frequency, and the date or threshold for review.
A - Attend alliance, safety, function, culture, access, and meaningTrack more than symptoms; include participation, relationships, daily functioning, quality of life, risk, barriers, and client interpretation.
C - Compare repeated information with goals and hypothesesExamine pattern, change, contradiction, missingness, adverse effects, and whether the working formulation still explains the response.
K - Keep, modify, step, refer, transfer, or closeChoose the next action collaboratively, state the rationale, assign responsibility, and set the next review point.

Build a Fit-for-Purpose Monitoring Plan

Planning questionHigh-yield answer
Who is the client and treatment unit?Identify whose goals, safety, functioning, relationships, and experience are being evaluated; do not treat one participant as the whole system.
What is the baseline?Describe the starting frequency, intensity, duration, impact, sequence, resources, and context before claiming improvement or decline.
What information is proportionate?Use the least burdensome reliable combination of repeated measures, behavioral indicators, qualitative feedback, and collateral data needed for the decision.
Is the method usable?Check language, literacy, disability access, developmental fit, cultural meaning, privacy, burden, technology, cost, and scoring competence.
When will the plan be reviewed?Set a time or clinical threshold and identify what would support continuation, adaptation, escalation, referral, transfer, or ending.

Multiple Perspectives Are Data, Not Noise

  • Invite each participant to describe desired change and the meaning of progress; do not silently privilege the therapist or most vocal family member.
  • Separate symptom relief from relationship function, school or work performance, caregiving burden, safety, quality of life, and treatment participation.
  • Treat alliance differences as clinically meaningful. A strong alliance with one participant does not prove that the treatment unit is engaged.
  • Do not force contradictory reports into a single average. Ask what each source observes, when the pattern changes, and what power or access factors shape reporting.
  • Document what was gathered, what remains uncertain, how the client system interpreted it, and the decision that followed.

Case Lab 1: Symptoms Better, System Worse

A 15-year-old reports fewer panic episodes after eight sessions. School attendance has declined, the caregiver is missing work to supervise mornings, and family conflict has intensified. The therapist is preparing to document “good overall progress” from the symptom score alone.

Decision point: How should progress be evaluated before deciding whether to continue the same plan?

MODEL REASONING
Use TRACK. Return to the identified client and treatment unit, compare symptoms with school functioning, caregiver strain, conflict, safety, alliance, and the family’s goals. Clarify baseline and missing information, explore why domains are moving differently, and revise the plan or hypothesis rather than converting one improving score into a global success judgment.

CHAPTER 2

Research Literacy and Evidence Appraisal for Systemic Practice
BIG IDEA
Research informs a clinical decision only after the question, design, sample, effects, harms, limitations, client-system fit, and feasibility are examined together.

The LENS Evidence-Appraisal Sequence

StepClinical task
L - Locate the clinical decision questionSpecify the client system, problem or mechanism, intervention or exposure, comparison, outcomes, timeframe, and decision the evidence must inform.
E - Examine design, source, sample, comparison, and measuresDetermine whether the design can answer the question and whether recruitment, retention, comparison conditions, and measurement support the inference.
N - Note effect, harm, bias, limits, cultural fit, and missing voicesLook beyond statistical significance to magnitude, uncertainty, adverse effects, selective reporting, representation, and who was excluded.
S - Synthesize with expertise, preferences, feasibility, and contextIntegrate the best available research with clinical expertise and client characteristics, culture, values, access, burden, setting, and resources.

Match the Design to the Question

Evidence formUseful forDo not overclaim
Randomized comparisonEstimating average causal effects under defined conditions when implementation and follow-up are adequate.It does not guarantee fit for every client system, setting, outcome, or adaptation.
Observational studyDescribing associations, patterns, risks, service use, or outcomes when randomization is impractical or unethical.Association alone does not establish causation; confounding and selection may shape results.
Qualitative studyUnderstanding experience, meaning, process, acceptability, context, and perspectives that fixed measures may miss.Depth in a selected sample does not establish prevalence or average comparative effect.
Systematic review/meta-analysisSynthesizing a defined body of studies and examining consistency, heterogeneity, and uncertainty.The synthesis cannot erase bias, poor measurement, missing studies, or mismatch in the underlying evidence.
Practice-based evidenceLearning from repeated real-world outcomes, alliance, implementation, and context in the therapist’s own setting.Local patterns require careful measurement and cannot automatically establish general causal claims.

Applicability Questions Before Adoption

  • Does the sample resemble the client system in clinically relevant ways, and which voices or circumstances are absent?
  • Were the setting, therapist preparation, treatment dose, comparison, and outcome priorities similar enough to support transfer?
  • Are the benefits large and meaningful enough to justify burden, opportunity cost, possible harms, and resource demands?
  • Can the intervention be adapted for culture, language, disability, family structure, access, and preference without losing its intended mechanism?
  • What monitoring and stopping rules will show whether the chosen application is helping this client system?

Case Lab 3: Strong Study, Uncertain Fit

A well-designed trial supports a structured intervention for adult couples treated weekly in an academic clinic. The therapist is considering it for a multilingual rural family with adolescents, limited transportation, intermittent internet, and goals focused on caregiving coordination. The original study did not report outcomes for similar families or delivery conditions.

Decision point: How should the therapist use the study without either dismissing or overgeneralizing it?

MODEL REASONING
Use LENS. Clarify the actual decision question, examine the study’s sample, setting, comparison, measures, effect and harms, and identify the transfer gaps. Integrate the evidence with the family’s goals, preferences, culture, language, access, therapist competence, and available alternatives. If adapted, define the mechanism to preserve and monitor response and burden explicitly.

CHAPTER 3

Practice-Based Data, Meaningful Change, and Documentation
BIG IDEA
Repeated data must be interpreted as a pattern with uncertainty, not as a verdict. Trend, function, client meaning, missing information, and measure limitations control the inference.

The SIGNAL Interpretation Sequence

StepClinical task
S - Specify indicator, expected direction, and timeframeState what is being tracked, for whom, how change should look, and when interpretation is warranted.
I - Integrate repeated and multi-informant informationCombine scores with behavior, function, alliance, safety, qualitative feedback, and relevant collateral perspectives.
G - Gauge trend, meaningful change, harm, and uncertaintyConsider direction, magnitude, consistency, clinical value, adverse effects, and plausible alternative explanations.
N - Notice missingness, context, and measurement limitsAsk who or what is absent, why data are missing, whether floor/ceiling or access problems exist, and how context changed.
A - Anchor interpretation to goals and functionReturn to client-defined priorities and observable life or relationship effects rather than treating the instrument as the goal.
L - Log the rationale and next checkDocument the information used, client participation, uncertainty, decision, responsibilities, and next review point.

Meaningful Change: Four Questions

QuestionInterpretive safeguard
Did the number change?Verify scoring, direction, timing, missing items, and expected variability before interpreting the difference.
Is the change likely larger than measurement noise?Use appropriate measure guidance and competence; do not invent certainty from a small difference.
Is the change clinically meaningful?Ask whether the shift changes distress, safety, functioning, relationships, quality of life, or goal attainment.
Is the change meaningful to the client system?Explore value, burden, tradeoffs, whose perspective differs, and whether the outcome reflects client priorities.

Document the Decision, Not Just the Score

  • Name the measure or information source, purpose, date, respondent, conditions, and any language, access, administration, or scoring limitation.
  • Describe the repeated pattern and relevant contradictory data rather than copying an isolated number into the record.
  • Connect interpretation to goals, baseline, safety, functioning, alliance, context, and the client system’s own view.
  • State uncertainty and alternatives considered. A defensible record does not pretend the data are more precise than they are.
  • Record the collaborative decision, clinical rationale, consultation or referral, assigned actions, and next reassessment point.

Case Lab 4: A Jagged Line With Missing Sessions

A family’s weekly functioning rating improves, worsens, then improves again across six sessions. Two ratings are missing because interpretation support was unavailable. During the same period, housing stabilized and one family member began a new medication. The therapist wants to credit the intervention for a “statistically significant upward trend.”

Decision point: What can responsibly be concluded and documented?

MODEL REASONING
Use SIGNAL. Verify the indicator and timeframe, examine the small and incomplete series, gather multi-informant function and alliance data, and note the access-related missingness and major contextual changes. Avoid unsupported statistical or causal claims. Document the observed pattern, client meaning, uncertainty, plausible contributors, current decision, and next review point.

CHAPTER 4

Nonresponse, Deterioration, Rupture, and Plan Revision
BIG IDEA
When treatment stalls or worsens, do not accumulate techniques or blame the client. Reassess safety, diagnosis, systemic hypotheses, alliance, access, implementation, competence, and level of care; then revise for a stated reason.

The REPAIR Revision Sequence

StepClinical task
R - Reassess safety, diagnosis, hypothesis, and fitDetermine whether acuity changed, key information was missed, the formulation no longer explains the pattern, or the current care is mismatched.
E - Elicit client-system feedback and meaningAsk each relevant participant what feels helpful, harmful, irrelevant, inaccessible, unsafe, or inconsistent with their goals.
P - Probe alliance, implementation, access, and dropout barriersAssess rupture, power, attendance, homework or practice fit, language, disability, transportation, finances, technology, competing duties, and treatment burden.
A - Adapt goals, sequence, modality, frequency, or participantsChange only the elements supported by the revised hypothesis and collaborative decision; preserve what is working.
I - Involve consultation, referral, or level-of-care changeUse supervision, specialty input, medical or psychiatric coordination, and step-up/down decisions when needs exceed competence or setting.
R - Review responseDefine what improvement, harm, or continued nonresponse will mean and when the revised plan will be evaluated.

The Nonresponse Reassessment Ladder

LevelQuestions before changing technique
1. Safety and stabilityHas danger, abuse, coercion, substance or medical instability, severe impairment, or participation capacity changed?
2. Assessment and hypothesisWere diagnosis, treatment unit, relational sequence, trauma, development, culture, health, substance use, or environmental conditions misunderstood?
3. Goals and measurementAre goals still relevant, indicators valid and accessible, perspectives represented, and change thresholds realistic?
4. Alliance and powerDoes each participant feel understood and safe enough to disagree? Has the therapist joined unevenly or missed a rupture?
5. Implementation and accessWas the plan delivered as intended, practiced, affordable, accessible, acceptable, and possible in the client’s context?
6. Model, competence, and level of careDoes the mechanism fit? Is consultation, different expertise, another format, or a higher/lower intensity needed?

Write a Testable Revision

A plan revision should name the new information, updated hypothesis, unchanged priorities, specific modification, alternatives considered, client participation, responsibilities, expected response, safety plan when relevant, and date or threshold for review. “Try something different” is not a clinical rationale.

CHAPTER 5

Step-Up, Step-Down, Referral, Transfer, and Continuity
BIG IDEA
Change level, modality, or provider because acuity, response, fit, competence, preference, and feasibility support the change. Explain the decision, coordinate with permission, preserve interim responsibility, and confirm continuity.

The STEPS Transition Sequence

StepClinical task
S - Screen acuity, function, competence, and continuity riskDirectly assess current danger, impairment, stability, participation capacity, treatment response, and what could happen during a gap.
T - Test current treatment fit and alternativesCompare continuation, adaptation, consultation, specialty referral, step-up/down, transfer, and ending against needs and least restrictive appropriate care.
E - Explain options, benefits, burdens, and rolesSupport informed participation using understandable language and realistic information about access, cost, timing, limits, and responsibilities.
P - Plan permission, information limits, and warm handoffIdentify who may communicate, for what purpose, what minimum information is needed, and how the receiving resource will be engaged.
S - Secure interim care and confirm the transitionClarify who remains responsible, what happens if access fails or risk changes, whether contact occurred, and how continuity is documented.

Choose the Transition That Matches the Need

DecisionTypical rationaleContinuity safeguard
Adapt current careThe setting and competence remain appropriate, but goals, participants, frequency, modality, sequence, or access supports need revision.Document the change, response indicator, and review threshold.
Step upAcuity, instability, impairment, monitoring need, or failed outpatient response requires more intensive or specialized care.Address immediate safety, coordinate promptly, and define interim responsibility.
Step downStability and gains support lower intensity while maintenance, support, and re-entry planning remain active.Define warning signs, supports, follow-up, and how to resume care.
Refer while continuingA bounded specialty need can be addressed by another professional while MFT treatment remains indicated.Clarify client identity, roles, permission, information limits, and coordination question.
TransferAnother provider or setting will assume primary responsibility because of fit, competence, access, or service change.Use a warm handoff when possible and confirm the receiving plan.

When the Handoff Is Incomplete

  • Do not assume that giving a phone number completes a transfer. Verify whether the client can access, afford, understand, and use the option.
  • Clarify the therapist’s ongoing role and reasonable interim availability consistent with acuity, law, ethics, policy, competence, and setting.
  • If the first option fails, reassess risk and barriers, discuss alternatives, document outreach and decisions, and avoid punitive discharge language.
  • Share only authorized and appropriate information for the stated purpose, subject to controlling law and emergency exceptions.
  • A payer or administrative decision may constrain options, but it does not eliminate clinical assessment, communication, documentation, and continuity planning.

Conduct direct current risk and stability assessment, mobilize emergency or higher-level resources as indicated, involve appropriate supports under controlling consent and emergency rules, and coordinate the transition. Do not frame acuity-based escalation as simple termination; document interim responsibility and confirm continuity.

CHAPTER 6

Termination, Gains Maintenance, Relapse Prevention, and Follow-Up
BIG IDEA
Termination is a clinical transition, not a disappearing act. Use agreed criteria, current safety and response evidence, client meaning, maintenance supports, warning signs, follow-up options, and responsible records.

The CLOSE Termination Sequence

StepClinical task
C - Clarify indication, criteria, readiness, and safetyIdentify why ending is being considered, whether agreed goals and criteria are met, whose view differs, and whether current risk permits the planned timing.
L - Link progress evidence to goals and client meaningReview baseline, repeated outcomes, functioning, relationships, alliance, client-defined change, and remaining uncertainty.
O - Outline gains, remaining needs, vulnerabilities, and early warningsName what changed, what did not, likely stress points, risk cues, and when additional help would be appropriate.
S - Strengthen client-owned supportsConsolidate skills, relational agreements, community and professional supports, coping resources, and responsibility for ongoing practice.
E - Establish maintenance, relapse, follow-up, and re-entry optionsSpecify action steps, contact routes, referral resources, record and billing closure, follow-up if offered, and how care may be resumed.

Different Endings Require Different Responses

EndingPrimary task
Goals met / planned terminationConsolidate change, review remaining needs, plan maintenance and follow-up, process meaning, and close collaboratively.
Client-initiated endingRespect autonomy, invite feedback, assess current risk, discuss options and consequences, provide appropriate resources, and document.
Therapist-initiated endingUse a clinically and ethically supportable reason, adequate notice when possible, referrals or transfer, records, and continuity safeguards.
Administrative or financial interruptionSeparate the external constraint from the clinical assessment; explore options and maintain reasonable continuity planning.
Unplanned dropout / loss of contactUse proportionate outreach based on risk, policy, consent, and context; document attempts, resources, and closure decisions.

Build a Maintenance and Re-Entry Plan

  • Name the client system’s own explanation for what changed and which skills, relationships, routines, meanings, and supports produced the gains.
  • Identify predictable stressors, vulnerabilities, early-warning signs, relapse indicators, and the difference between a lapse and a crisis.
  • Write specific first responses: who notices, who asks, what the system tries, how long it tries, and what result triggers professional help.
  • Clarify ongoing medications, medical or community supports, referrals, peer resources, safety steps, and who owns each action.
  • Explain follow-up availability, emergency limits, records access, re-entry procedures, and circumstances that require a different level of care.

Review baseline and evidence of change, invite each partner’s meaning, identify remaining vulnerabilities and early warnings, consolidate client-owned repair steps and supports, rehearse a response to caregiving stress, clarify follow-up and re-entry options, and document the collaborative termination criteria and plan.

B. Continue indefinitely because fear of relapse means termination criteria are not met.

C. End immediately and avoid discussing future services so dependence is not reinforced.

D. Require monthly booster sessions whether or not the couple wants them.

Confidence: 1 2 3 4 Answer: ______

10. Coverage ends while goals remain active and no receiving provider is confirmed. Which plan BEST protects continuity?

A. Record that insurance caused termination and close services on the final covered day.

B. Separate the payer constraint from clinical need, explore feasible options, make a consented warm referral, plan interim care, and verify the handoff.

C. Promise free care until another provider accepts the case, regardless of setting policy or feasibility.

D. Transfer responsibility to the client as soon as a referral list is sent.

Confidence: 1 2 3 4 Answer: ______

Formative Answer Key and Learning Rationales

1. Correct Answer: A

Why A: Correct. Evaluation starts with a collaborative, observable target, baseline, represented perspectives, and a defined review decision.

Why B: Measure-as-goal error. A tool may support monitoring, but the family’s goal and relevant functional and relational indicators control the plan.

Why C: Too late. Waiting until termination removes the information needed to revise treatment while it is occurring.

Why D: Therapist-impression trap. Clinical observation matters but should be integrated with client-system feedback and other relevant data.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When a goal is vague, define the target, baseline, indicators, and review point before choosing a measure.

Transfer rule: Progress requires a shared decision frame, not a score alone. Remediation: R1

2. Correct Answer: B

Why A: Averaging erases a clinically important alliance and safety difference between participants.

Why B: Correct. The dropout and safety signal requires direct, participant-specific exploration and possible repair before more technique.

Why C: Invalidating and unsafe. Discomfort may occur, but the therapist must not presume that feeling unsafe is evidence of effectiveness.

Why D: Feedback neglect. A credible dropout or safety signal should not wait for the calendar.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Discordant alliance data call for curiosity about each participant, power, safety, goals, and therapist contribution.

Transfer rule: Treat disagreement among informants as information to understand, not noise to average away. Remediation: R3

3. Correct Answer: C

Why A: Overcorrection. Differences limit certainty but do not make relevant research useless.

Why B: Hierarchy error. Rigor does not erase applicability, feasibility, culture, access, or informed preference.

Why C: Correct. This option preserves the evidence, identifies transfer gaps, integrates client context, and makes adaptation testable.

Why D: Familiarity is not evidence of fit and does not answer the client-specific decision question.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Research strength and client-system applicability are separate judgments; evaluate both.

Transfer rule: Integrate evidence, expertise, client characteristics, culture, preferences, and feasibility. Remediation: R4

4. Correct Answer: D

Why A: Universal-effect error. An average result does not prove benefit for every client system.

Why B: Overstatement. A small mean effect may matter in some contexts and not others; magnitude must be interpreted with outcomes and burden.

Why C: Hierarchy error. Missing harm information remains a limitation even in a high-level synthesis.

Why D: Correct. The evidence supports a qualified conclusion that preserves effect size, variation, harms, applicability, and client priorities.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Look past the publication label to magnitude, uncertainty, adverse effects, and fit.

Transfer rule: A rigorous source can still support only a cautious, bounded inference. Remediation: R4

5. Correct Answer: A

Why A: Correct. The available pattern is incomplete and must be interpreted with missingness, access, function, measure limits, and client meaning.

Why B: Endpoint bias. First-to-last comparison can misrepresent fluctuation, missing data, and unchanged function.

Why C: Unsupported certainty. Variability alone does not establish failure.

Why D: Data-cleaning error. Access-related missingness is clinically meaningful and must be documented, not erased.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When data are sparse or missing, narrow the conclusion and widen the contextual inquiry.

Transfer rule: Document uncertainty and missingness before claiming trend or cause. Remediation: R5

6. Correct Answer: B

Why A: Technique accumulation. More activity is not a substitute for reassessing why the current plan is not working.

Why B: Correct. REPAIR returns to safety, formulation, alliance, access, implementation, competence, and level of care before revising.

Why C: Blaming and unsupported. Completing the plan without improvement may signal a plan or fit problem, not noncompliance.

Why D: Passive continuation ignores available evidence of nonresponse and risks extending ineffective care.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Nonresponse is a reassessment cue before it is a technique cue.

Transfer rule: Revise the hypothesis and plan for a stated reason, then define the next response threshold. Remediation: R7

7. Correct Answer: C

Why A: Poor sequencing. Comparable routine data do not take priority over new acute risk.

Why B: Unsafe delay. New intent and loss of safety-plan capacity require current assessment and action.

Why C: Correct. Direct risk assessment and appropriate stabilization or level-of-care response override routine evaluation.

Why D: Abandonment risk. Higher-level care may be needed, but the transition must be actively assessed and coordinated.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Acute deterioration changes the decision point from routine evaluation to safety and level of care.

Transfer rule: Escalation is a coordinated transition, not a reason to disappear. Remediation: R6/R8

8. Correct Answer: D

Why A: A referral list does not establish access or transfer responsibility.

Why B: Continuity failure. Barriers and current need require a proportionate clinical response, not punitive distance.

Why C: Overdisclosure. Information sharing requires an appropriate legal and consent basis and should fit the purpose.

Why D: Correct. The therapist reassesses, addresses barriers, coordinates with permission, and clarifies interim ownership.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY A handoff is not complete until access, roles, information boundaries, and interim responsibility are addressed.

Transfer rule: Verify continuity; do not infer it from a referral being offered. Remediation: R9

9. Correct Answer: A

Why A: Correct. CLOSE converts gains into a client-owned maintenance, relapse-response, follow-up, and re-entry plan.

Why B: Fear of relapse can be addressed through planning and does not automatically justify indefinite treatment.

Why C: Abrupt closure wastes a clinically useful transition and leaves early warnings and response steps unclear.

Why D: Therapist-owned aftercare ignores informed preference and individual need.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When goals are met, consolidate change and prepare for predictable stress rather than extending treatment automatically.
TRANSFER RULE
Good termination increases client ownership and makes future help-seeking clearer. Remediation: R10

10. Correct Answer: B

Why A: Administrative causation does not erase clinical assessment and continuity duties.

Why B: Correct. The response integrates clinical need, realistic alternatives, permission, a warm referral, interim planning, and confirmation.

Why C: Unsustainable promise. Continuity does not require ignoring competence, policy, resources, or boundaries.

Why D: Cold-transfer error. Sending a list does not establish that another provider accepted responsibility.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Separate the external reason for interruption from the clinical steps needed for a safe transition.
TRANSFER RULE
Administrative endings still require assessment, communication, documentation, and reasonable continuity. Remediation: R9
