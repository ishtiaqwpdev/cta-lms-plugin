How to Use This Workbook

Workbook 8 teaches intervention choice as a disciplined decision sequence. Begin with the identified client, treatment unit, maintaining mechanism, agreed goal, culture, access, safety, readiness, and preference. Select a model because its stance and mechanism fit that decision point. Observe what happens for each participant, then continue, adapt, consult, refer, or stop according to the evidence.

LEARN -> ORGANIZE -> APPLY -> ANALYZE -> REPAIR Study the six chapters in order. Use the six reasoning tools to organize each case, complete all eight case labs and the reusable decision record, answer all ten formative questions before reading the rationales, and remediate every miss or low-confidence response. Mnemonics organize judgment; they do not replace assessment, model-specific competence, current law, ethics, consultation, or supervision.
SAFETY AND CHOICE OVERRIDE
When danger, coercion, abuse, acute impairment, loss of regulation, dissociation, or inability to participate voluntarily is present, direct assessment, stabilization, and an appropriate level-of-care response take priority over routine model-specific technique.

Workbook Learning Objectives

  • Select a coherent intervention model from the client system's maintaining mechanism, treatment unit, goals, readiness, culture, strengths, access needs, and preferences.
  • Maintain a systemic and collaborative stance while tracking language, power, therapist influence, alliance, culture, and client authorship.
  • Apply narrative externalizing, mapping, unique-outcome, re-authoring, and preferred-identity practices without erasing responsibility or material context.
  • Use solution-focused and motivational interviewing methods to elicit preferred futures, exceptions, resources, change talk, autonomy, and small observable next steps.
  • Use experiential and emotion-focused process methods to deepen present-moment awareness, meaning, expression, and relational contact within a tolerable and voluntary range.
  • Apply behavioral and cognitive interventions through functional assessment, skills practice, reinforcement, experiments, and appraisal work connected to reciprocal relational patterns.
  • Use mindfulness- and acceptance-informed practices with rationale, choice, accessibility, trauma sensitivity, values linkage, and monitoring for adverse or avoidant effects.
  • Integrate methods coherently, evaluate alliance and outcome response, revise nonresponsive plans, and recognize safety, evidence, competence, ethical, documentation, or referral limits.

CHAPTER 1

Intervention Coherence, Treatment Unit, and Model Fit
BIG IDEA
Choose an intervention because it fits the client system, maintaining mechanism, agreed goal, readiness, culture, access, evidence, and preference. Technique follows formulation; feedback controls revision.

The MATCH Model-Selection Sequence

StepClinical task
M - Map the maintaining sequenceDescribe the observable reciprocal pattern, relevant individual/contextual contributors, and evidence supporting or challenging the hypothesis.
A - Align mechanism and agreed goalState what process must change and how that change connects to the client system's own goals.
T - Test treatment unit and readinessConfirm who must participate, the appropriate format, regulation, capacity, motivation, timing, and practical feasibility.
C - Check safety, culture, access, evidence, and preferenceScreen for overrides and adapt for identity, disability, language, resources, values, and informed choice.
H - Hold coherence while tracking feedbackUse a model-consistent intervention, observe response and alliance, and continue or revise according to data.

Build the Clinical Logic Chain

QuestionExample of a coherent answer
What pattern maintains the concern?A cue is interpreted as rejection, prompting criticism; withdrawal follows and confirms the rejection meaning.
What is the agreed target?Recognize the sequence sooner and replace criticism/withdrawal with a brief, direct request and reliable return.
What mechanism should change?Meaning, regulation, and the reinforcing interactional response.
Why this model now?The clients endorse the target, can remain sufficiently regulated, and prefer a relationally focused approach.
What will count as response?Less escalation, faster recovery, clearer requests, improved alliance, and generalization outside sessions.
What would prompt revision?Fear, shutdown, worsening conflict, no measurable movement, poor fit, access barriers, or competence limits.

Common Factors and Model-Specific Methods

Alliance, hope, collaboration, cultural responsiveness, credible rationale, attention to context, and ongoing feedback support treatment across models. They do not make models interchangeable. A narrative question, motivational reflection, experiential enactment, behavioral experiment, cognitive reappraisal, and mindfulness practice each carries a different stance, target, and mechanism. Use shared factors to support a coherent method—not to disguise drift.

  • State the intervention target in observable, relational, and client-relevant language.
  • Explain why the proposed method fits and what alternatives are reasonable; obtain meaningful consent for higher-intensity or unfamiliar procedures.
  • Consider the quality and limits of evidence, client characteristics and preferences, therapist competence, and practical feasibility.
  • Do not force a model onto a client system whose goals, culture, access needs, safety, or readiness make it a poor fit.
  • Reassess when new information changes the treatment unit, formulation, level of care, or expected mechanism.

Case Lab 1: Technique Before Formulation

A therapist recently attended a workshop on a structured communication exercise and plans to use it with every couple. New clients Priya and Lena request help after repeated conflict about caregiving for an ill parent. The therapist has not clarified whether the conflict is driven primarily by workload, grief, cultural expectations, unspoken resentment, safety concerns, or a recurring pursue-withdraw sequence.

Decision point: What should the therapist do before assigning the familiar exercise?

MODEL REASONING
Use MATCH. Clarify the client and treatment unit, map the observable sequence and contextual demands, identify the agreed target, assess safety and readiness, elicit cultural meanings and preferences, and then choose a method whose mechanism fits. The exercise may eventually be useful, but familiarity is not sufficient rationale.

CHAPTER 2

Narrative Intervention and Re-Authoring
BIG IDEA
Narrative work treats people as meaning-makers rather than problem identities. Use collaborative language to externalize the problem, map effects and influence, locate unique outcomes, and strengthen preferred identity and action while preserving accountability and context.

The STORY Narrative Sequence

StepClinical task
S - Separate the person from the problemUse the client system's language to describe the problem as a pattern or influence rather than an identity.
T - Trace effects, influence, and contextMap how the problem affects relationships and how people respond; include power, culture, discrimination, resources, and material conditions.
O - Open space for exceptions and unique outcomesIdentify moments when the problem had less influence and ask what those moments reveal about skills, values, commitments, or relationships.
R - Re-author preferred identity and relationshipsDevelop a richer account of who clients are, what they stand for, and which relational futures they prefer.
Y - Yield concrete next actionsTranslate the preferred account into observable actions, supportive audiences, documents, rituals, or reminders that clients choose.

Externalize Carefully

Useful moveSafeguard
Name the problem influenceUse tentative, client-preferred language; do not impose a clever label or turn experience into a slogan.
Map effectsAsk about emotional, relational, practical, cultural, bodily, and opportunity costs without assuming every effect is negative.
Map personal influenceIdentify responses, resistance, survival, responsibility, and support; avoid suggesting that clients are powerless.
Locate social discourseExplore messages about family, gender, race, disability, faith, class, sexuality, success, or respect without reducing the person to a category.
Preserve accountabilityExternalizing identity does not externalize choices, impact, repair obligations, or safety responsibility.

From Unique Outcome to Preferred Story

  • Identify a specific event that does not fit the problem-saturated account.
  • Ask what made the event possible and who noticed, supported, or would understand its significance.
  • Link the event to values, intentions, skills, commitments, relationships, or cultural knowledge.
  • Develop the account across time: where else has this quality appeared, and what future would it support?
  • Invite a small action, document, letter, ritual, witness, or reminder only when it fits client preference and privacy.
  • Evaluate whether the new story increases agency and connection rather than pressuring optimism or denying constraint.
ACCOUNTABILITY BOUNDARY
Do not rename violence, coercion, exploitation, discrimination, or repeated harmful choices in a way that softens responsibility. A person is more than a harmful pattern and remains accountable for behavior, impact, safety, and repair.

Case Lab 2: The Problem-Saturated Identity

A family repeatedly calls 16-year-old Mateo “the liar.” Mateo has hidden missed assignments and has also recently begun asking a teacher for help. Caregivers feel frightened about school failure and trust. Mateo says the label makes honesty feel pointless. There is no indication of immediate danger, but the academic and relational consequences are real.

Decision point: How can narrative work reduce the identity label without excusing concealment?

MODEL REASONING
Use STORY to separate Mateo from “the hiding pattern,” map its effects on trust and school functioning, explore what the pattern promises or protects, and identify moments when Mateo moved toward honesty or support. Develop a preferred account centered on responsibility and help-seeking, then choose an observable repair action. Preserve caregiver concerns and Mateo's accountability.

CHAPTER 3

Solution-Focused and Motivational Interviewing Approaches
BIG IDEA
Brief, collaborative methods build from client-defined futures, exceptions, resources, coping, autonomy, and change language. They do not bypass safety, invalidate suffering, or convert therapist preference into client motivation.

The SPARK Brief-Change Sequence

StepClinical task
S - Specify the preferred futureAsk what clients want instead, how they would recognize it, and who would notice first.
P - Prioritize autonomy and readinessRespect choice, clarify the focus, and determine whether the client is exploring, preparing, acting, or not seeking change.
A - Ask about exceptions, scales, coping, and change talkElicit detailed evidence of resources, movement, reasons, ability, need, commitment, and conditions that support change.
R - Reflect strengths and ambivalenceUse accurate reflections and summaries rather than cheerleading, confrontation, or selective listening.
K - Keep the next step small and observableTranslate insight or scale movement into a feasible client-chosen action and review what happens.

Solution-Focused Questions: Sequence Matters

Question typePurposeCommon misuse
Preferred futureMake desired change observable and relationally meaningful.Asking for an ideal future before validating crisis, loss, discrimination, or concrete barriers.
ExceptionIdentify when the concern is absent, less intense, or handled differently.Treating one exception as proof that the problem is easy or fully controllable.
CopingRecognize survival, persistence, support, and what prevents worsening.Romanticizing endurance or overlooking that coping is exhausted.
ScalingClarify position, meaning, resources, confidence, readiness, or progress.Using a number as a score without exploring what it represents.
Next-stepConvert existing resources into a small observable experiment.Assigning a therapist-selected task that ignores access, culture, or preference.

Motivational Interviewing: Evoke, Do Not Persuade

SignalTherapist response
AmbivalenceReflect both the reasons to change and the reasons not to change; avoid taking the pro-change side of an argument.
Change talkExplore desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment, activation, and steps in the client's own words.
Sustain talkListen without labeling resistance; understand values, concerns, costs, identity, and context.
DiscordAttend to relational strain, autonomy threat, misattunement, or an imposed focus; repair rather than intensify persuasion.
Planning readinessMove toward a plan when commitment and readiness are present; otherwise continue exploration and support autonomy.

Case Lab 3: Exceptions, Scaling, and an Observable Step

Dana and Bo say, “We never cooperate in the mornings.” Asked about a recent morning that was even slightly better, they recall preparing school bags the night before and using one shared reminder. They rate current cooperation 3 out of 10 and say a 4 would mean one calm handoff before leaving home.

Decision point: What should the therapist do with the exception and scale information?

MODEL REASONING
Use SPARK to build detail around what was different, who contributed, and why it mattered. Clarify the preferred 4-out-of-10 interaction, identify the smallest feasible repeatable step, and ask what could interfere. Treat the experiment as information and review its effects rather than promising that the exception is a complete solution.

Case Lab 4: Ambivalence Is Not a Cue to Argue

Noor says cannabis helps with sleep and connection with friends but is also contributing to missed morning responsibilities and conflict with a partner. CHAPTER 4

Experiential and Emotion-Focused Process Work
BIG IDEA
Experiential work uses present-moment process, emotion, meaning, embodiment, therapist presence, and relational contact to create new experience. Regulation, permission, choice, and pacing determine whether evocation is therapeutic.

The FEEL Experiential Sequence

StepClinical task
F - Focus present processSlow down and notice what is happening now in emotion, body, meaning, interaction, and therapist-client contact.
E - Establish regulation, permission, and choiceAssess tolerance, consent, safety, and alternatives; clarify that the client may pause or decline.
E - Evoke emotion and meaning within toleranceDeepen, symbolize, enact, or express only as far as the client can remain oriented and engaged.
L - Link experience to the relational patternConnect present experience to protection, unmet need, identity, interaction, and the treatment target.
L - Leverage new expression or actionShape a congruent statement, boundary, request, compassionate response, or other new contact; process impact.

Regulation Before Evocation

ObservePossible response
Tolerable activationSlow the process, name experience tentatively, confirm permission, and support continued contact.
Flooding or escalating conflictReduce intensity, orient to the room, support physiological regulation, and reconsider the task.
Shutdown, freezing, or dissociationStop deeper inward focus, reorient externally, restore choice, assess safety, and use trauma-informed alternatives.
Compliance without ownershipAsk what the client wants, whether the exercise fits, and how therapist influence or power is affecting participation.
Shame or alliance ruptureName the impact, invite correction, repair therapist contribution, and do not defend the technique.

Therapist Use of Self

  • Use immediacy or therapist observation to illuminate present process, not to make the session about the therapist.
  • Offer interpretations tentatively and check resonance; emotional intensity does not prove accuracy.
  • Track rescue impulses, pressure for catharsis, discomfort with silence, cultural assumptions about expression, and preference for visible emotion.
  • Adapt for neurodivergence, disability, language, spirituality, trauma history, and communication style; congruent contact can look different across clients.
  • Process the intervention afterward: what changed, what felt helpful or intrusive, and what should happen next?
PACING SAFEGUARD
Do not equate intensity, tears, disclosure, catharsis, or compliance with progress. The best next step may be to slow down, orient externally, restore choice, repair the alliance, or select a less evocative method.

Case Lab 5: When Emotion Exceeds the Window

During a family exercise, Sam begins breathing rapidly, stops responding to questions, and stares past the therapist. Another family member continues urging Sam to “finally say what happened.” The therapist had obtained general consent for experiential work but did not discuss how anyone could pause the exercise.

Decision point: What should the therapist do first?

MODEL REASONING
Use FEEL by stopping evocation, reducing social pressure, orienting Sam to the present environment, restoring explicit choice, and assessing regulation, dissociation, safety, and immediate needs. Do not continue for the sake of completion. Later, repair informed consent and decide whether a different pace, format, skill, consultation, or referral is indicated.

CHAPTER 5

Behavioral and Cognitive Interventions in Relational Systems
BIG IDEA
Behavioral and cognitive methods are systemic when they connect antecedents, appraisals, actions, consequences, reinforcement, and reciprocal effects across people and contexts. Assessment precedes the experiment; response data guide revision.

The CHANGE Behavioral-Cognitive Sequence

StepClinical task
C - Clarify the behavioral, cognitive, and relational targetDefine what each participant does, thinks, predicts, or avoids and how the target connects to agreed goals.
H - Hunt antecedents, consequences, appraisals, and reciprocal effectsMap cues, meanings, behavior, short-term payoff, long-term cost, and how one response becomes the next person's cue.
A - Arrange a skill or experimentSelect a feasible, consensual practice that tests the hypothesis and includes necessary supports.
N - Notice responseTrack behavior, emotion, cognition, relationship impact, adherence, barriers, and unintended effects.
G - Generalize learningIdentify where learning transfers, which contexts differ, and what supports maintenance.
E - Evaluate and reviseContinue, shape, simplify, change the formulation, consult, refer, or stop according to findings.

Functional Analysis in a Relational Loop

ElementQuestions
Antecedent / cueWhat happens immediately before? Which person, setting, demand, memory, sensation, or interpretation is involved?
AppraisalWhat does each participant predict or conclude about self, other, relationship, danger, failure, or control?
BehaviorWhat is observable: request, criticize, avoid, reassure, check, accommodate, withdraw, argue, use a substance, or seek support?
Immediate consequenceWhat relief, attention, escape, control, certainty, closeness, or reduced conflict follows?
Longer-term consequenceWhat happens to trust, functioning, symptoms, opportunity, burden, or the next cycle?
Reciprocal reinforcementHow does each person's response become a cue or consequence that strengthens the other person's pattern?

Skills, Experiments, and Cognitive Work

  • Teach and rehearse a specific skill only after identifying the performance gap, context, motivation, and supports; knowledge is not always the missing variable.
  • Frame homework as a collaborative experiment with rationale, choice, feasibility, prediction, tracking, and review—not a compliance test.
  • Use cognitive methods to examine appraisals and alternative meanings while respecting cultural reality, discrimination, trauma, and actual danger.
  • Link one person's belief to the relational loop: how does the appraisal shape behavior, how does the other respond, and what feedback follows?
  • Adapt written materials, exposure-like tasks, technology, timing, sensory demands, language, cost, caregiving burden, and privacy for access.
  • Do not use reassurance reduction, exposure, response prevention, or high-intensity experiments beyond competence or without adequate assessment and consent.

Case Lab 6: A Behavioral-Cognitive Cycle, Not One Faulty Thought

When Eli arrives home late, Morgan thinks, “I am not important,” and sends repeated messages. Eli interprets the messages as proof of being controlled, silences the phone, and delays returning. Morgan then checks more urgently. Both want less escalation. Work schedules are unpredictable, and neither has reliable access to a phone during every shift.

Decision point: How should the therapist design a behavioral-cognitive experiment?

MODEL REASONING
Use CHANGE to map cues, appraisals, behaviors, immediate relief, longer-term cost, and reciprocal reinforcement. Collaboratively test a feasible plan that fits workplace access—for example, a predictable check-in window plus an agreed response to uncertainty—while examining the meanings driving urgent checking and withdrawal. Track effects for both people and revise rather than treating nonadherence as resistance.

CHAPTER 6

Mindfulness, Acceptance, Integration, and Response Evaluation
BIG IDEA
Mindfulness and acceptance practices require rationale, choice, adaptation, and monitoring. Integration requires one coherent hypothesis. Alliance, outcome, adverse response, and competence data determine whether to continue, revise, consult, refer, or stop.

The PAUSE Mindfulness-Acceptance Sequence

StepClinical task
P - Prepare safety, rationale, accessibility, and choiceExplain the purpose, assess fit and contraindications, offer alternatives, and establish how to pause.
A - Attend to present experienceNotice selected internal or external experience with a duration and focus that fit the client.
U - Unhook and observe with opennessRelate differently to thoughts, feelings, urges, or sensations rather than forcing them away or treating them as commands.
S - Select a values-linked responseConnect awareness and acceptance to a chosen action in the client's relational and cultural context.
E - Evaluate impact and adaptAsk what happened; monitor grounding, detachment, shame, spiritual conflict, avoidance, access, and usefulness.

Mindfulness Is Not Neutral

Fit questionAdaptation examples
Attention focusOffer external sensory orientation, movement, eyes open, relational focus, or brief contact instead of sustained inward attention.
Trauma/dissociationUse short doses, active orientation, explicit stop choices, grounding, and close monitoring; discontinue when detachment increases.
Disability/neurodivergenceAdapt posture, stillness, breath focus, sensory load, language, timing, and response format.
Culture/spiritualityAsk about meaning and preference; do not assume a secular or spiritual frame, or appropriate a practice without context.
Acceptance languageClarify that acceptance means making room for present experience when useful—not approving injustice, danger, abuse, or avoidable barriers.
Values and actionTranslate awareness into a chosen relational action; avoid using mindfulness only to tolerate conditions that require protection or change.

Integrate Through MATCH

Integration controlQuestion
One working hypothesisWhat maintaining sequence or process currently organizes the case?
Explicit functionWhat does each method contribute, and why is it sequenced now?
Client authorshipDo the goals, language, values, preferences, and choices belong to the client system?
Safety and competenceAre there overrides, consent needs, evidence limits, or skills requiring consultation/referral?
Distributed responseWhat changes for each participant, and who feels helped, pressured, excluded, unsafe, or burdened?
Revision ruleWhich findings support continuation, adaptation, discontinuation, consultation, or transfer?

When the Plan Is Not Working

  • Reassess safety, impairment, trauma response, dissociation, medical/substance factors, capacity, and level of care before interpreting nonresponse as motivation.
  • Review alliance and outcome by participant; improvement for one member does not erase pressure, disengagement, or harm for another.
  • Restate the treatment unit, goals, hypothesis, and mechanism. Stop adding disconnected methods.
  • Check therapist contribution, cultural misattunement, access barriers, poor dose or pacing, unclear consent, and whether the task was feasible.
  • Consult or refer when model-specific skill, risk expertise, accessibility, language, medical coordination, or another competence is needed.
  • Document the data, client feedback, clinical reasoning, consent updates, action taken, and next reassessment point.

Case Lab 7: Mindfulness Increases Detachment

A trauma-exposed client agrees to try a five-minute eyes-closed body scan. After one minute, the client becomes still, reports feeling far away, and later says, “I disappeared so I could finish what you asked.” The therapist considers praising persistence and repeating the exercise until it becomes easier.

Decision point: What should the therapist do first and how should the plan change?

MODEL REASONING
Use PAUSE. Stop the inward practice, orient externally, assess dissociation, safety, and current functioning, and explicitly restore choice. Validate the client's feedback rather than praising compliance. Offer alternatives such as eyes-open sensory orientation, movement, shorter duration, or a non-mindfulness method, and consult or refer if trauma-specific needs exceed competence.

Case Lab 8: Integration Has Become Drift

A therapist has used externalizing questions, scaling, a chair exercise, communication homework, cognitive restructuring, and mindfulness with a family over five sessions. The therapist cannot state how the methods connect. One caregiver reports feeling blamed, the adolescent has stopped speaking, and the original target is unchanged.

Decision point: What should happen before another technique is introduced?

MODEL REASONING
Return to MATCH. Review safety, alliance, and outcomes by participant; clarify the client, treatment unit, and agreed target; restate or revise the systemic hypothesis; identify whether one model and mechanism fit; repair therapist contribution; and consult, refer, or change format when indicated. Discontinue methods without clear rationale or benefit.

Answer Key and Detailed Rationales

Review the clinical decision, not only the letter. For every miss or low-confidence correct answer, identify the remediation code and write a transferable rule.

1. Correct Answer: B

Why A: Premature. A structured skill may help, but its usefulness depends on the mechanism, treatment unit, readiness, context, and response plan.

Why B: Correct. MATCH places formulation, agreed goal, fit, safety, preference, and observable feedback before technique selection.

Why C: Insufficient. Client preference matters, but choosing a label is not the same as building a clinical rationale.

Why D: Incorrect. Adding methods before clarifying one working hypothesis increases technique accumulation and obscures evaluation.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When the stem lacks a formulation, choose the option that establishes the logic chain before technique.

Transfer rule: Intervention choice follows mechanism, goal, fit, and a response indicator. Remediation: R1/R2

2. Correct Answer: D

Why A: Minimizing. A softer label can erase concrete harm and responsibility rather than support meaningful externalizing.

Why B: Incorrect. Narrative practice separates identity from the problem but does not remove accountability for behavior and impact.

Why C: Incomplete. Strengths matter, but exclusive positive focus bypasses safety, effects, repair, and the family's stated concern.

Why D: Correct. STORY protects personhood while mapping harmful effects, context, responsibility, and accountable preferred action.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Externalizing is not exoneration; look for both non-shaming identity and accurate accountability.

Transfer rule: Separate the person from the pattern without separating behavior from impact and repair. Remediation: R4

3. Correct Answer: A

Why A: Correct. A unique outcome becomes clinically useful when its details are linked to agency, values, abilities, relationships, and a preferred future.

Why B: Overgeneralized. One exception does not establish that the concern is resolved.

Why C: Prescriptive. Exact replication may be impossible and ignores which elements actually mattered.

Why D: Incorrect. Exceptions and unique outcomes are central resources in narrative re-authoring.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Do not stop at finding an exception; develop its meaning and link it to future action.

Transfer rule: Thicken a unique outcome into a client-authored account of agency and preference. Remediation: R3

4. Correct Answer: C

Why A: Unsafe sequencing. Preferred-future work must not replace direct assessment of escalating threats and immediate safety.

Why B: Premature. An exception-based task may increase risk or minimize danger before stabilization and planning.

Why C: Correct. Safety and level-of-care decisions override routine solution-focused technique.

Why D: Incorrect. A scale can supplement but cannot substitute for behaviorally specific safety assessment.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Risk changes the order of operations even when the requested model is brief and strengths-based.

Transfer rule: Assess and stabilize danger before routine exception, future, or task questions. Remediation: R5

5. Correct Answer: B

Why A: Persuasive and likely to evoke argument. The client already voices concern; the therapist should explore rather than take the pro-change side.

Why B: Correct. The response reflects ambivalence and evokes the client's own values, reasons, and readiness while protecting autonomy.

Why C: Autonomy-limiting. Recruiting a partner to pressure change can increase discord and distort the treatment alliance.

Why D: Incorrect. Sustain talk is information, not proof of resistance or a reason to withdraw care.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When ambivalence is explicit, prefer reflection and evocation over confrontation or persuasion.

Transfer rule: Reflect both sides and let the client voice the argument for change. Remediation: R6

6. Correct Answer: D

Why A: Unsafe. Completion does not take priority over orientation, regulation, and the client's ability to choose.

Why B: Premature and blaming. Observable disorientation requires assessment before an interpretive conclusion.

Why C: Inappropriate. Another person should not replace direct stabilization and assessment of the client.

Why D: Correct. FEEL requires stopping evocation, orienting, restoring choice, and assessing dissociation, safety, and immediate needs.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Shutdown or disorientation moves the item from emotional deepening to regulation and safety.

Transfer rule: Evocation proceeds only within a tolerable, voluntary range. Remediation: R7

7. Correct Answer: A

Why A: Correct. CHANGE begins with functional and relational assessment plus fit before arranging an experiment.

Why B: Blaming. Homework is a collaborative test, not a compliance device assigned to one partner.

Why C: Technique-first reasoning. Availability of a worksheet does not establish its relevance or accessibility.

Why D: Therapist preference is not a clinical mechanism and should not control the intervention.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Before homework, look for an explicit target, functional analysis, relational loop, and feasibility check.

Transfer rule: A behavioral experiment tests a hypothesis and must fit the client system. Remediation: R2/R8

8. Correct Answer: C

Why A: Too individual and potentially invalidating. The appraisal should be examined within the actual relational and contextual feedback loop.

Why B: Likely to reinforce dependence on reassurance and does not create a sustainable alternative interaction.

Why C: Correct. The option links cognition, protective behavior, partner response, reciprocal reinforcement, and an observable experiment.

Why D: Incorrect. Systemic cognitive-behavioral work includes appraisals when connected to interaction and context.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
The systemic answer connects thought, action, other-person response, and feedback.

Transfer rule: Cognition is one part of a reciprocal loop, not an isolated defect. Remediation: R8

9. Correct Answer: B

Why A: Unsafe. Persistence may reflect compliance while dissociation or detachment worsens.

Why B: Correct. PAUSE prioritizes stopping, external orientation, assessment, choice, adaptation, and client feedback.

Why C: Incorrect. Increased detachment is an adverse response to assess, not automatic evidence of therapeutic defusion.

Why D: Unsafe and unsupported. Removing therapist monitoring could increase harm and does not repair consent.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Mindfulness is never automatically correct; adverse response and loss of choice control the next step.

Transfer rule: Offer rationale and alternatives, then adapt or stop when the practice destabilizes. Remediation: R9

10. Correct Answer: A

Why A: Correct. MATCH reconnects hypothesis, target, distributed outcome, alliance, competence, and revision before further intervention.

Why B: Incorrect. Another technique deepens drift and makes response harder to interpret.

Why C: Feedback neglect. Worsening alliance and outcomes require reassessment now, not passive continuation.

Why D: Blaming and unsupported. Multiple attempted methods do not establish that the client is resistant.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When many methods and worsening feedback appear together, the answer is coherent reassessment and revision.

Transfer rule: Integration requires one working hypothesis, explicit functions, and response-based revision. Remediation: R1/R10
