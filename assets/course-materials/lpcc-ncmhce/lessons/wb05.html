How to Use This Workbook

Workbook 5 teaches diagnosis as a disciplined comparison process. Complete the chapters in order. For every case, build a timeline, separate symptoms from episodes, identify impairment and risk, check substance, medication, medical, developmental, cultural, and situational explanations, and compare at least two realistic alternatives before choosing a diagnosis. Do not memorize isolated symptom lists without learning what distinguishes one pattern from another.

BIG IDEA
The best diagnosis is the explanation most fully supported by the whole pattern after reasonable alternatives and exclusions have been considered—not the first label suggested by one dramatic symptom.

Learning Objectives

  • Apply a structured diagnostic method using symptoms, duration, onset, course, severity, impairment, risk, context, exclusions, and corroborating information.
  • Differentiate depressive from bipolar-spectrum presentations by assessing lifetime episodes, energy, activity, sleep, activation, psychosis, and functional change.
  • Differentiate anxiety, panic, phobic, social-anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive presentations using the feared outcome, trigger, thought process, avoidance, and safety behavior.
  • Distinguish trauma- and stressor-related conditions, grief, and adjustment presentations by examining the qualifying event, timing, symptom pattern, impairment, and alternative explanations.
  • Differentiate primary psychotic disorders from mood-related, trauma-related, substance/medication-induced, and medical or neurological explanations while recognizing urgent referral needs.

Why This Topic Matters

The current NCMHCE gives substantial weight to intake, assessment, and diagnosis. Diagnostic questions rarely ask for a definition alone. They present incomplete or evolving information and ask what the counselor should assess next, which diagnosis is best supported, which alternative must still be ruled out, or how new facts change the formulation. Common distractors diagnose from a screening score, confuse a symptom with a disorder, ignore a lifetime history of mood elevation, treat intrusive thoughts as psychosis, or overlook medical, medication, substance, cultural, grief, and developmental explanations.

BLUEPRINT ALIGNMENT
This workbook primarily supports the current NCMHCE Intake, Assessment, and Diagnosis domain and Areas of Clinical Focus. It also supports the July 1, 2027 task of selecting a DSM/ICD diagnosis using all available data and contributes to treatment-planning decisions that follow diagnosis.

1Diagnostic Reasoning, Coding, and Differential Method

Build the diagnosis from the timeline and total pattern, then test it against alternatives.

Diagnosis Is a Working Clinical Conclusion

A diagnosis organizes information, supports treatment planning, facilitates communication, and may be required for reimbursement or service eligibility. It should remain open to revision when new evidence changes the pattern. A responsible counselor distinguishes confirmed facts, client report, observed behavior, screening results, collateral information, clinical inference, and unresolved questions.

The DIFFER Diagnostic Sequence

StepCounselor TaskExam Question
D - Describe the syndromeIdentify the full symptom cluster, central complaint, observable signs, functional effects, and severity.What pattern is actually present—not merely what word appears in the case?
I - Identify onset and courseMap first onset, episode length, recurrence, baseline functioning, developmental context, and relationship to events.Is this episodic, persistent, situational, progressive, or fluctuating?
F - Find impairment and riskClarify work, school, relationship, self-care, judgment, sleep, medical, and safety consequences.Does the pattern cause clinically significant distress or impairment, and is urgent action needed?
F - Filter exclusionsAssess substances, medications, medical and neurological factors, sleep deprivation, bereavement, culture, and normative development.What explanation must be ruled out before assigning a primary mental-disorder diagnosis?
E - Evaluate alternatives and co-occurrenceCompare realistic differential diagnoses and determine whether more than one condition may be present.Which diagnosis best accounts for the total pattern, and what does it fail to explain?
R - Review, record, and reviseDiscuss the formulation in understandable language, document supporting evidence and uncertainty, coordinate care, and revise when needed.What is the most accurate conclusion now, and what evidence would change it?

The Diagnostic Evidence Hierarchy

Evidence TypeValueLimit
Longitudinal timelineOften reveals episode polarity, recurrence, progression, and event relationships.May require collateral records or repeated assessment.

Coding and Specificity

Select the most specific diagnosis and code that the available evidence supports. Do not add a subtype, severity, remission status, psychotic feature, anxious distress feature, or other specifier simply because it sounds plausible. When required information is not yet available, document the uncertainty, gather the missing data, and use the current manual’s guidance rather than inventing precision. A code is not a substitute for a written formulation.

COMMON EXAM TRAP
A diagnosis can be the best-supported answer even when every detail is not known. The exam asks for the best answer from the information provided—not perfect certainty. However, a missing fact that could reverse the diagnosis may make further assessment the best next step.

Chapter 1 Summary

  • Diagnosis is a working conclusion supported by the total pattern, not a label attached to one symptom.
  • A timeline is often the fastest way to distinguish episode-based, persistent, situational, and progressive presentations.

2Depressive and Bipolar Disorders

Before diagnosing unipolar depression, assess the client’s entire lifetime for elevated or activated episodes.

Depressive Presentations: Pattern Before Label

Depressive symptoms may include low or irritable mood, loss of interest or pleasure, sleep or appetite change, low energy, slowed or agitated movement, guilt or worthlessness, concentration difficulty, hopelessness, and suicidal thinking. The counselor must determine the duration, pervasiveness, functional impact, recurrence, relationship to stressors, seasonal or reproductive timing, psychotic features, medical contributors, substance effects, and prior periods of elevated or unusually activated mood.

The MOOD Episode Timeline

CueQuestions
M - Mood polarityAcross the lifetime, have there been distinct periods of depression, elevation, expansiveness, or marked irritability?
O - Observable changeDid others notice a clear departure from the person’s usual behavior, speech, judgment, productivity, or sociability?
O - Onset, duration, and recurrenceHow long did each episode last, how often did it recur, and what was the baseline between episodes?
D - Drive, energy, activity, and sleepWas there increased goal-directed activity, reduced need for sleep, pressured speech, racing thoughts, grandiosity, or risky behavior?

Depressive Versus Bipolar-Spectrum Clues

FindingWhy It Matters
Distinct periods of markedly increased energy or activityMay indicate mania or hypomania rather than unipolar depression.
Reduced need for sleep without fatigueDifferent from insomnia in which the person wants sleep and feels tired.
Grandiosity, pressured speech, racing thoughts, or risky goal-directed behaviorSuggests an elevated or activated episode when clustered and episodic.
Hospitalization, psychosis, or severe impairment during elevationSupports a manic-level episode rather than hypomania.
Depressive episodes plus past activation after an antidepressantRequires careful timeline assessment; medication effects and spontaneous episodes must be distinguished using the current manual.
Family history and early recurrent episodesCan increase suspicion but never establish the diagnosis alone.

Grief, Demoralization, and Depression

Grief and depression can overlap. The counselor examines the pattern rather than assuming that bereavement excludes depression or that sadness after loss is automatically a disorder. Consider whether distress occurs in waves tied to reminders or is pervasive, whether self-esteem is generally preserved or replaced by global worthlessness, whether positive emotion remains possible, whether suicidal thoughts center on reunion or on self-devaluation and hopelessness, and whether the total syndrome and impairment support a depressive disorder. Cultural and spiritual meanings of loss are essential.

Psychotic Features and Mood Congruence

Psychotic symptoms may occur during severe mood episodes. Determine whether hallucinations or delusions occur only during mood episodes or also during sustained periods without prominent mood symptoms. This timing helps differentiate mood disorders with psychotic features from schizoaffective or primary psychotic disorders. Do not infer the diagnosis from the content alone; map psychosis and mood on the same timeline.

CTA EXAM TIP
A client presenting with depression should still be asked about lifetime periods of unusually high energy, reduced need for sleep, increased activity, impulsivity, and observable change. The absence of a spontaneous complaint about “mania” does not mean the history is negative.

Chapter 2 Summary

  • Assess the entire lifetime before concluding that a depressive presentation is unipolar.
  • Reduced need for sleep, increased energy, observable change, and risky goal-directed behavior are high-yield bipolar clues.
  • Depressive symptoms after loss require pattern, impairment, culture, and differential analysis rather than an automatic exclusion or diagnosis.
  • Map psychotic symptoms and mood episodes on the same timeline.
  • Medication, substance, medical, and sleep factors may cause or amplify mood symptoms.
3Anxiety and Obsessive-Compulsive Disorders

Identify what the client fears, what triggers it, and what behavior keeps the cycle going.

Anxiety Is a Family of Patterns

Anxiety may be diffuse and difficult to control, linked to panic attacks, focused on social evaluation, tied to a specific object or situation, organized around separation, or maintained by avoidance and safety behavior. The key exam task is not to notice that the client is anxious; it is to identify the structure of the fear and distinguish anxiety from trauma, OCD, illness anxiety, psychosis, substance effects, and medical conditions.

The FEAR-O Differential Grid

CueQuestions
F - Feared outcomeWhat does the client believe will happen—embarrassment, panic sensations, contamination, harm, separation, illness, trauma recurrence, or something else?
E - Eliciting triggerIs anxiety cued by social scrutiny, a specific object, internal sensations, intrusive thoughts, uncertainty, or a broad range of life domains?
A - Avoidance and accommodationWhat situations, thoughts, sensations, people, or responsibilities are avoided? Who participates in reassurance or rituals?
R - Repetition, duration, and reachIs the pattern episodic or persistent? How much time does it consume and how broadly does it impair functioning?
O - Obsessions, compulsions, and insightAre thoughts intrusive and unwanted? Are repetitive acts performed to reduce distress or prevent a feared event? How convinced is the client?

High-Yield Anxiety Differentials

PatternDistinguishing Focus
Generalized anxietyExcessive anxiety and worry across multiple domains, difficult to control, with associated tension or arousal.
Panic disorderRecurrent unexpected panic attacks plus persistent concern or maladaptive behavior related to future attacks.
AgoraphobiaFear or avoidance of situations in which escape or help may be difficult if symptoms occur.
Social anxietyFear of scrutiny, negative evaluation, embarrassment, rejection, or offending others in social or performance situations.
Specific phobiaMarked fear tied to a circumscribed object or situation.
Trauma-related fearSymptoms organized around a qualifying traumatic exposure, intrusion, avoidance, negative changes, and arousal/reactivity.
Medical/substance anxietyTemporal relationship to a medical condition, medication, stimulant, withdrawal, endocrine change, or other physiological factor.

Obsessions, Compulsions, Worry, and Psychosis

Exam questionWhat function does the repetitive act serve?What domain is being worried about and how broad is it?Is reality testing impaired and what medical/substance factors must be ruled out?
COMMON TRAP
A violent or sexual intrusive thought is not automatically intent, dangerousness, or psychosis. Assess whether it is unwanted, ego-dystonic, resisted, linked to rituals or reassurance, accompanied by intent or planning, and consistent with the person’s values and behavior. Risk assessment is still required when the facts indicate possible intent.

Chapter 3 Summary

  • Differentiate anxiety disorders by feared outcome, trigger, avoidance, time course, and functional impact.
  • Panic attacks can occur in many disorders; panic disorder requires a broader pattern than one attack.
  • OCD is organized around obsessions, compulsions, distress reduction, and impairment—not simply perfectionism or preference.
  • Intrusive thoughts, worry, and delusions differ in function, insight, and reality testing.
  • Medical, medication, substance, and trauma explanations remain part of the differential.
4Trauma, Stressor, Grief, and Adjustment Presentations

Confirm the event, map the response over time, and avoid pathologizing every reaction to adversity.

The TRACE Timeline

CueQuestions
T - Type of event or stressorWas there exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, sexual violence, another severe event, or a non-traumatic psychosocial stressor?
R - Response clustersAre there intrusion, avoidance, negative cognition/mood, arousal/reactivity, dissociation, grief, anxiety, depression, or behavioral symptoms?
A - Aftermath and impairmentHow have safety, relationships, work, school, sleep, identity, beliefs, and functioning changed?
C - Course and clockWhen did symptoms begin, how long have they lasted, and were there delayed, persistent, or fluctuating patterns?
E - Exclusions and explanationsCould the presentation be better explained by grief, depression, panic, OCD, substance use, medical factors, psychosis, culture, or another condition?

PTSD, Acute Stress, and Adjustment Patterns

PatternCore Diagnostic Question
Acute stress response / disorderIs there a qualifying trauma and a broad trauma-related symptom pattern during the early post-trauma period?
Post-traumatic stress disorderDoes a qualifying trauma support the required symptom clusters, duration, distress/impairment, and exclusions?
Adjustment disorderIs the response to an identifiable stressor clinically significant but not better explained by another disorder or normal bereavement?
Depressive or anxiety disorderDo symptoms form an independent syndrome not adequately explained by the stressor-linked pattern?
Grief-related presentationDoes the pattern reflect culturally shaped mourning, prolonged grief, depression, trauma, or a combination requiring careful differentiation?

Trauma Exposure Is Not the Diagnosis

Many people experience distress after trauma without developing PTSD. The counselor must establish the qualifying exposure, symptom clusters, duration, impairment, and exclusions. Exposure may be direct, witnessed, learned about in certain close relationships, or repeated/extreme occupational exposure under the current manual’s conditions. Media exposure alone generally does not qualify unless work-related under the manual’s rules. Use the manual for exact criteria.

Grief and Cultural Context

Grief varies across culture, religion, family, developmental stage, relationship, circumstances of death, and community practices. The counselor considers yearning, preoccupation, identity disruption, disbelief, avoidance, emotional pain, loneliness, meaning, functional impairment, duration, and cultural expectations without assuming that intensity alone is pathological. Grief can coexist with depression, PTSD, substance use, or other conditions.

CTA EXAM TIP
When a case follows a difficult life event, do not automatically select adjustment disorder. First ask whether the symptoms meet criteria for another disorder, whether the stressor is a qualifying trauma, and whether the reaction may be culturally expected grief or distress.

Chapter 4 Summary

  • Trauma exposure is necessary for PTSD but is not sufficient by itself.
  • Map event type, symptom clusters, duration, impairment, and exclusions.
  • Adjustment disorder is not a default diagnosis whenever symptoms follow stress.
  • Grief must be understood in cultural and relational context and may coexist with other disorders.
  • Trauma-related symptoms can overlap with depression, anxiety, OCD, dissociation, psychosis, substance use, and medical conditions.
5Psychotic Disorders and Medical/Medication Differentials

Describe the psychosis precisely, determine its timeline, and rule out urgent causes before settling on a primary disorder.

Psychosis Is a Syndrome, Not a Single Diagnosis

Psychotic symptoms can include delusions, hallucinations, disorganized thinking or speech, grossly disorganized or catatonic behavior, and negative symptoms. Similar presentations may arise in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders, mood disorders, trauma-related conditions, substance or medication effects, delirium, neurocognitive disorders, neurological or endocrine conditions, sleep deprivation, and culturally sanctioned experiences. The counselor must assess the symptom, timing, context, function, and safety rather than diagnosing from the word “voices” or “paranoid.”

The REALITY Differential Check

CueQuestions
R - Reality-testing featuresWhat exactly is perceived or believed? How fixed is it? What evidence is considered? Is thought or speech disorganized?
E - Episode timelineWhen did psychosis begin, how long has it lasted, and how does it relate to mood episodes, trauma, substances, medications, sleep, and medical events?
A - Affect, mood, and activationAre there depressive, manic, mixed, or severe anxiety symptoms? Does psychosis occur outside prominent mood episodes?
L - Level of function and negative symptomsWhat changes occurred in self-care, work, relationships, motivation, emotional expression, speech, and cognition?
I - Intoxication, withdrawal, medication, and illnessWhat substances, prescribed or nonprescribed medications, neurological signs, infection, endocrine factors, seizures, head injury, or cognitive changes are present?
T - Threat, command, and capacityIs there danger to self or others, inability to care for basic needs, command content, impaired judgment, or need for urgent evaluation?
Y - Yield the next actionWhich diagnosis is best supported now, what remains uncertain, and what medical, psychiatric, or coordinated specialty referral is needed?

High-Yield Psychosis Differentials

PatternDiscriminating Evidence
Primary psychotic disorderPsychosis and functional decline follow the required duration/course and are not better explained by mood, substance/medication, or medical conditions.
Mood disorder with psychotic featuresPsychosis occurs during severe mood episodes and does not persist independently of the mood syndrome.
Schizoaffective patternThe longitudinal record includes both a major mood episode and a sustained period of psychosis without a major mood episode, under the current manual’s requirements.
Substance/medication-induced psychosisSymptoms develop in temporal relationship to exposure, intoxication, withdrawal, or medication and are not better explained by an independent disorder.
Psychosis due to another medical conditionHistory, examination, or testing supports a direct physiological relationship to a medical or neurological condition.
Trauma/dissociation or culturally sanctioned experienceThe experience is better explained by trauma processes, dissociation, spiritual/cultural context, or another nonpsychotic formulation after careful assessment.

First-Episode and Medical Urgency

New-onset psychosis, abrupt cognitive change, fluctuating attention, fever, seizure, head injury, severe headache, weakness, altered consciousness, pregnancy/postpartum concern, medication toxicity, or significant substance exposure may require urgent medical evaluation. Early psychosis also benefits from timely specialty coordination. The counselor should not delay necessary medical or psychiatric assessment while attempting to complete every diagnostic detail in outpatient counseling.

BIAS AND CULTURE CHECK
Psychosis diagnoses can be affected by language differences, mistrust rooted in discrimination, unfamiliar spiritual practices, clinician bias, and incomplete context. Use interpreters and cultural consultation when needed, ask what the experience means in the client’s community, and distinguish culturally shared beliefs from idiosyncratic fixed beliefs with impaired functioning.

Chapter 5 Summary

  • Psychosis is a syndrome with multiple possible diagnoses and causes.
  • Map psychosis, mood, trauma, substances, medications, medical events, and functioning on one timeline.
  • The presence or absence of psychosis outside mood episodes is a high-yield differential clue.
  • New or abrupt psychosis with medical or neurological warning signs may require urgent medical evaluation.
  • Use cultural humility, language access, collateral information, and specialty referral to reduce error and bias.

Cumulative Three-Part Case Lab

Case: Priya - Part 1, Intake

CASE INFORMATION
Priya is a 34-year-old pharmacist who seeks counseling for six weeks of low mood, loss of interest, guilt, poor concentration, early-morning awakening, and reduced appetite after a relationship ended. She reports occasional passive thoughts that “it would be easier not to wake up” but denies intent, plan, or preparatory behavior. She describes herself as “always anxious” and says she has had two prior periods of depression. A PHQ-family screener suggests severe depressive symptoms. She asks whether she has major depressive disorder and whether she should request an antidepressant from her primary-care clinician.
PromptStudent Notes
What diagnoses are initially plausible? 
What safety assessment remains necessary? 
What lifetime mood-history questions are essential before a unipolar-depression conclusion? 
What medical, medication, substance, sleep, reproductive, cultural, and grief factors should be assessed? 
What can and cannot be concluded from the screening score? 
PART 1 DEBRIEF
A depressive disorder is plausible, but the screener does not establish the diagnosis. The counselor should complete suicide-risk assessment, assess duration and impairment, review prior episodes and lifetime activation, evaluate grief and relationship context, and coordinate medical evaluation when indicated. A treatment recommendation should not outrun the diagnostic assessment.

Case: Priya - Part 2, New History

NEW INFORMATION
Priya reports that three years ago she slept about three hours nightly for five days without feeling tired. During that period, she felt unusually confident, spoke rapidly, launched an online business, spent $9,000 on inventory, and became intensely social. Her sister said Priya was “not herself.” Priya did not require hospitalization and continued working, though coworkers complained that she was intrusive and disorganized. She later felt embarrassed and depressed. A similar but shorter period occurred in graduate school. She denies substance use during either episode.
PromptStudent Notes
Which new facts most strongly change the differential? 
What additional questions distinguish hypomania, mania, medication/substance effects, and normal productivity? 
How should the counselor revise the working diagnosis? 
What treatment-planning and referral implications follow from the revised formulation? 
How would you explain the uncertainty to Priya without presenting a diagnosis as a verdict? 
PART 2 DEBRIEF
The distinct activated episodes, reduced need for sleep, increased goal-directed behavior, risky spending, pressured speech, observable change, and impairment make a bipolar-spectrum diagnosis more likely than unipolar depression. The counselor must determine episode duration, severity, psychosis, hospitalization, medication/substance factors, and whether the pattern meets the current manual’s requirements. Referral for medication evaluation should communicate the bipolar concern rather than assuming a routine antidepressant pathway.

Case: Priya - Part 3, Perceptual Experience

NEW INFORMATION
Priya later reports that during the five-day activated period she heard her deceased grandmother’s voice once while falling asleep, saying, “You can do this.” She has never heard voices while fully awake and has no fixed delusional beliefs, disorganization outside mood episodes, negative symptoms, neurological changes, or functional decline between episodes. In her family and religious tradition, dreams and sensed contact with deceased relatives are discussed openly.
PromptStudent Notes
Does this fact establish a psychotic disorder? Why or why not? 
What questions clarify sleep-related, cultural/spiritual, grief-related, mood-related, and psychotic explanations? 
Which evidence argues against a primary psychotic disorder? 
What should be documented and monitored? 
What is the best-supported working formulation at this point? 
PART 3 DEBRIEF
One sleep-transition experience with cultural and grief context does not establish a psychotic disorder. The counselor should clarify timing, state of consciousness, meaning, recurrence, conviction, distress, impairment, and relationship to mood. The longitudinal evidence still points primarily toward a bipolar-spectrum formulation, subject to completion of the diagnostic assessment and current DSM-5-TR criteria.

Correct Answer: B

A. Incorrect. Appointment preference does not distinguish unipolar from bipolar depression.

B. Correct. Lifetime activation, reduced need for sleep, increased activity, impulsivity, and observable change are high-yield bipolar clues.

C. Incorrect. Reading about a disorder is not diagnostic evidence.

D. Incorrect. Weather sensitivity may be relevant later but does not replace a lifetime polarity assessment.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Before labeling depression as unipolar, scan the lifetime for distinct elevated or activated episodes.

Question 3 (LPCC-WB05-Q003)

Correct Answer: C

A. Incorrect. Severity alone does not distinguish grief from major depression.

B. Incorrect. A stressor does not automatically make adjustment disorder the best diagnosis.

C. Correct. Grief requires cultural, temporal, functional, depressive, trauma, and safety assessment.

D. Incorrect. Bereavement can coexist with depression, PTSD, prolonged grief, or other disorders.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
After loss, compare grief, depression, trauma, and adjustment patterns instead of using the stressor as a shortcut.

Question 4 (LPCC-WB05-Q004)

Correct Answer: B

A. Incorrect. Attack length does not identify the organizing fear.

B. Correct. Panic disorder centers on unexpected attacks and concern/behavior about recurrence; social anxiety centers on scrutiny and evaluation.

C. Incorrect. Family history can inform risk but is not the key discriminator.

D. Incorrect. Caffeine matters clinically but does not answer the main diagnostic distinction.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Ask what the client fears will happen and in which situations—not merely whether panic symptoms occurred.

Question 5 (LPCC-WB05-Q005)

Correct Answer: B

A. Incorrect. Unwanted, ego-dystonic images with no desire or plan do not by themselves prove intent.

B. Correct. The pattern suggests obsession and compulsion, while actual risk must still be assessed.

C. Incorrect. A feared possibility is not automatically a fixed delusional belief.

D. Incorrect. Reassurance can reinforce OCD cycles and does not replace assessment.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Differentiate thought content from thought function, insight, intent, and the behavior used to neutralize distress.

Question 6 (LPCC-WB05-Q006)

Correct Answer: B

A. Incorrect. Support affects course and treatment but does not establish the diagnosis.

B. Correct. PTSD requires the qualifying event plus the required trauma-related pattern, duration, impairment, and exclusions.

C. Incorrect. Calendar timing alone is insufficient.

D. Incorrect. Prior counseling history does not distinguish PTSD from adjustment disorder.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
For trauma questions, confirm the event and symptom architecture before selecting the diagnosis.

Question 7 (LPCC-WB05-Q007)

Correct Answer: B

A. Incorrect. Shame does not distinguish mood-related from primary psychosis.

B. Correct. Psychosis confined to mood episodes supports a mood disorder with psychotic features.

C. Incorrect. Family history of depression is not the decisive timing evidence.

D. Incorrect. Mood congruence may be informative but does not establish the longitudinal relationship.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Map psychosis and mood on the same timeline. Timing is more diagnostic than the content alone.

Question 8 (LPCC-WB05-Q008)

Correct Answer: C

A. Incorrect. Psychotherapy would delay evaluation of an acute medical syndrome.

B. Incorrect. Abrupt onset, fever, disorientation, fluctuation, and medication change argue against routine outpatient diagnosis first.

C. Correct. Urgent medical evaluation is the priority because delirium, toxicity, infection, or another acute condition is possible.

D. Incorrect. Waiting could be dangerous.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When psychosis is accompanied by acute cognitive or medical warning signs, address medical safety before routine diagnostic refinement.

Question 9 (LPCC-WB05-Q009)

Correct Answer: B

A. Incorrect. A screening score does not override a bipolar-spectrum history.

B. Correct. The score reflects depressive symptom burden, while the interview changes the diagnostic differential.

C. Incorrect. Bipolar disorders can include major depressive episodes.

D. Incorrect. The score may still be useful for severity and monitoring; it is not automatically invalid.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Integrate measures with the interview. New discriminating evidence can change the diagnosis without making the measure useless.

Question 10 (LPCC-WB05-Q010)

Correct Answer: C

A. Incorrect. A single culturally contextualized voice experience does not automatically establish psychosis.

B. Incorrect. Cultural context should be assessed, not used to ignore clinically relevant information.

C. Correct. Clarifying meaning, context, recurrence, reality testing, distress, impairment, and function supports culturally responsive diagnosis.

D. Incorrect. Immediate confrontation can damage alliance and reflects premature pathologizing.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Before diagnosing psychosis, examine cultural meaning and functional context along with reality testing and course.
