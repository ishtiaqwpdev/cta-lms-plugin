CHAPTER 1
Authority Hierarchy, Ethics, Scope, and Competence
BIG IDEA
Start with the exact issue and the controlling authority; then apply ethics, scope, competence, consultation, and client-welfare reasoning.

Legal and ethical problems become confusing when candidates mix different sources of authority. A statute may require an action. An ethics code may guide professional judgment. An agency policy may organize workflow. A supervisor or attorney may clarify application. The counselor must identify which source controls the specific decision and avoid assuming that one source answers every question.

The LAYER Authority Tool

StepQuestion to Ask
L - Locate the exact issueWhat right, duty, risk, or professional standard is actually being tested?
A - Ascertain authorityWhat current law, regulation, board rule, ethics standard, court directive, or policy applies?
Y - Your role and limitsWhat is the counselor authorized and competent to do? Is supervision, consultation, or referral needed?
E - Ethical and clinical effectsHow do autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice, fidelity, and cultural context affect the response?
R - Respond and recordTake the least premature compliant action, communicate clearly, document, and arrange follow-up.

Practical Authority Hierarchy

LevelUse
1. Applicable law and regulationDefines mandatory, permitted, and prohibited conduct.
2. Licensing-board requirementsApplies profession-specific scope, notice, record, telehealth, advertising, supervision, and disciplinary standards.
3. Court order or legal processMay create specific obligations; distinguish an order from a request or subpoena.
4. Ethical standardsGuide professional conduct, conflict resolution, competence, boundaries, technology, and client welfare.
5. Agency and payer policyControls workflow only when consistent with higher authority and clinical obligations.
6. Consultation and professional judgmentClarifies application; it does not transfer the counselor’s responsibility.

High-Yield California LPCC Controls

  • California LPCC scope includes counseling interventions and assessment activities for counseling goals, but excludes certain specialized testing and requires referral when a need exceeds the counselor’s education, training, or experience.
  • A license or registration does not establish competence for every population, modality, assessment, technology, or forensic role.
  • California defines multiple forms of unprofessional conduct, including gross negligence or incompetence, misrepresentation, practice beyond scope, breach of confidentiality, failure to disclose fees, referral compensation, false or misleading advertising, telehealth noncompliance, and willful violation of patient-record access law.
  • Client welfare and truthful representation remain central when marketing, accepting a case, using credentials, supervising, or selecting technology.
COMMON TRAP
“My supervisor told me to” or “agency policy says so” is not enough when a higher legal or ethical duty controls. Consultation improves decision-making but does not erase individual professional responsibility.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY
When authority is unclear, pause before taking an irreversible action. Define the question, preserve safety and records, obtain qualified consultation, and document the basis for the next step.
  • Identify the issue before citing a rule.
  • Use the highest applicable authority.
  • Scope and competence are separate questions.
  • Consultation supports but does not replace judgment.
  • Document the basis for decisions, not merely the outcome.
CHAPTER 2
Consent, Confidentiality, Privilege, Records, and Access
BIG IDEA
Protect information by identifying the client, the right involved, the information requested, the authority to disclose, and the minimum necessary response.

Privacy questions are often missed because candidates treat confidentiality, privilege, authorization, and record access as synonyms. They are related but distinct. The counselor must know who holds the right, what information is involved, why it is requested, and whether disclosure is required, permitted, or prohibited.

The PRIVACY Information-Decision Tool

StepQuestion to Ask
P - PurposeWhy is the information being requested or considered for use?
R - Right and roleWho is the client, patient, privilege holder, representative, requestor, or record custodian?
I - Information typeIs this a treatment record, progress note, psychotherapy note, collateral statement, billing record, test material, or other protected information?
V - Valid authorityIs there a valid authorization, legal exception, mandatory duty, treatment basis, or court order?
A - AmountWhat is the least information necessary and relevant to the purpose?
C - Channel and controlHow will identity, recipient, transmission, storage, and redisclosure risk be managed?
Y - Yield, document, and follow upRespond within required time, document the basis, and track completion.

Do Not Collapse These Concepts

ConceptCore Meaning
ConfidentialityThe professional duty to protect client information and disclose only as authorized or required/permitted by law.
PrivacyThe client’s interest in controlling personal information and the conditions under which it is collected, used, stored, or shared.
PrivilegeAn evidentiary right, generally held by the patient, to prevent disclosure of protected psychotherapist-patient communications in legal proceedings, subject to exceptions.
Authorization / consentPermission meeting applicable requirements for a defined use or disclosure; it should identify scope, recipient, purpose, and limits.
Record accessA patient or authorized representative may have statutory rights to inspect or receive records, subject to defined limits and procedures.
Minimum necessary / relevanceDisclose only what is needed for the lawful purpose; do not automatically release the entire chart.

California Records and Client Notices

  • California LPCCs retain health service records for at least seven years after termination; for a minor, at least seven years after the client reaches age 18.
  • California patient-access law generally provides timely inspection and copy rights, with defined exceptions, formats, timelines, and cost limits. A clinical note is not automatically a separately maintained HIPAA psychotherapy note.
  • Before psychotherapy, California LPCC licensees and registrants provide the required Notice to Clients, including current identifying and license/registration information, and document delivery.
  • Corrections should preserve the original entry, identify the correction or addendum, date it, and explain the new information. Records should never be silently rewritten after a complaint, subpoena, billing review, or adverse event.
COMMON TRAP
A signed release does not automatically make every disclosure appropriate. Confirm identity, validity, purpose, scope, expiration, recipient, and whether a narrower disclosure better protects the client.

CHAPTER 3
Mandated Duties, Third Parties, Subpoenas, and Court Involvement
BIG IDEA A request for information is not the same as legal authority, and a legal duty is not satisfied by internal discussion alone.

The exam distinguishes between mandatory reporting, permitted disclosure, duty to protect, ordinary coordination, subpoenas, court orders, and forensic roles. The first task is to identify the trigger and the deadline. Then act through the correct recipient, document the response, and continue clinically appropriate care.

The TRIGGER Duty-Response Tool

StepQuestion to Ask
T - Type of concernIs this child abuse, elder/dependent-adult abuse, threatened violence, serious danger, legal process, or an ordinary third-party request?
R - Required thresholdWhat facts satisfy the applicable legal or clinical threshold? Do not demand certainty when reasonable suspicion is enough.
I - ImmediacyWhat must happen now, and what deadline applies to follow-up documentation or written reporting?
G - Governing recipientWhich agency, victim, law-enforcement body, court, attorney, or authorized party is legally relevant?
G - Gather only necessary factsClarify enough to act; do not conduct an investigation that delays a required report.
E - Execute and documentComplete the required communication/report, preserve evidence, document facts and actions, and consult as appropriate.
R - Review safety and continuityAddress immediate safety, therapeutic response, notification effects, and follow-up care.

High-Yield Trigger Distinctions

SituationBest Initial Frame
Suspected child abuse or neglectReasonable suspicion triggers the individual reporting duty; internal notification is not a substitute.
Suspected elder or dependent-adult abuseIdentify whether the counselor is a mandated reporter, the abuse type, setting, recipient, and timing requirements.
Serious threat against an identifiable victimAssess the communicated threat and apply California duty-to-protect requirements; consultation should not delay necessary protective action.
Ordinary third-party requestObtain valid authorization or identify another lawful basis before disclosure.
Attorney subpoenaDo not ignore it and do not automatically disclose; protect privilege/confidentiality and obtain qualified guidance.
Court orderRead the scope carefully, verify authenticity, respond narrowly, and seek legal guidance when needed.
Forensic request or testimonyClarify role, competence, informed consent, limits, conflicts, and whether the request changes the treatment relationship.

Subpoena Does Not Equal Automatic Release

  • A subpoena is legal process that requires timely attention, but it may not by itself resolve privilege, confidentiality, authorization, objections, or the permissible scope of disclosure.
  • Do not alter, delete, embellish, or create retrospective records after receiving legal process.
  • Notify the appropriate record custodian, supervisor, risk manager, or legal counsel according to role and setting. Preserve the original request and document actions taken.
  • If disclosure is ultimately authorized or ordered, respond only within the scope of that authority and protect unrelated information.
COMMON TRAP
Reporting to a supervisor, coworker, school administrator, or agency manager does not substitute for a legally required report to the designated agency. Consultation may occur alongside the report, not instead of it.

CHAPTER 4
Telehealth, Electronic Communication, Social Media, AI, and EHRs
BIG IDEA
Technology is not separate from clinical practice; it changes jurisdiction, consent, privacy, access, documentation, and the counselor’s responsibility for oversight.

Technology questions are professional-practice questions. A tool may improve access and efficiency while introducing privacy, bias, security, clinical-fit, documentation, and role risks. The counselor remains responsible for selecting, explaining, monitoring, and documenting technology use.

The TECH Technology-Safety Tool

StepQuestion to Ask
T - Territory and identityWhere are the counselor and client? Is practice authorized in the relevant jurisdiction? Is identity and current location confirmed?
E - Evaluate fit and platformIs this modality clinically appropriate, accessible, secure, reliable, and usable for this client and service?
C - Consent, confidentiality, and contingencyHas the client been informed about telehealth or technology use, privacy limits, communication rules, recordings/transcription, emergencies, and backup plans?
H - Human oversight and harm monitoringDoes the counselor review outputs, detect errors or bias, protect data, maintain boundaries, document decisions, and remain accountable?

Technology Decision Matrix

TechnologyKey Risks and Controls
Telehealth platformJurisdiction, identity/location, consent, privacy, accessibility, emergency contacts, backup plan, and platform security.
Email, text, and portalResponse expectations, emergency limits, identity, device access, message retention, encryption, and documentation.
Social mediaSearches, friend/follow requests, public interaction, testimonials, boundaries, privacy, and false/misleading advertising.
AI transcription or documentationClient notice/consent as applicable, vendor terms, data retention, accuracy, bias, hallucination, human review, and record status.
AI clinical supportNo independent diagnosis or treatment decision; verify sources and outputs, protect PHI, assess bias, and document professional judgment.
Electronic health recordAccess control, audit trails, templates, copy-forward errors, amendments, exports, backups, and role-based permissions.

California Telehealth Core

  • Before delivering care via telehealth, the provider informs the patient about telehealth, obtains verbal or written consent, and documents that consent.
  • Telehealth does not expand scope or eliminate ordinary confidentiality, patient-rights, professional-responsibility, and standard-of-practice duties.
  • At each clinically relevant encounter, confirm the client’s location and emergency resources rather than assuming the client remains where the intake occurred.
  • If the client is located in another jurisdiction, verify whether the counselor is authorized to practice there before continuing nonemergency services.
AI CURRENCY CONTROL
California and national AI requirements are evolving. Teach durable duties: disclose relevant use, protect data, obtain required consent, preserve human review, verify accuracy, monitor bias and harm, avoid unauthorized practice by a tool, and update policies as law and guidance change.
COMMON TRAP
Efficiency is not clinical appropriateness. A platform, transcription tool, chatbot, or generated note is not acceptable merely because the employer purchased it or the output sounds professional.

CHAPTER 5
Billing, Documentation Integrity, Emergency Planning, Continuity, and Risk Management
BIG IDEA A defensible practice is accurate, transparent, prepared, and continuous; it does not create documentation after the fact to justify an unsupported decision.

Professional practice includes the systems around counseling: fees, coding, insurance, recordkeeping, scheduling, emergency coverage, termination, disability access, business continuity, liability protection, and quality improvement. These systems affect client welfare and are exam-relevant because failures often create preventable harm.

The DEFEND Professional-Practice Tool

StepQuestion to Ask
D - Document contemporaneouslyWhat facts, assessment, decision, intervention, response, consultation, and follow-up belong in the record?
E - Emergency and disruption planningHow will urgent messages, absences, disasters, technology failures, hospitalization, or sudden practice closure be handled?
F - Fees, billing, and insuranceWere fees and policies disclosed? Does the claim accurately reflect the service, diagnosis, time, provider, and documentation?
E - Ethical accuracy and auditabilityCan the record, schedule, claim, and communication be reconciled without hidden alteration, cloning, or exaggeration?
N - Next step and continuityWho needs follow-up, referral, transition, termination planning, records transfer, or closure communication?
D - Debrief, update, and protectReview incidents, consultation, competence, policy changes, liability coverage, and system improvements.

Documentation That Teaches the Record

IncludeAvoid
Relevant observations and client reportUnsupported conclusions or stigmatizing labels
Assessment, risk formulation, and clinical reasoningA checklist with no interpretation when interpretation is needed
Intervention and client responseGeneric copied language that did not occur
Consultation, coordination, and legal/ethical actionDefensive blame or irrelevant personal opinions

Billing and Insurance Integrity

  • Disclose fees or the basis for calculating them before treatment begins and explain insurance or out-of-network limits accurately.
  • Bill only for services actually provided, by the actual provider, for the documented time and service type, using accurate diagnosis and coding information.
  • Do not create or intensify a diagnosis solely to obtain reimbursement. When a diagnosis is required, it must be clinically supportable and documented.
  • Do not waive, adjust, or collect fees in a deceptive or discriminatory way. Follow contract, law, and payer requirements while maintaining informed client communication.

Continuity and Emergency Systems

  • Maintain a practice emergency plan for client crises, counselor incapacity, natural disasters, technology outages, records access, and unexpected closure.
  • Plan termination and transfer rather than ending abruptly when clinically active needs remain. Give reasonable notice, referrals, records procedures, and crisis information as appropriate.
  • Review coverage, voicemail, portal, and after-hours statements so clients understand what is and is not monitored and where to seek urgent help.
  • Use professional liability coverage, consultation, incident review, and annual legal/technology updates as risk-management supports rather than substitutes for competent care.
COMMON TRAP
A thorough note does not cure a poor decision, and a short note does not automatically prove negligence. The best documentation accurately shows the facts, reasoning, action, client response, and follow-up that actually occurred.

CLINICAL TAKEAWAY
Risk management is not fear-driven overdocumentation. It is a coordinated practice of accurate care, transparent policies, secure records, ethical billing, emergency preparation, and reliable follow-through.
  • Document what actually occurred.
  • Never backdate or silently alter records.
  • Bill accurately and transparently.
  • Plan for emergencies and service disruption.
  • Protect continuity at termination and transfer.

Cumulative Three-Part Case Lab

CASE INSTRUCTIONS
Read only the information available in each part. Answer the prompts before continuing. Do not add facts or assume that an agency policy, signed form, or technology vendor automatically resolves the professional issue.

Case: Morgan Lee

Morgan is a California LPCC in a small group practice. A new client, Elena, age 34, begins treatment for panic symptoms, insomnia, and work-related conflict. The practice uses telehealth, an electronic health record, automated appointment texts, and an optional AI transcription feature.

Part 1 - Intake, Technology, and Consent

At the first video session, Elena is in California and confirms identity and location. Morgan reviews telehealth, emergency procedures, fees, electronic communication limits, and the Notice to Clients. Midway through the session, Morgan notices that the platform’s AI transcription feature was enabled by default and is sending audio to a third-party service. The consent materials do not mention transcription or AI processing.

  • What is the first professional-practice problem Morgan must address?
  • Which facts should be documented immediately?
  • What must Morgan verify about the vendor, data handling, consent, and the status of any generated transcript?
  • Which response would be premature or insufficient?
MODEL REASONING
Pause the unapproved transcription, protect any captured data, inform Elena transparently, assess whether authorization or consent is required, review vendor/security terms, and decide whether the tool should be disabled or used only after a lawful and clinically appropriate process. Document the event and response without deleting or disguising what occurred.

Part 2 - Mandated Duty and Third-Party Contact

In session three, Elena reports that her sister repeatedly leaves an eight-year-old child alone overnight while intoxicated. Elena provides the child’s name, location, and recent details. She pleads with Morgan not to tell anyone. Later that day, Elena’s employer’s EAP coordinator emails Morgan and asks for “a full update” because the employer is paying for the sessions.

  • What legal category and threshold should Morgan evaluate first?
  • Why does Elena’s request for secrecy not control a mandatory reporting duty?
  • How should Morgan respond to the EAP request?
  • What information belongs in the documentation and follow-up plan?
MODEL REASONING
Evaluate reasonable suspicion and complete the individual mandated-reporting duty without delaying for proof or substituting internal discussion. Explain the limit of confidentiality and address therapeutic impact. Treat the EAP email as a separate third-party request: verify the service agreement and authorization, identify the exact permitted purpose, and disclose only what is lawfully authorized and necessary.

Part 3 - Subpoena, Billing, and Record Integrity

Two months later, Morgan receives a subpoena from an attorney seeking Elena’s complete record. The practice manager says to send everything immediately. An insurer also asks Morgan to change the original diagnostic date and add a more severe diagnosis so prior sessions will be reimbursed. Morgan notices that several AI-generated progress-note summaries contain statements Elena never made.

  • What should Morgan do first with the subpoena?
  • How should Morgan respond to the insurer request?
  • How should the inaccurate AI-generated notes be corrected?
  • What system-level changes should follow?
MODEL REASONING
Preserve the subpoena and records, identify privilege and authorization issues, follow practice procedure, and obtain qualified legal/risk guidance rather than ignoring the subpoena or releasing automatically. Refuse to falsify diagnosis or dates. Correct inaccurate notes transparently through dated amendments or addenda that preserve the original record. Disable or redesign the AI workflow until human review, privacy, consent, and accuracy controls are reliable.

Review the reasoning, not only the correct letter. For each miss, name the controlling authority, the professional task, and the first action that should have occurred.

1. Correct Answer: B

Why B is correct: The counselor must identify whether law, contract, authorization, or another valid basis permits the disclosure and resolve the policy conflict before releasing protected information.

Why A is not best: Record ownership does not erase client confidentiality or disclosure law.

Why C is not best: Selecting one document type without authority is still an unauthorized disclosure.

Why D is not best: A promise does not create legal authority or define the proper scope.

Why B is not best: The concepts overlap but are not identical.

Why C is not best: Both may apply to oral and written information.

Why D is not best: The counselor does not automatically own or waive the patient’s privilege.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Ask what right is being tested, who holds it, and in what setting it operates.

3. Correct Answer: C

Why C is correct: California Business and Professions Code section 4999.75 requires LPCC health service records to be retained for at least seven years from termination for an adult client.

Why A is not best: Two years is shorter than the statutory minimum.

Why B is not best: The retention period is not measured from intake.

Why D is not best: A client request does not automatically eliminate the provider’s retention duty.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
For record questions, identify profession, client age, termination date, and the current statutory minimum.

4. Correct Answer: D

Why D is correct: The reporting duty is individual. A supervisor or internal meeting cannot delay or replace the required report to the designated agency.

Why A is not best: Supervision supports judgment but does not transfer the reporter’s duty.

Why B is not best: Reasonable suspicion, not certainty, is the relevant threshold.

Why C is not best: The counselor should not delay a required report to conduct an investigation.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Mandated-report questions turn on threshold, recipient, and timing. Internal notification is not the report.

5. Correct Answer: C

Why C is correct: A subpoena requires timely attention, preservation, and qualified guidance, but it does not automatically authorize disclosure of the complete record or resolve privilege.

Why A is not best: Automatic release may breach confidentiality and privilege.

Why B is not best: Destruction after legal process is improper and may create additional liability.

Why D is not best: Ignoring legal process is unsafe and may violate legal obligations.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Subpoena: preserve, protect, consult, and respond through the proper process. Court order: read scope and comply narrowly with guidance.

6. Correct Answer: B

Why B is correct: Client location affects jurisdiction and emergency response. The counselor should verify location, assess urgent needs, and determine legal authority before continuing routine care.

Why A is not best: A California license does not automatically authorize practice everywhere the client travels.

Why C is not best: The record should accurately reflect clinically and legally relevant facts.
