How to Use This Workbook

Workbook 6 continues the diagnostic method introduced in Workbook 5. The central task is not memorizing isolated labels. It is deciding whether the client’s presentation is best explained by a primary mental disorder, a substance or medication effect, a developmental pattern, a medical or sleep condition, a personality pattern, or more than one condition at the same time.

BIG IDEA
When symptoms have multiple possible causes, build one timeline, identify the most urgent explanation, and keep co-occurring conditions visible instead of forcing the case into a single label.

Learning Objectives

  • Differentiate substance use disorders, intoxication, withdrawal, substance/medication-induced conditions, and primary mental disorders by using pattern, timing, impairment, and symptom course during abstinence.
  • Differentiate enduring personality patterns from trauma responses, mood episodes, developmental differences, situational reactions, substance effects, and culturally contextualized behavior.
  • Assess neurodevelopmental, intellectual, and neurocognitive concerns using developmental history, cross-setting evidence, collateral information, functional impact, and competence-based referral.
  • Recognize diagnostic and medical-referral issues in eating, sleep, somatic, dissociative, and sexual-functioning presentations.
  • Formulate co-occurring conditions, prioritize safety and medical needs, coordinate care, document medication effects, and select specialized referrals within California LPCC scope.

Why This Topic Matters

Complex cases often contain overlapping symptoms: poor concentration may reflect ADHD, depression, anxiety, sleep deprivation, intoxication, withdrawal, medication effects, or a medical condition. Emotional instability may reflect a personality pattern, trauma, current relationship violence, a mood episode, substance use, or developmental stress. The NCMHCE rewards disciplined sequencing: assess broadly, rule out urgent causes, compare timelines, and revise as the case develops.

1Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders

Establish what was used, when it was used, what happened during use or withdrawal, and whether symptoms persist during meaningful abstinence.

Screening Is the Beginning, Not the Diagnosis

A positive alcohol or drug screen identifies the need for further assessment. It does not, by itself, establish a substance use disorder, severity level, intoxication, withdrawal, or a substance-induced mental disorder. Follow the screen with a detailed pattern assessment: quantity, frequency, route, context, loss of control, craving, role impairment, hazardous use, tolerance, withdrawal, unsuccessful change efforts, and consequences.

The CLEAR Substance Timeline

StepCounselor TaskHigh-Yield Question
C - Current agentsList alcohol, prescribed medications, nonmedical use, cannabis, nicotine, caffeine, supplements, and other substances.What has the client taken, in what amount, by what route, and with what combinations?
L - Last use and patternRecord last use, frequency, escalation, binge pattern, periods of nonuse, and access.When did the pattern change, and what preceded the change?
E - Effects and consequencesMap desired effects, adverse effects, blackouts, accidents, role failures, mood changes, anxiety, psychosis, and sleep disruption.Which symptoms appear during use, intoxication, or the hours/days after use?
A - Abstinence evidenceIdentify the longest meaningful abstinence periods and symptom course during them.What improved, persisted, or emerged when the substance was absent?
R - Risk and referralAssess overdose, withdrawal risk, polysubstance use, pregnancy, medical complications, suicidality, and need for specialty care.Can this client safely remain in outpatient counseling while the substance issue is evaluated?

Four Diagnostic Questions

QuestionWhat It Helps Distinguish
Is there a maladaptive pattern with clinically significant impairment or distress?A substance use disorder from use without sufficient disorder-level evidence.
Are symptoms temporally linked to intoxication, withdrawal, or medication exposure?A substance/medication-induced condition from a primary mental disorder.
Do symptoms persist outside expected intoxication or withdrawal windows?A primary or co-occurring mental disorder from a time-limited substance effect.
Is there dangerous withdrawal, overdose risk, or medical instability?Routine outpatient assessment from urgent medical or higher-level care.
COMMON EXAM TRAP
Do not tell a physiologically dependent client to stop alcohol or sedative use abruptly without medical assessment. Tremor, autonomic symptoms, perceptual disturbance, confusion, or a history of withdrawal seizure can make the correct answer medical evaluation rather than routine counseling.

Behavioral and Process Concerns

The exam may present gambling, gaming, sexual behavior, shopping, exercise, or internet use described as “addictive.” Assess loss of control, preoccupation, consequences, function, and attempts to reduce the behavior. Avoid labeling every frequent or culturally disapproved behavior as an addiction. Use the diagnostic system accurately and describe clinically significant patterns when a formal diagnosis is not supported.

Chapter 1 Summary

  • A screen identifies risk; diagnosis requires a full pattern assessment.
  • One timeline should include use, intoxication, withdrawal, abstinence, psychiatric symptoms, sleep, medications, and functioning.

2Personality Patterns and Disorders

Diagnose the enduring pattern, not the counselor’s frustration with one session, one crisis, or one difficult relationship.

Personality Diagnosis Requires Longitudinal Evidence

A personality disorder is not simply a strong trait, interpersonal conflict, self-harm, anger, dependency, avoidance, or a reaction the counselor finds challenging. The pattern must be enduring, inflexible, culturally contextualized, associated with distress or impairment, and not better explained by an episode, developmental condition, substance effect, medication, medical condition, or current environment.

The PATTERN Longitudinal Check

LetterDiagnostic CheckEvidence to Seek
PPervasiveDoes the pattern appear across relationships, work/school, self-concept, and emotional regulation?
AAcross contextsIs it present in multiple settings rather than only with one person or during one role?
TTime-stableHas it persisted over years rather than emerging only during a recent episode or crisis?
TTraceable onsetIs there evidence by adolescence or early adulthood, while accounting for development and context?
EExclusions testedCould trauma, mood, psychosis, neurodevelopment, substances, medications, sleep, or medical factors explain it better?
RRelational/functional impactHow does the pattern affect intimacy, work, safety, decision-making, and treatment engagement?
NNorms and cultureIs the behavior outside the client’s cultural, community, identity, and situational context?

Trait Versus State

State-Like PresentationTrait-Like Presentation
Begins with a mood episode, trauma, medical event, medication change, substance pattern, or acute relationship crisis.Predates the current crisis and recurs across many contexts over a long period.
Improves substantially when the episode, environment, or substance effect resolves.Remains relatively stable, although intensity can change with stress.
Behavior is uncharacteristic compared with the client’s baseline.Behavior reflects a familiar pattern in self-concept, affect regulation, impulse control, and relationships.
Collateral sources describe a clear change from prior functioning.Collateral sources describe a longstanding pattern with repeated consequences.
CTA EXAM TIP
When a question asks for the FIRST diagnostic action, seek a longitudinal baseline before assigning a personality diagnosis. “Since when, in which settings, and outside which episodes?” often separates the best answer from a premature label.

  • Personality diagnosis requires enduring, pervasive, inflexible patterns and meaningful impairment.
  • One crisis, one relationship, or one emotionally intense session is insufficient.

3Neurodevelopmental, Intellectual, and Neurocognitive Concerns

Developmental conditions begin early; neurocognitive conditions represent decline. Both require careful history, function, collateral information, and scope-aware referral.

The DEVELOP Evidence Matrix

LetterAssessment TaskWhat the Counselor Is Testing
DDevelopmental onsetWere relevant features present in childhood, even if they were masked or not recognized?
EEvidence across settingsDo symptoms appear in more than one setting and create functional impairment?
VVariability and maskingHow do structure, interest, sensory demand, gendered expectations, culture, and coping strategies change visibility?
EExclusions and overlapCould sleep, anxiety, depression, trauma, substance use, medication, learning, or medical factors better explain the symptoms?
LLearning and adaptive functionWhat are the client’s academic, occupational, communication, daily-living, and independent-functioning strengths and needs?
OObservation and collateralWhat do records, family, partners, teachers, or prior evaluators show, with appropriate consent?
PProfessional scope and referralWhich questions can be addressed through counseling assessment, and which require specialized psychological, neuropsychological, medical, or educational evaluation?

Adult ADHD: High-Yield Diagnostic Logic

Current concentration problems do not prove ADHD. Look for childhood onset, persistence, impairment in at least two settings, and corroborating history. Compare ADHD with anxiety, depression, trauma, sleep deprivation, substance use, medication effects, thyroid or other medical conditions, and environmental overload. Rating scales support assessment but do not substitute for the clinical evaluation.

Autism Across the Lifespan

Adult autism evaluation focuses on developmental social-communication patterns, restricted or repetitive behavior/interests, sensory experiences, functional impact, and history from early development. Anxiety, ADHD, trauma, personality assumptions, and cultural communication differences can overlap. A client may have learned to mask or compensate; absence of an earlier diagnosis does not establish late onset.

Intellectual, Learning, and Neurocognitive Concerns

ConcernCore QuestionReferral Signal
Intellectual functioningAre there longstanding limitations in intellectual and adaptive functioning beginning during development?Individual intelligence testing and formal diagnostic clarification require appropriately qualified referral.
Specific learning concernIs there a persistent, circumscribed academic skill difficulty despite opportunity and support?Educational/psychological testing may be needed to distinguish skill disorder from attention, language, sensory, or instruction factors.
Neurocognitive declineIs there a documented decline from prior ability in memory, executive function, language, attention, perception, or social cognition?New, progressive, fluctuating, or functionally significant decline warrants medical and often neuropsychological evaluation.
Delirium/acute confusionIs attention or awareness acutely disturbed or fluctuating?Urgent medical evaluation; do not treat as routine dementia, psychosis, or anxiety.
CALIFORNIA SCOPE SAFEGUARD
California LPCC assessment does not include projective personality techniques, individually administered intelligence tests, neuropsychological testing, or a battery of three or more tests to determine psychosis, dementia, amnesia, cognitive impairment, or criminal behavior. Counselors assess within competence and refer when the question requires excluded or specialized testing.

  • Developmental onset and cross-setting impairment are central to ADHD and autism reasoning.
  • Late recognition is not the same as late onset; masking, structure, and cultural context affect visibility.

4Eating, Sleep, Somatic, Dissociative, and Sexual-Functioning Concerns

Do not assume these concerns are “only psychological.” Assess the behavioral pattern, distress and impairment, medical risk, medications, culture, identity, trauma, and the need for coordinated care.

Eating Presentations: Behavior and Medical Risk

PatternHigh-Yield EvidenceImmediate Concern
RestrictionIntake restriction, fear/avoidance, weight/shape meaning or sensory/fear-based avoidance, exercise, weight trajectory, developmental context.Syncope, chest pain, severe weakness, dehydration, abnormal vital signs, rapid change, or inability to maintain intake.
Binge eatingRecurrent loss-of-control episodes, amount/context, distress, secrecy, triggers, and functional impact.Metabolic and gastrointestinal concerns, suicidality, severe shame, or rapid deterioration.
Compensatory behaviorVomiting, laxatives/diuretics, fasting, excessive exercise, medication misuse, frequency, and medical consequences.Electrolyte/cardiac risk, blood in vomit, fainting, confusion, or severe dehydration.

CLINICAL PEARL
A person can be medically unstable at any body size. Appearance is not a reliable severity measure. Medical assessment and nutritional coordination may be necessary even when the client does not appear underweight.

Sleep: Symptom, Cause, or Disorder?

QuestionDiagnostic Value
What is the actual sleep opportunity and schedule?Distinguishes insufficient sleep or circadian mismatch from inability to sleep despite adequate opportunity.
Is there loud snoring, gasping, witnessed apnea, restless legs, unusual movements, or extreme daytime sleepiness?Signals possible sleep-related breathing, movement, or hypersomnolence disorders requiring medical/sleep evaluation.
Did sleep change with mood activation, trauma, substances, medications, pain, or medical illness?Tests whether sleep is secondary to another condition or contributes to it.
Does the client feel rested after reduced sleep?Reduced need for sleep without fatigue differs from insomnia with exhaustion.
What do sleep diary or collateral data show?Improves accuracy and identifies schedule, variability, and environmental contributors.

Somatic Symptom and Illness-Anxiety Reasoning

The client’s physical symptoms are real experiences whether or not a medical explanation has been found. The diagnostic question concerns the pattern of thoughts, anxiety, behaviors, impairment, and health-care use—not whether the counselor believes the symptom is “all in the head.” Coordinate appropriate medical care, avoid repeated reassurance cycles, and do not use a somatic diagnosis to dismiss new red flags.

Dissociation Versus Psychosis, Neurological Events, and Substance Effects

FeatureDissociative PossibilityAlternative to Assess

Memory gapsAmnesia inconsistent with ordinary forgetting, often linked to trauma or identity-state shifts.Substances, seizures, head injury, sleep, malingering/forensic context, neurocognitive condition.
Identity disruptionDiscontinuity in sense of self, agency, behavior, memory, perception, or affect.Cultural/spiritual experiences, trauma, psychosis, personality pattern, neurological condition.

Sexual-Functioning Concerns

Assess onset, context, distress, relationship factors, trauma, pain, medical conditions, hormonal factors, medications, substances, cultural and religious meaning, sexual orientation, gender identity, and consent. Do not pathologize consensual behavior, identity, low or variable desire without distress, or culturally different norms. Medical or specialty referral may be necessary when pain, sudden change, endocrine concerns, medication effects, or complex trauma are present.

Chapter 4 Summary

  • Eating-disorder severity cannot be judged by appearance or body size.
  • Sleep complaints require schedule, opportunity, medical, substance, medication, mood, trauma, and breathing/movement assessment.

5Medical Factors, Medication Effects, Co-Occurrence, and Specialized Referral

The best formulation may contain more than one diagnosis, a medical condition, medication effects, environmental stressors, and a clear statement of what still requires evaluation.

The INTEGRATE Formulation Sequence

StepCounselor Action
I - Immediate needsAddress safety, overdose, withdrawal, medical instability, delirium, severe malnutrition, acute neurological change, and urgent level of care first.
N - Name plausible conditionsList primary, substance-induced, developmental, medical, sleep, personality, trauma, and contextual explanations supported by the case.
T - Timeline relationshipsCompare onset, escalation, remission, abstinence, medication changes, medical events, developmental history, and functional decline.
E - Evidence for and againstUse symptoms, duration, impairment, observations, collateral, screening, records, and response to prior interventions.
G - Generate integrated formulationExplain how conditions interact rather than forcing one condition to explain everything.
R - Referral and role clarityIdentify who will evaluate medical, psychiatric, addiction, nutrition, sleep, psychological, neuropsychological, or educational questions.
A - Address medication informationDocument the client’s report of benefits, side effects, adherence, access barriers, and concerns; coordinate with the prescriber rather than directing medication changes.
T - Treatment sequenceMatch urgency, readiness, level of care, and coordination plan to the client’s needs.
E - Evaluate and reviseMonitor outcomes, symptoms, functioning, substance pattern, sleep, medical status, and new information; update the formulation.

Co-Occurring Does Not Mean One Condition Is Secondary

A client may simultaneously meet criteria for a substance use disorder and a mental disorder. The clinical task is to screen for both, identify immediate risk, determine what can be addressed in the current setting, and coordinate integrated or closely linked care. SAMHSA’s “no wrong door” principle means that a client should be identified, assessed, and connected to appropriate care regardless of which service system they enter first.

Medical and Medication Differential Signals

SignalExamples of Counselor Response
Abrupt onset, fluctuating attention, disorientation, fever, severe headache, new neurological signsTreat as possible medical emergency; arrange urgent evaluation rather than routine psychotherapy.
New symptoms after medication start, dose change, discontinuation, interaction, or nonadherenceDocument timing and client-reported effects; coordinate promptly with prescriber or medical provider.
Progressive cognitive or functional declineGather baseline/collateral information and refer for medical and specialized cognitive evaluation.
Endocrine, cardiac, respiratory, pain, infectious, nutritional, reproductive, or neurological cluesAvoid psychiatric closure; coordinate medical assessment while addressing emotional and behavioral impact.
Multiple conditions and providers with conflicting plansClarify releases, roles, goals, medication list, risk plan, and communication method.
SCOPE AND COMPETENCE RULE
A California LPCC may conduct counseling assessments within competence, but must refer when the client’s needs or the required testing exceed education, training, experience, or statutory assessment scope. Diagnosis does not authorize medical treatment, medication management, neuropsychological testing, or individually administered intelligence testing.

Chapter 5 Summary

  • Co-occurring conditions require integrated formulation rather than forced either/or reasoning.
  • Safety, withdrawal, medical instability, and acute cognitive change are prioritized before routine diagnostic refinement.
  • Medication effects are assessed and documented; medication changes are coordinated with qualified prescribers.
  • Referral should identify the exact clinical question and the professional needed to answer it.
  • Formulations remain provisional and should be revised as abstinence, treatment response, medical data, collateral, and functioning change.

Cumulative Three-Part Case Lab

HOW TO USE THE CASE
Read only one part at a time. Record your working formulation, evidence, urgency, and next action before revealing the next section. Notice which early impressions must be revised.

Case: Riley - Part 1, Intake

Riley Chen is a 31-year-old nonbinary respiratory therapist who seeks counseling for “anxiety, insomnia, and losing focus.” Riley reports drinking five to seven alcoholic drinks most evenings, using cannabis nightly, consuming high-dose energy drinks during shifts, and taking a coworker’s stimulant tablets “a few times a month” to finish documentation. They wake with sweating and hand tremors that improve after drinking. Two years ago, Riley had a seizure after trying to stop alcohol for several days but did not receive follow-up care. Riley denies current suicidal intent but reports increasing shame, missed shifts, and a recent driving incident.

Your TaskNotes
What information belongs on the CLEAR timeline? 
What is the most urgent risk? 
Which answer would be premature: routine anxiety treatment, abrupt alcohol cessation advice, or urgent medical/addiction evaluation? Why? 
What conditions remain plausible after the immediate risk is addressed? 
PART 1 DEBRIEF
The immediate priority is medical/addiction evaluation for potentially dangerous alcohol withdrawal, especially given current withdrawal symptoms and a prior seizure. Anxiety, sleep, attention, cannabis/stimulant effects, and a possible substance use disorder remain important, but routine outpatient psychotherapy is not the first response to the withdrawal risk.

Case: Riley - Part 2, After Medical Stabilization

After medically supervised withdrawal and three weeks without alcohol or nonprescribed stimulants, Riley’s tremor resolves and sleep improves to six hours. Anxiety decreases, but they continue to miss details, lose items, underestimate time, and start many tasks without finishing. Riley’s parent reports similar patterns beginning in elementary school and occurring at home, school, and work. Riley also describes lifelong sensory sensitivity, rehearsing conversations, intense interests, and exhaustion after social interaction. Their school records show strong grades in preferred subjects and repeated comments about disorganization. Riley asks whether this is ADHD, autism, “a personality disorder,” or damage from substance use.

Your TaskNotes
Which DEVELOP elements are now supported? 
What evidence supports ADHD, autism, both, or neither? 
Which symptoms may still reflect recovery, sleep debt, anxiety, or substance effects? 
What assessment questions are within LPCC counseling practice, and what specialized evaluation may be needed? 
PART 2 DEBRIEF
The developmental, cross-setting attention history supports further ADHD evaluation. Lifelong social-communication, sensory, and restricted-interest features support further autism evaluation. These can co-occur. Three weeks of abstinence does not answer every differential question, so continued monitoring and specialty assessment are appropriate. California scope limits must guide any formal cognitive, intelligence, or neuropsychological testing.

Case: Riley - Part 3, New Eating and Relationship Information

Riley later reports recurrent binge eating followed by self-induced vomiting, increasing dizziness, palpitations, and one fainting episode. They also describe intense fear of abandonment and impulsive messages during the past six months in a relationship marked by coercion and threats. Prior partners and family do not describe the same pattern. Riley takes prescribed sertraline and recently doubled the dose without consulting the prescriber because “more should work faster.”

Your TaskNotes
What requires immediate medical or prescriber coordination? 
Why would a personality diagnosis be premature? 
Which conditions or contextual factors belong in the integrated formulation? 
Write the next-step sequence using INTEGRATE. 
PART 3 DEBRIEF
Fainting, palpitations, vomiting, and possible electrolyte disturbance require prompt medical assessment. The unsupervised medication change requires prescriber coordination. The abandonment-related behavior is recent and context-bound to coercive relationship stress, so personality diagnosis is premature. The integrated plan addresses eating behavior, medical risk, relationship safety, substance recovery, developmental assessment, sleep, anxiety, medication use, and coordinated care.

Correct Answer: C

A. Incorrect. Independent taper advice is outside safe routine counseling when severe withdrawal risk is present.

B. Incorrect. Relaxation skills do not address potentially life-threatening withdrawal.

C. Correct. Current autonomic symptoms plus a prior withdrawal seizure require urgent medical/addiction evaluation.

D. Incorrect. Unsupervised abstinence to “observe” symptoms could provoke dangerous withdrawal.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Medical and withdrawal danger outrank routine counseling. Prior seizure is a decisive urgency clue.

Question 3 (LPCC-WB06-Q003)

Correct Answer: A

A. Correct. Repeated onset during stimulant binges and resolution during sustained abstinence strongly support a substance-induced explanation.

B. Incorrect. Embarrassment does not distinguish primary from substance-induced psychosis.

C. Incorrect. Family depression history is not the key temporal evidence.

D. Incorrect. Insomnia occurs in both substance-related and primary psychiatric conditions.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Use the timeline. The strongest differential clue is the consistent relationship between exposure and symptom remission.

Question 4 (LPCC-WB06-Q004)

Correct Answer: D

A. Incorrect. One breakup does not establish a pervasive, enduring personality pattern.

B. Incorrect. Behavior confined to a mood episode may be state-dependent.

C. Incorrect. Intoxication-linked conflict may be better explained by substance effects.

D. Correct. Longstanding, inflexible, cross-setting impairment with exclusions considered is the appropriate personality-disorder pattern.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Personality questions require years, settings, impairment, and exclusions—not severity alone.

Question 5 (LPCC-WB06-Q005)

Correct Answer: B

A. Incorrect. Severity does not replace longitudinal evidence.

B. Correct. The current substance pattern and partner violence can create state-like behavior; baseline and sobriety evidence are needed.

C. Incorrect. Alcohol may contribute but should not automatically explain all symptoms.

D. Incorrect. Neutral documentation of clinically relevant behavior is appropriate and necessary.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When behavior is recent and context-bound, assess the baseline before assigning a trait diagnosis.

Question 6 (LPCC-WB06-Q006)

Correct Answer: C

A. Incorrect. Current symptoms and one rating scale are insufficient.

B. Incorrect. Concentration difficulty limited to depression may be secondary to the mood episode.

C. Correct. Developmental onset, multiple settings, corroboration, impairment, and rule-outs provide the strongest ADHD evidence.

D. Incorrect. Caffeine response is nonspecific and not diagnostic.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
For neurodevelopmental conditions, prioritize childhood onset and cross-setting evidence.

Question 7 (LPCC-WB06-Q007)

Correct Answer: A

A. Correct. Developmental history, collateral evidence, masking, function, and an experienced evaluator support accurate adult autism assessment.

B. Incorrect. Social exhaustion and masking do not establish avoidant personality disorder.

C. Incorrect. Employment does not rule out autism.

D. Incorrect. California LPCC scope excludes individually administered intelligence testing, and IQ testing does not confirm autism by itself.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Do not use functioning stereotypes to rule out developmental conditions. Choose scope-aware developmental assessment and referral.

Question 8 (LPCC-WB06-Q008)

Correct Answer: D

A. Incorrect. Origins can be explored after immediate medical risk is addressed.

B. Incorrect. Precise subtype assignment is not the first priority in a potentially unstable presentation.

C. Incorrect. Waiting may be unsafe.

D. Correct. Vomiting, fainting, palpitations, and severe restriction can signal urgent medical complications and require coordinated specialty care.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Medical instability outranks diagnostic precision. Look for fainting, cardiac symptoms, dehydration, and severe restriction.

Question 9 (LPCC-WB06-Q009)

Correct Answer: B

A. Incorrect. Anxiety may coexist, but snoring, gasping, headaches, and severe sleepiness point to a medical sleep differential.

B. Correct. The pattern warrants medical or sleep evaluation for a possible breathing-related disorder.

C. Incorrect. Behavioral sleep recommendations should not precede assessment of a potentially serious breathing disorder.

D. Incorrect. Daytime sleepiness alone does not establish a hypersomnolence diagnosis.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Sleep questions often test whether the counselor recognizes breathing, movement, or neurological referral signals.

Question 10 (LPCC-WB06-Q010)

Correct Answer: C

A. Incorrect. Delaying all depression care until abstinence can miss risk and reduce engagement.

B. Incorrect. Treating only one condition ignores co-occurrence and medication/medical needs.

C. Correct. Integrated assessment, safety prioritization, coordination, and monitoring address the whole presentation.

D. Incorrect. Referral may be part of care, but co-occurrence does not automatically require abandoning the client.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Choose “both/and” integrated care when the case supports multiple conditions, while keeping safety and role clarity first.
