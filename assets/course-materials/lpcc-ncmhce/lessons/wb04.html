How to Use This Workbook

Workbook 4 teaches the decision sequence used when facts may not safely wait. Complete the chapters in order. For each scenario, identify the immediate danger, the information still needed, the legal or ethical duty that may be activated, the least restrictive option that is actually safe, and the documentation needed to support the decision. Crisis questions reward disciplined sequencing—not dramatic action without assessment and not prolonged assessment while danger escalates.

BIG IDEA
In a crisis, the best answer protects life and dignity by doing the next necessary task—not the most extreme task and not the most comfortable task.

Learning Objectives

  • Apply a structured crisis sequence that prioritizes immediate safety, de-escalation, focused assessment, collaboration, and timely escalation.
  • Differentiate suicide screening, comprehensive risk assessment, risk formulation, safety planning, and disposition decisions.
  • Recognize California duties involving serious threats, child abuse or neglect, and elder or dependent-adult abuse without delaying required action to investigate.
  • Distinguish trauma-related distress from psychosis, mania, intoxication, withdrawal, cognitive change, and medical or neurological urgency.
  • Select and justify an appropriate level of care, consultation pathway, safety plan, crisis resource, and documentation response.

Why This Topic Matters

The NCMHCE often embeds crisis facts inside ordinary counseling cases. The challenge is deciding when the case has crossed from routine exploration to urgent assessment, when a positive screen requires a fuller evaluation, when a duty must be activated, and when outpatient care is no longer sufficient. Common distractors minimize danger, overreact without gathering discriminating facts, substitute a form for clinical judgment, or confuse a safety plan with a guarantee.

1Crisis Sequence, Stabilization, and De-Escalation

Slow the interaction enough to think clearly while moving fast enough to protect life.

The STABLE Crisis Sequence

STABLE organizes the first minutes of a crisis encounter. It is not a substitute for setting-specific protocol. When active violence, overdose, severe medical compromise, or an immediately life-threatening act is occurring, emergency response may begin before a complete interview.

StepCounselor TaskExam Question
S - Secure immediate safetyIdentify active violence, weapons, overdose, medical instability, inability to remain safely connected, or environmental danger.What cannot safely wait?
T - Tune in and lower arousalUse calm tone, simple language, nonjudgmental reflection, choice where possible, and reduced stimulation.What will help the client stay engaged long enough to assess?
A - Ask focused crisis questionsClarify precipitant, current thoughts and behavior, intent, plan, means, targets, psychosis, substance use, medical concerns, abuse, supports, and capacity.Which missing fact changes the next action?
B - Build collaborationAcknowledge distress, state concern transparently, identify reasons for living, supports, preferences, and barriers.How can safety be increased without false reassurance?
L - Link to the needed responseUse supports, crisis lines, mobile crisis, urgent evaluation, emergency services, medical care, or a higher level of care as indicated.What is the safest proportionate disposition?
E - Explain, execute, and evaluateState the plan, confirm handoff, document facts and rationale, and arrange follow-up or reassessment.How will the counselor know the plan actually occurred?

De-Escalation Is an Intervention and an Assessment

De-escalation is not persuasion to “calm down.” It reduces threat and cognitive load so the counselor can obtain usable information and the client can participate in decisions. The response should be culturally responsive and attentive to disability, language, trauma history, power, policing concerns, and prior harmful system experiences.

Helpful ResponseWhy It HelpsCommon Trap
Use brief, concrete statementsHigh arousal reduces working memory and processing capacity.Giving a long lecture or many choices.
Name observable facts and concernTransparency reduces surprise and hidden power.Using labels such as “manipulative” or “noncompliant.”
Offer bounded choicesChoice supports dignity while preserving safety limits.Offering choices that are not actually available.
Reduce stimulation and audiencePrivacy and lower stimulation may reduce escalation.Ignoring staff safety or separating from needed support.
Ask directly about dangerDirect questions clarify risk and do not create suicidal or violent thoughts.Avoiding direct language because it feels uncomfortable.
CTA EXAM TIP
When two answers are clinically reasonable, prefer the option that addresses the immediate safety cue while preserving enough alliance and information to choose the next level of response.
COMMON TRAP
Do not confuse “least restrictive” with “least active.” The least restrictive option must still be capable of managing the identified risk.

2Suicide, Self-Injury, Homicide, and Lethal Means

Ask directly, formulate risk from the whole pattern, and match the response to acuity.

Screening, Assessment, Formulation, and Disposition Are Different Tasks

TaskPurposeWhat It Does Not Do Alone
ScreeningIdentify who needs further suicide-risk evaluation.It does not independently establish current acuity or disposition.
Focused assessmentClarify ideation, intent, plan, preparation, behavior, means, modifiers, and protective factors.It does not replace clinical judgment or environmental facts.
Risk formulationSynthesize acute and chronic risk, protective factors, reliability, and foreseeable changes.It is not a permanent label such as “low risk.”
DispositionChoose the safest feasible response and level of care.It is not determined by a score alone.
Ongoing managementReduce risk through safety planning, treatment, means safety, support, and follow-up.It is not completed by a “no-harm contract.”

The IDEA-M Risk Formulation

ElementQuestions to Clarify
I - Ideation and imageryWhat thoughts, wishes, images, targets, commands, or rehearsals are occurring? How often, how long, and how controllable?
D - Desire, intent, and deterrentsDoes the person want the act to occur? What has stopped them? What could increase intent?
E - Evidence of plan, preparation, and behaviorIs there a method, time, place, rehearsal, note, giving away possessions, stalking, weapon preparation, or prior attempt/assault?
A - Access to means and opportunityWhat lethal means or target access exists now? Can access be reduced collaboratively and promptly?
M - Modifiers and management factorsConsider intoxication, withdrawal, agitation, psychosis, mania, insomnia, recent loss, pain, shame, isolation, supports, willingness, capacity, and follow-up reliability.

Suicide and Nonsuicidal Self-Injury

Nonsuicidal self-injury may function to regulate affect, interrupt dissociation, communicate distress, or punish the self. It is not automatically a suicide attempt, but it is clinically significant and can coexist with suicidal intent. Assess the function, method, medical severity, frequency, escalation, suicidal thoughts, access to means, substance use, and ability to use alternatives.

Homicide and California Duty to Protect

California Civil Code section 43.92 addresses a psychotherapist’s duty to protect when a patient communicates a serious threat of physical violence against a reasonably identifiable victim or victims. The statute provides protection from monetary liability when reasonable efforts are made to communicate the threat to the victim or victims and to law enforcement. Clinical action may also include emergency evaluation, consultation, documentation, and other steps required by the facts and setting.

EXAM DISTINCTION
Anger, fantasy, vague hostility, and a serious communicated threat are not interchangeable. Assess specificity, intent, target, means, preparation, history, psychosis, intoxication, and immediacy before deciding which duty or crisis response is activated.

Lethal-Means Safety

Lethal-means counseling is a collaborative effort to reduce access during periods of elevated risk. The plan should be specific, feasible, temporary when appropriate, and verified. It may involve trusted supports, secure storage, off-site storage consistent with law, medication quantity changes coordinated with prescribers, or removal from an unsafe environment. A vague promise to “stay safe” is not enough.

Strong PlanWeak Plan
Names the exact means and current location.Says only “avoid weapons.”
Identifies who will act, when, and how access will be reduced.Relies on the client’s promise without a concrete step.
Checks legal, relational, and practical barriers.Assumes a support person is willing and able.
Includes reassessment and a backup plan.Treats one conversation as permanent protection.

Chapter 2 Summary

  • A positive screen requires further assessment; a score does not choose the level of care.
  • Assess ideation, intent, plan, preparation, behavior, access, modifiers, and protective factors as a pattern.
  • Nonsuicidal self-injury and suicidal behavior require distinct but overlapping assessment.
  • Serious threats toward identifiable victims require careful California duty-to-protect analysis and timely action.
  • Lethal-means safety must be concrete, collaborative, feasible, and verified.
3Abuse, Violence, Exploitation, and Mandated Duties

Protect first, identify the governing duty, report at the legal threshold, and continue clinical care.

The DUTY Decision Map

StepDecision
D - Determine the protected categoryChild; elder; dependent adult; identifiable threatened victim; client or other person in immediate danger; or another legally defined category.
U - Urgency and immediate safetyAddress active injury, current perpetrator access, medical needs, weapons, safe shelter, emergency transport, and child or vulnerable-adult location.
T - Threshold and timingApply the correct legal standard—such as reasonable suspicion, known/suspected abuse, or serious communicated threat—without demanding proof.
Y - Yield the required actionMake the report or protective communication to the correct recipient, share what is legally required, document, consult as appropriate, and continue support.

Child Abuse and Neglect

Under California Penal Code section 11166, a mandated reporter must report when, in a professional capacity or within the scope of employment, the reporter knows or reasonably suspects that a child has been abused or neglected. The initial report is made by telephone immediately or as soon as practicably possible, followed by a written, faxed, or electronically transmitted report within 36 hours. Reasonable suspicion does not require certainty, proof, or a specific medical indication.

COMMON TRAP
Do not investigate, confront the alleged perpetrator, require the child to repeat details unnecessarily, or wait for supervision before meeting an individual reporting duty. Consultation may support the clinician, but it does not replace a required report.

Elder and Dependent-Adult Abuse

California Welfare and Institutions Code section 15630 identifies mandated reporters and reporting duties for known, suspected, or alleged physical abuse, abandonment, abduction, isolation, financial abuse, or neglect of an elder or dependent adult. Reports are generally made immediately or as soon as practicably possible; when made by telephone, a written or authorized internet report generally follows within two working days. The correct destination depends on the setting and type of facility.

Adult Intimate-Partner Violence, Sexual Violence, and Exploitation

Do not assume that every disclosure by a competent adult automatically creates the same mandated-reporting duty. First assess immediate danger, injuries and medical needs, children or vulnerable adults who may be exposed, coercion, trafficking or exploitation, target-specific threats, capacity, and the client’s choices. Apply any relevant law, employer policy, and professional duty while avoiding unnecessary disclosure. Safety planning should account for surveillance, financial control, immigration concerns, disability, language, housing, pets, technology, and retaliation risk.

Clinical PriorityExamples
Immediate safetyCurrent perpetrator access, strangulation history, weapons, stalking, escalating threats, safe communication, emergency shelter.
Protected personsChildren, elders, dependent adults, or identifiable threatened victims.
Client autonomyOffer choices and resources when the law does not require overriding the competent adult’s decision.
Minimum necessary disclosureShare information required for the report or protective action; do not disclose unrelated therapy content.
Continuity after reportingExplain the process, anticipate reactions, repair alliance when possible, and update the treatment and safety plan.

Chapter 3 Summary

  • Identify the governing duty before acting; different categories use different thresholds and recipients.
  • Reasonable suspicion is not proof, and mandated reporters do not conduct the official investigation.
  • Child-abuse reporting duties are individual and cannot be transferred to a supervisor.
  • Adult violence disclosures require safety assessment and careful legal analysis rather than automatic assumptions.
  • Reporting is not the end of treatment; prepare for alliance, retaliation, housing, and follow-up needs.
4Trauma, Psychosis, Mania, Substance, and Medical Urgency

Do not let a familiar psychiatric explanation hide an acute medical or substance-related danger.

Trauma-Informed Crisis Assessment

Trauma-informed care emphasizes safety, transparency, collaboration, choice, empowerment, peer and community support, and responsiveness to culture, history, and gender. In an exam case, trauma history may explain distress, but it should not be used to dismiss new confusion, intoxication, psychosis, mania, injury, withdrawal, or medical instability.

The ACUTE Differential Check

CueUrgency Question
A - Altered awareness or consciousnessIs there new disorientation, fluctuating attention, fainting, inability to stay awake, seizure, or severe dissociation that cannot be confidently distinguished?
C - Cognitive or neurological changeIs there sudden confusion, weakness, speech change, head injury, severe headache, memory gap, or markedly changed behavior?
U - Unstable behavior or inability to careIs the person unable to maintain basic safety, shelter, food, medical care, or behavioral control?
T - Toxicity, intoxication, or withdrawalCould medication, alcohol, sedative, stimulant, opioid, or other substance effects explain or worsen the presentation?
E - Escalating psychosis, mania, or exposureAre there command hallucinations, severe insomnia, grandiosity, impulsivity, aggression, exploitation, environmental exposure, or rapidly worsening function?

Psychosis and Command Hallucinations

Do not assume that hallucinations automatically require hospitalization or that a calm appearance means safety. Clarify content, frequency, distress, perceived power, ability and willingness to resist, intent, access to targets or means, substance and medical contributors, sleep, history of acting on commands, supports, and functional change.

Mania and Severe Activation

Assess reduced need for sleep, increased energy, pressured speech, racing thoughts, grandiosity, impulsive spending or travel, sexual risk, aggression, psychosis, medication changes, substances, medical causes, and ability to care for self. The level-of-care decision depends on danger, grave disability, judgment, supports, willingness, and feasibility—not the diagnostic label alone.

Substance and Medical Urgency

Crisis presentations may reflect intoxication, withdrawal, overdose, medication interaction, infection, metabolic disturbance, head injury, pregnancy-related emergency, pain, or other medical conditions. New confusion, altered consciousness, severe autonomic symptoms, seizure, injury, overdose, or rapidly changing cognition generally moves medical evaluation ahead of routine psychotherapy assessment. Follow emergency protocols and do not ask the client to drive when unsafe.

CTA EXAM TIP
When the case contains both psychiatric distress and an acute medical or neurological warning sign, the best next step often addresses medical safety first while maintaining behavioral-health support.

5Safety Planning, Level of Care, Consultation, and Documentation

Choose a response that can actually manage the risk, then make the handoff visible.

Safety Planning Is Collaborative Risk Reduction

A safety plan is a prioritized, individualized set of actions the client can use before or during a crisis. It is developed collaboratively, written in accessible language, rehearsed, stored where it can be found, and updated when circumstances change. It does not replace emergency evaluation when the person cannot safely use the plan.

Plan ElementQuality Check
Personal warning signsConcrete cues the client can recognize before the crisis peaks.
Internal coping actionsBrief actions the client can try without contacting another person.
People or places for distractionSpecific and realistically available.
People who can helpNamed supports, consent considerations, and backup contacts.
Professionals and crisis resourcesCounselor/clinic instructions, 988, local mobile crisis, urgent care, emergency department, or 911 as appropriate.
Lethal-means safetySpecific method, responsible person, timing, verification, and backup.
Barriers and rehearsalTransportation, privacy, language, disability, technology, fear, cost, and what to do if the first step fails.

California 5150 and 5585.50 Orientation

Welfare and Institutions Code section 5150 authorizes specified professionals to take or cause an adult to be taken into custody for up to 72 hours for assessment, evaluation, crisis intervention, or placement when statutory criteria are met. Section 5585.50 addresses specified detention authority for minors when, as a result of a mental disorder, the minor is a danger to others, a danger to self, or gravely disabled and authorization for voluntary treatment is unavailable. Candidate answers should respect both clinical need and the counselor’s actual legal authority.

Chapter 5 Summary

  • A safety plan is collaborative and specific; it is not a substitute for emergency evaluation when the client cannot use it safely.
  • Level of care depends on severity, collaboration, supports, environment, duties, and feasibility.
  • Use 988, mobile crisis, emergency services, medical care, and higher levels according to the actual need and local availability.
  • Do not claim detention authority that your role does not provide.
  • Documentation should show facts, formulation, consultation, actions, handoff, and follow-up.

Elena is a 36-year-old client seen in an outpatient clinic. She arrives without an appointment, appears frightened, and repeatedly checks the hallway. Elena says, “I cannot do this anymore,” but asks the counselor not to call anyone because she fears losing housing and custody.

Section 1 - Immediate Engagement

Elena is tearful and breathing rapidly. She can answer brief questions but becomes overwhelmed when asked for a detailed relationship history. She reports that the former partner is not in the building. Clinic staff can secure the entrance and remain nearby.

The counselor should document the facts, quotes, sources, formulation, report, consultations, handoff, and follow-up. The exact emergency pathway depends on local resources, clinic policy, and the counselor’s legal authority.

Answers and Detailed Rationales

Question 1 (LPCC-WB04-Q001)

Correct Answer: B

A. Incorrect. Hospitalization may become necessary, but the available facts do not yet establish acuity or inability to use a less restrictive safe option.

B. Correct. The statement requires direct assessment of current suicidal thinking, intent, plan, behavior, and means before selecting the response.

C. Incorrect. Normalizing before assessment can minimize a safety cue and discourage disclosure.

D. Incorrect. Cognitive work comes after the counselor clarifies immediate risk.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
When a client introduces a suicide cue, assess directly before reassuring, treating, or choosing disposition.

Question 2 (LPCC-WB04-Q002)

Correct Answer: C

A. Incorrect. A screen identifies the need for further assessment; it does not prove imminent danger.

B. Incorrect. Repeating a screen does not replace a focused safety assessment.

C. Correct. A positive response should lead to a trained clinician’s further assessment and disposition decision.

D. Incorrect. A no-harm agreement is not a risk assessment or safety plan.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Screening asks who needs more assessment. Disposition follows the assessment and formulation.

Question 3 (LPCC-WB04-Q003)

Correct Answer: B

A. Incorrect. Denied intent does not eliminate suicide risk or medical concerns, and the function and pattern remain clinically important.

B. Correct. The counselor distinguishes NSSI from suicidal behavior while assessing overlap, severity, escalation, and safety.

C. Incorrect. Nonsuicidal self-injury is not automatically a law-enforcement matter.

D. Incorrect. One behavior does not establish a personality-disorder diagnosis.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Do not equate NSSI with a suicide attempt, but never treat the distinction as permission to skip suicide assessment.

Question 4 (LPCC-WB04-Q004)

Correct Answer: C

Specific target + serious physical threat + access/preparation moves the question from exploration to protective action.

Question 5 (LPCC-WB04-Q005)

Correct Answer: B

A. Incorrect. The clinician does not investigate or confront before making a required report.

D. Incorrect. The reporting duty is individual; supervision does not transfer it.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Reasonable suspicion triggers reporting. Proof and supervisor permission are not prerequisites.

Question 6 (LPCC-WB04-Q006)

Correct Answer: B

A. Incorrect. A past IPV disclosure by a competent adult does not automatically create the same reporting duty as child or elder abuse.

B. Correct. The counselor assesses current danger and applicable duties while supporting autonomy, resources, and a tailored safety plan.

C. Incorrect. Directing the client to leave can increase danger and disregards autonomy and feasibility.

D. Incorrect. Clinical support does not depend on a police report.

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
Identify the specific protected category or duty; do not invent a universal adult-IPV reporting rule.

Question 7 (LPCC-WB04-Q007)

Correct Answer: C

A. Incorrect. Grounding is not sufficient for new slurred speech, confusion, and reduced consciousness.

B. Incorrect. Trauma history does not justify assuming dissociation when acute medical signs are present.

C. Correct. The presentation requires urgent medical evaluation and emergency procedures.

D. Incorrect. A symptom inventory would delay care and cannot rule out medical emergency.

New neurological or consciousness changes outrank routine psychiatric assessment.

Question 8 (LPCC-WB04-Q008)

CTA EXAM STRATEGY
A safety plan is a rehearsed action sequence, not a promise or a single phone number.

Question 9 (LPCC-WB04-Q009)
