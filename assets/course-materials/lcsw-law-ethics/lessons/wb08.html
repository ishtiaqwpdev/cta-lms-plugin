Section I

Confidentiality Across Treatment Relationships

Why This Section Matters

Confidentiality is one of the foundations of the therapeutic relationship.

Clients must be able to share sensitive information with confidence that it will be protected. However, confidentiality becomes more complicated when treatment involves more than one person, when information is shared outside the session, or when another individual or organization requests access.

In individual therapy, confidentiality usually centers on one client. In couple, family, and group treatment, the therapist may need to consider:

  • Who is legally and clinically considered the client
  • Whether multiple people hold client status
  • Whose information is being requested
  • What confidentiality expectations were established
  • Whether disclosure has been authorized
  • Whether another legal basis permits or requires disclosure

The California LCSW Law and Ethics Examination frequently tests whether the social worker pauses to identify the treatment relationship, client status, information involved, request type, authority, and applicable source layer before releasing information.

The strongest response is rarely to disclose immediately. The therapist must first determine who is protected, who is requesting information, and what authority exists for the disclosure.

Topics Covered

This section addresses:

  • Confidentiality in individual treatment
  • Confidentiality in couple and family treatment
  • Confidentiality in group therapy
  • Identifying clients, participants, and collateral contacts
  • Separate communications and secrets policies
  • Conflicting requests for information
  • Records involving multiple people
  • Authorizations and releases of information
  • Limits on disclosure

Key Principle

The presence of another person in treatment does not automatically give that person the right to receive confidential information.

Before disclosing, the therapist must identify the client or clients, review the treatment agreements, and determine whether authorization or another lawful basis exists.

Big Idea

Confidentiality becomes more complex when treatment involves multiple people.

NASW Code of Ethics section 1.07 requires social workers to respect privacy, disclose confidential information only with valid consent or another compelling legal/ethical basis, and explain limits as early as feasible. Sections 1.08 and 3.04 add access-to-records and documentation duties. In multi-person work, the LCSW/ASW must clarify who the clients are, what confidentiality expectations apply, and how records and separate communications will be managed.

Before disclosing information, the therapist must identify:

  • The treatment setting
  • The client or clients
  • Whose information is involved
  • What confidentiality policies were established
  • Whether valid authorization or another lawful basis exists

Client

A client receives professional services and is owed duties involving confidentiality, informed consent, competent care, documentation, and client welfare.

Joint Clients

In couple or family treatment, more than one person may hold client status.

The therapist must consider the rights and interests of all clients rather than automatically treating one participant as primary.

Participant

A participant may attend or contribute to treatment without becoming a client.

Attendance alone does not establish client status.

Collateral Contact

A collateral contact provides information or support related to treatment but is not receiving therapy.

Examples include:

  • A spouse attending one individual session
  • A teacher providing behavioral information
  • A caregiver assisting with planning
  • A physician discussing coordination of care

Third Party

A third party is an outside person or organization requesting or receiving information.

Examples include:

  • Employer
  • School
  • Attorney
  • Insurance company
  • Relative
  • Other provider

Interest in the client does not create authority to receive confidential information.

Red FlagDo not assume that everyone present in a session has the same clinical or legal status.

Individual Therapy

Individual therapy usually has the clearest confidentiality structure because one person is identified as the client.

Information should generally remain confidential unless:

  • The client authorizes disclosure
  • A legal exception applies
  • Another lawful basis permits or requires disclosure

High-Yield Rule

Even confirming that a person is receiving treatment may reveal confidential information.

Common Request

An adult client’s family member asks whether the client is attending sessions.

The therapist should first determine whether the client authorized disclosure.

The family member’s concern does not create access.

Information From Others

A relative or other person may provide information without receiving information in return.

The therapist should consider:

  • Whether receiving the information is clinically appropriate
  • Whether the information should enter the record
  • Whether the client should be informed
  • Whether ongoing contact could create role confusion

Red Flag“I need to tell you something, but you cannot tell the client.”

The therapist should avoid promising secrecy before considering how the information affects treatment and documentation.

Couple Therapy

Couple treatment requires clear expectations before problems arise.

The therapist should clarify:

  • Who the clients are
  • Whether both partners are joint clients
  • How separate communications will be handled
  • Whether individual meetings may occur
  • Whether a secrets policy applies
  • How records and releases will be managed
  • What happens if the partners disagree

Separate Communications

Separate communications may occur through:

  • Individual sessions
  • Calls
  • Emails
  • Text messages
  • Portal messages
  • Conversations before or after joint sessions

These communications can create divided loyalties when one partner asks the therapist to conceal important information.

Example

One partner reveals an affair during an individual check-in and demands permanent secrecy.

The therapist should review the policy established during informed consent rather than automatically promising secrecy or immediately disclosing the information.

Secrets Policies

A secrets policy explains how private disclosures will be handled in couple or family treatment.

When a Secret Disrupts Treatment

A secret may make joint treatment difficult when it:

  • Directly affects treatment goals
  • Requires the therapist to mislead another client
  • Creates divided loyalties
  • Prevents informed participation
  • Creates safety or legal concerns
  • Makes the therapist’s role unworkable

Possible responses may include:

  • Encouraging disclosure
  • Explaining the effect of continued secrecy
  • Revisiting the treatment agreement
  • Seeking consultation
  • Pausing joint treatment
  • Referring or transferring care

What Do I Do First?Review the confidentiality agreement and secrets policy before deciding how to respond.

Family therapy may include several people with different roles.

The therapist should clarify:

  • Who is receiving treatment
  • Whether the family is the treatment unit
  • Whether some people are collateral contacts
  • How separate communications will be handled
  • Who may request information
  • How records and releases will be managed

Group Therapy

The therapist is responsible for maintaining confidentiality, but cannot fully control what group members disclose outside the group.

High-Yield Rule

The therapist may require and reinforce confidentiality, but cannot guarantee that every member will comply.

Before Group Treatment

Members should understand:

  • Confidentiality is expected
  • The therapist is bound by professional duties
  • Other members may not be bound in the same way
  • The therapist cannot guarantee member behavior
  • Outside disclosure may affect continued participation
  • Privacy risks should be discussed before treatment begins

When a Breach Occurs

If a member reports that another participant disclosed private information outside the group, the therapist should:

  • Assess what occurred.
  • Address the breach.
  • Review group expectations.
  • Evaluate the effect on safety and trust.
  • Determine whether corrective action is needed.
  • Document significant concerns.

The therapist should not promise that no future disclosure can occur.

Records Involving Multiple People

Couple, family, and group records may contain information about several individuals.

This becomes important when someone requests:

  • Inspection
  • Copies
  • A treatment summary
  • Release to another provider
  • Records for legal proceedings
  • Information for an insurer or employer

Clinical Applications

Scenario 1: Concerned Family Member

An adult client’s sister calls the therapist and asks whether the client has been attending sessions and making progress.

What should the therapist do first?

A. Provide a brief update because the sister is concernedB. Confirm attendance but withhold clinical detailsC. Determine whether the client authorized disclosureD. Ask the sister to explain why she needs the information

Correct Answer: C

The therapist should determine whether the client authorized disclosure. A family member’s concern does not permit the therapist to confirm attendance, treatment status, or progress.

Why the Other Answers Are Incorrect

  • A is incorrect because good intentions do not eliminate confidentiality.
  • B is incorrect because confirming attendance may itself disclose confidential information.
  • D is incorrect because the sister’s reason does not create authority to receive information.

Scenario 2: Private Disclosure in Couple Therapy

During an individual check-in connected to couple therapy, one partner reports an affair and demands permanent secrecy.

What should the therapist do first?

A. Promise to keep the information secretB. Disclose the affair during the next joint sessionC. Review the established confidentiality and secrets policyD. Terminate couple therapy immediately

Correct Answer: C

The therapist should first review the policy established during informed consent.

Why the Other Answers Are Incorrect

  • A is incorrect because the therapist should not make an immediate promise that may conflict with the treatment agreement.
  • B is incorrect because immediate disclosure may violate the policy or exceed the therapist’s authority.
  • D is incorrect because termination is not automatically the first response.

Scenario 3: Parent Request During Family Treatment

A parent requests copies of all family-therapy notes because the parent attended every session.

What should the therapist evaluate first?

A. Whether the parent paid for treatmentB. Whether the parent attended every sessionC. Client status, legal authority, and whose information appears in the recordD. Whether the request was submitted in writing

Correct Answer: C

The therapist must determine who holds client status, what authority the parent has, and whether the record contains information involving other participants.

Why the Other Answers Are Incorrect

  • A is incorrect because payment does not automatically create access rights.
  • B is incorrect because attendance does not establish unrestricted access.
  • D is incorrect because a written request does not resolve the underlying authority question.

Scenario 4: Group Confidentiality Breach

A group member reports that another participant shared details about the member’s trauma history outside the group.

What is the therapist’s strongest response?

A. Guarantee that the information will never be shared againB. Ignore the issue because group members are not therapistsC. Assess what occurred, address the breach, and reinforce group expectationsD. Disclose the violating member’s treatment history to the group

Correct Answer: C

The therapist should assess the incident, address its impact, reinforce expectations, and determine whether corrective action is needed.

Why the Other Answers Are Incorrect

  • A is incorrect because the therapist cannot guarantee another member’s behavior.
  • B is incorrect because the breach may affect safety and trust.
  • D is incorrect because that response would create another confidentiality violation.

Scenario 5: One Partner Requests the Entire Record

One partner in couple therapy signs a release authorizing the therapist to send the complete treatment record to an attorney.

What should the therapist do?

A. Send the entire file because one client signed the releaseB. Refuse every request involving couple recordsC. Determine whether the authorization is sufficient and whether the record contains protected information about the other partnerD. Remove the other partner’s name and send the rest automatically

Correct Answer: C

The therapist should evaluate whether one partner has authority to authorize release of the full record and whether disclosure would expose information about the other partner.

Why the Other Answers Are Incorrect

  • A is incorrect because one participant may not have authority to release the entire joint record.
  • B is incorrect because some disclosures may be permitted after proper review.
  • D is incorrect because removing a name may not adequately protect identifiable information.

Common Exam Traps

Trap 1

Assuming Participation Equals Client Status

A person may attend treatment without becoming a client.

Better reasoning: Clarify the person’s role before determining rights or obligations.

Trap 2

Assuming Family Concern Creates Authorization

A relative may be sincere and worried, but concern does not create permission to disclose.

Better reasoning: Determine whether authorization or another lawful basis exists.

Trap 3

Treating Couple Therapy Like Individual Therapy

Joint treatment may involve multiple clients, separate communications, and conflicting requests.

Better reasoning: Review the treatment structure and secrets policy.

Trap 4

Guaranteeing Group Confidentiality

The therapist can require and reinforce confidentiality but cannot control every group member.

Better reasoning: Explain the limits before treatment begins.

Trap 5

Assuming One Signature Releases an Entire Multi-Person Record

A record may contain information involving several people.

Better reasoning: Evaluate authority, scope, and third-party information before disclosure.

Trap 6

Creating a Secrets Policy After a Secret Is Disclosed

Waiting until a conflict occurs creates confusion and divided loyalties.

Better reasoning: Establish the policy during informed consent.

Chapter Summary

  • Confidentiality applies across individual, couple, family, and group treatment.
  • The first step is identifying the client or clients.
  • Participation does not automatically create client status or access rights.
  • Couple and family treatment require clear policies for separate communications.
  • Secrets policies should be explained before treatment begins.
  • Group confidentiality can be expected and reinforced, but not guaranteed.
  • Multi-person records may contain information affecting several individuals.
  • One participant may not have authority to release an entire joint record.
  • Before disclosure, identify the treatment setting, client status, information involved, and legal authority.

Chapter 2

Authorizations, Releases of Information, and Limits on Disclosure

Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Explain the purpose of an authorization for release of information.
  • Distinguish authorization from informed consent, consultation, subpoena, and court order.
  • Identify the required elements of a valid authorization.
  • Determine whether an authorization is current, complete, and applicable to the requested disclosure.
  • Apply the authorized scope when releasing client information.
  • Explain how expiration and revocation affect disclosure.
  • Evaluate requests from providers, schools, employers, family members, insurers, and attorneys.
  • Recognize additional concerns involving psychotherapy notes and multi-person records.
  • Identify the appropriate first response when receiving a request for client information.

Big Idea

A signed authorization permits a specific use or disclosure of confidential information.

It does not:

  • Eliminate confidentiality
  • Make the client’s information public
  • Authorize disclosure to every requester
  • Permit release of the entire record
  • Replace the therapist’s responsibility to review the request

Before releasing information, the therapist must verify that the authorization is valid, current, and broad enough to cover the intended disclosure.

Core Content

What Is an Authorization?

An authorization is a document through which a client or authorized representative permits identified information to be used or disclosed for a stated purpose.

Authorizations can support:

  • Coordination of care
  • Communication with another provider
  • School accommodations
  • Employment or leave documentation
  • Insurance review
  • Legal representation
  • Transfer of records
  • Communication with family members

A release does not require the therapist to disclose everything requested. The disclosure must remain within the authorization and applicable law.

Required Elements of a Valid Authorization

A therapist should not stop at:

“The client signed a release.”

The therapist must determine whether the document contains enough information to support the planned disclosure.

Under HIPAA, a valid authorization generally includes a meaningful description of the information, who may disclose it, who may receive it, the purpose, an expiration date or event, and the client’s or personal representative’s dated signature.

Meaningful Specificity

An authorization should clearly describe the information to be released.

Examples include:

  • Attendance dates
  • Diagnosis
  • Treatment plan
  • Progress summary
  • Assessment results
  • Billing records
  • Specified progress notes
  • Complete clinical record

“Any and all information” may expose more information than the client understands or intends to release. The therapist should clarify vague or unusually broad language before disclosing.

Signature Alone Is Not Enough

A form may still be incomplete or unusable when it:

  • Does not identify the recipient
  • Does not describe the information
  • Has expired
  • Was revoked
  • Was signed by someone without authority
  • Applies to a different purpose
  • Authorizes a different provider
  • Contains contradictory information

What Do I Do First?Review the entire authorization—not only the signature line.

Scope of Disclosure

The authorization establishes the outer limit of what may be disclosed.

Example

A client authorizes the therapist to provide:

Appointment dates and attendance information to the employer.

The therapist should not automatically send:

  • Diagnosis
  • Treatment plan
  • Symptoms
  • Progress notes
  • Trauma history
  • Medication information
  • Complete clinical record

The client authorized attendance information, not the entire file.

Necessary Versus Authorized

Two related questions must be considered:

  • Is this information authorized?
  • Is this the appropriate amount of information for the stated purpose?

A therapist should not interpret a broad release as permission to disclose irrelevant or unnecessarily sensitive material.

Under HIPAA, the “minimum necessary” standard does not apply to every disclosure in the same way; for example, it generally does not apply to disclosures made pursuant to a valid authorization. The authorization’s actual scope still controls what may be disclosed.

Verifying Identity and Authority

Before releasing information, the therapist should verify:

  • The identity of the requester
  • The identity of the signer
  • The signer’s authority
  • The intended destination
  • The contact information used for transmission

Personal Representatives

A person may sometimes act for a client because of legally recognized authority.

Examples may include:

  • Parent or guardian
  • Conservator
  • Health-care decision-maker
  • Executor or other representative after death
  • Person otherwise authorized by law

The therapist should not assume authority based only on the person’s relationship to the client.

Expiration

An authorization must remain current when disclosure occurs.

Expiration may be based on:

  • A specific date
  • Completion of a stated purpose
  • End of a defined event
  • Another legally permissible expiration condition

HIPAA requires an expiration date or an expiration event related to the individual or the purpose of the disclosure.

Exam Warning

Do not assume that an old authorization remains usable merely because:

  • It is in the record
  • The client once signed it
  • The requester has received records before
  • The parties are still working together

Red FlagDates hidden in the question may determine whether the release is valid.

Revocation

A client may generally revoke an authorization in writing, subject to applicable law and limited exceptions.

Revocation usually affects future uses and disclosures. It does not ordinarily reverse a disclosure already made in reliance on a valid authorization.

When revocation is received, the therapist should:

  • Document the revocation.
  • Stop future disclosures covered by the authorization.
  • Update the client’s record or release-tracking system.
  • Inform relevant staff when necessary.
  • Determine whether another lawful basis independently permits or requires disclosure.

Electronic and Copied Authorizations

An authorization may be transmitted or completed electronically when applicable legal requirements are satisfied.

HIPAA permits electronic authorizations and electronic copies of valid signed authorizations, provided the electronic signature is valid under applicable law.

The therapist should still verify:

  • Legibility
  • Completeness
  • Authenticity when reasonably questioned
  • Recipient information
  • Secure transmission
  • Whether the authorization was altered

A scanned form is not valid merely because it looks official.

Psychotherapy Notes

Psychotherapy notes receive special treatment under HIPAA when they meet the federal definition and are maintained separately from the clinical record.

They do not include ordinary record information such as:

  • Diagnosis
  • Treatment plan
  • Symptoms
  • Prognosis
  • Progress
  • Session times
  • Testing results
  • Medication information

With limited exceptions, HIPAA requires specific authorization before psychotherapy notes are disclosed, including for disclosure to another provider.

Requests From Other Providers

Another provider’s involvement does not automatically create unrestricted access to the client’s record.

The therapist should determine:

  • Whether authorization is required
  • Whether another legal basis permits disclosure
  • What information supports coordination
  • Whether psychotherapy notes are involved
  • What the client understands and prefers
  • Whether state law is more protective

A request for treatment coordination does not necessarily justify release of the complete record.

Requests From Schools

School personnel may request information for:

  • Educational planning
  • Accommodations
  • Attendance concerns
  • Behavioral support
  • Coordination with school services

The therapist should not assume the school may access treatment information merely because the client is a student.

Appropriate Analysis

  • Identify who is requesting information.
  • Determine who may authorize disclosure.
  • Review the specific information requested.
  • Confirm the purpose.
  • Release only what is permitted.

Requests From Employers

Employers may request:

  • Attendance verification
  • Leave documentation
  • Accommodation information
  • Fitness-for-duty information
  • Return-to-work recommendations

Employment does not create a general confidentiality exception.

The therapist should avoid disclosing unnecessary:

  • Diagnoses
  • Trauma history
  • Session content
  • Family information
  • Treatment details

The therapist must also clarify whether providing an evaluation or opinion would place the therapist in a role beyond ordinary treatment.

Requests From Family Members

Family members may be involved, concerned, or financially supportive.

None of those facts automatically authorizes disclosure.

Before responding, determine:

  • Whether the client authorized communication
  • What information may be shared
  • Whether the client restricted the authorization
  • Whether the family member has legal authority
  • Whether another exception applies

The therapist may sometimes receive information from a family member without disclosing information in return.

Requests From Insurers and Payors

Payors may request information for:

  • Claims processing
  • Coverage determination
  • Utilization review
  • Medical-necessity review
  • Reimbursement

The therapist should determine:

  • What disclosure the client was informed about
  • What authorization or other legal basis applies
  • What the payor contract requires
  • What information is relevant
  • Whether the request exceeds what is permitted

A payor’s request does not automatically authorize release of the entire clinical record.

Broader billing, insurance, and financial responsibilities will be addressed in a later workbook.

Requests From Attorneys

An attorney’s letter, telephone call, or email is a request—not automatic authority to disclose.

The therapist should distinguish among:

  • Informal attorney request
  • Client-signed authorization
  • Subpoena
  • Court order
  • Request involving privilege
  • Request from the client’s own attorney
  • Request from an opposing attorney

Multi-Person Records

  • Whether third-party information must be protected
  • Whether a summary or narrower disclosure would be appropriate
  • Whether legal consultation is necessary

One client’s signature does not automatically resolve every confidentiality issue in a joint record.

Scenario 1: Attendance Authorization

A client signs an authorization allowing the therapist to confirm appointment dates to the client’s employer. The employer asks for the client’s diagnosis and treatment plan.

What should the therapist do?

A. Release everything because the client signed an authorizationB. Release only the information covered by the authorizationC. Refuse to provide even the attendance datesD. Ask the employer what diagnosis it suspects

Correct Answer: B

The authorization permits disclosure of appointment dates. It does not authorize the diagnosis or treatment plan.

Why the Other Answers Are Incorrect

  • A is incorrect because an authorization does not permit unlimited disclosure.
  • C is incorrect because the attendance dates may be disclosed if the authorization is valid.
  • D is incorrect because the employer’s assumptions do not determine the authorized scope.

Scenario 2: Incomplete Authorization

A therapist receives a signed authorization that identifies the client and the records but does not identify a recipient.

What should the therapist do first?

A. Send the records to the person who requested themB. Ask the client to clarify or complete the authorizationC. Release the records to every provider involvedD. Assume the client intended the therapist to choose the recipient

Correct Answer: B

The therapist should obtain clarification because the document does not identify who may receive the information.

Why the Other Answers Are Incorrect

  • A is incorrect because the requester may not be the authorized recipient.
  • C is incorrect because involvement in treatment does not create unlimited access.
  • D is incorrect because the therapist should not supply a missing authorization element through assumption.

Scenario 3: Expired Authorization

A school requests a progress summary using an authorization that expired three months ago.

What is the strongest response?

A. Send the summary because the client previously agreedB. Send only verbal informationC. Obtain a current valid authorization before disclosingD. Ask the school to promise confidentiality

Correct Answer: C

An expired authorization should not be relied upon for a new disclosure.

Why the Other Answers Are Incorrect

  • A is incorrect because prior permission does not continue after expiration.
  • B is incorrect because changing the communication method does not cure the expired authorization.
  • D is incorrect because the school’s promise does not replace client authorization.

Scenario 4: Revoked Authorization

A client revokes a release permitting communication with a family member. The family member calls the next day asking for an update.

What should the therapist do?

A. Provide the update because the family member was previously authorizedB. Stop future disclosures based on the revoked authorizationC. Disclose only positive informationD. Continue disclosure until the family member acknowledges revocation

Correct Answer: B

The therapist should not continue relying on the revoked authorization for future disclosures.

Why the Other Answers Are Incorrect

  • A is incorrect because the authorization no longer supports future disclosure.
  • C is incorrect because favorable information remains confidential.
  • D is incorrect because the recipient’s acknowledgment does not determine the effect of the client’s revocation.

Scenario 5: Attorney Request

An attorney sends a letter requesting a client’s complete treatment record. No authorization, subpoena, or court order is included.

What should the therapist do first?

A. Release the record because attorneys are officers of the courtB. Ignore the request permanentlyC. Determine whether valid authority for disclosure existsD. Send a treatment summary instead

Correct Answer: C

An attorney’s request alone does not automatically authorize disclosure.

Why the Other Answers Are Incorrect

  • A is incorrect because attorney status does not remove confidentiality protections.
  • B is incorrect because the therapist should respond appropriately rather than disregard the request.
  • D is incorrect because a summary is still a disclosure requiring authority.

Common Exam Traps

Trap 1

Stopping at “The Client Signed It”

A signature does not cure missing elements, expiration, revocation, or improper authority.

Better reasoning: Review the entire authorization.

Trap 2

Treating Authorization as Unlimited Permission

A release permits only the information, recipient, purpose, and time period identified.

Better reasoning: Match the planned disclosure to the authorization.

Trap 3

Ignoring Expiration or Revocation

Old permission may no longer support a current disclosure.

Better reasoning: Verify current validity immediately before releasing information.

Trap 4

Assuming Professional Requesters Have Automatic Access

Providers, schools, employers, insurers, and attorneys do not receive unlimited access because of their professional roles.

Better reasoning: Identify the lawful basis for each request.

Trap 5

Psychotherapy notes are separately maintained notes with special protections under HIPAA.

Better reasoning: Identify what type of record is actually being requested.

Trap 6

Releasing a Multi-Person Record Based on One Signature

A joint record may contain protected information about several clients or participants.

Better reasoning: Evaluate client status, authority, and third-party information.

Trap 7

Sending Records Without Verifying the Destination

A valid authorization does not prevent a confidentiality breach caused by misdirected transmission.

Better reasoning: Confirm the recipient and delivery method before sending.

What Is Protected Health Information?

Under HIPAA, Protected Health Information, commonly called PHI, is individually identifiable health information created, received, maintained, or transmitted by a HIPAA-covered entity or its business associate.

PHI may exist in:

  • Paper records
  • Electronic records
  • Verbal communication
  • Emails
  • Text messages
  • Billing systems
  • Scheduling systems
  • Client portals
  • Audio or video recordings

Electronic PHI is commonly called ePHI.

Important Distinction

Not every confidential therapy record is automatically HIPAA PHI.

Information may still be protected by:

  • California confidentiality law
  • California medical-information law
  • Professional ethical duties
  • Contractual obligations
  • Agency policies
  • Other specialized federal laws

HIPAA applies only when the person or organization falls within HIPAA’s regulated structure. HHS explains that the Privacy Rule applies to health plans, health-care clearinghouses, and certain health-care providers, along with business associates performing covered functions for them.

When Does HIPAA Apply?

HIPAA generally applies to:

  • Health plans
  • Health-care clearinghouses
  • Health-care providers that conduct covered electronic transactions
  • Business associates performing certain services involving PHI for covered entities

A therapist should not assume:

“I provide health care, so every part of my practice is automatically governed by HIPAA.”

The therapist must determine whether the practice is a covered entity and whether the information is being handled within a HIPAA-regulated function.

Business Associate

A business associate is a person or organization that performs specified services for a covered entity and needs access to PHI.

Examples may include:

  • Billing company
  • Electronic-record vendor

Psychotherapy Notes

Psychotherapy notes are not another name for all therapy records.

Under HIPAA, psychotherapy notes are the therapist’s separate notes documenting or analyzing the contents of counseling conversations and maintained separately from the general clinical record.

They generally do not include:

  • Diagnosis
  • Treatment plan
  • Symptoms
  • Prognosis
  • Progress
  • Session dates
  • Medication information
  • Test results
  • Summary of functional status

With limited exceptions, HIPAA requires authorization before psychotherapy notes are disclosed, including disclosure to another provider for treatment.

California Privacy Protections

For LCSW/ASW examination reasoning, Business and Professions Code section 4992.3(n) identifies failure to maintain client confidentiality—except as required or permitted by law—as unprofessional conduct. Section 4992.3(w) addresses clinically and professionally appropriate records, and section 4992.3(z) makes willful violation of California patient-access law a disciplinary ground. These provisions operate alongside the CMIA, patient-access statutes, privilege law, NASW ethics, HIPAA when applicable, and other specialized authority.

California law may protect medical and mental-health information independently of HIPAA.

The California Confidentiality of Medical Information Act, often called the CMIA, generally restricts health-care providers, health plans, and contractors from disclosing medical information without authorization unless a statutory exception applies.

California may provide stronger protection than federal law in particular situations. The California Attorney General has specifically noted circumstances in which the CMIA imposes stricter medical-privacy limits than federal privacy law.

Access Is Different From Disclosure to a Third Party

A client asking for their own records is exercising a potential access right.

Those situations require different analyses.

Who May Request Access?

Access may be requested by:

  • An adult client
  • A minor who was legally authorized to consent to the care reflected in the records
  • A legally recognized personal representative
  • Another person acting under specific lawful authority

California law gives adult patients, qualifying minors, and personal representatives rights to inspect records and obtain copies, subject to limited exceptions.

Client

An adult client may generally request access to records relating to their care.

The therapist may require reasonable identity verification, but the verification process cannot be used to frustrate or delay lawful access.

Personal Representative

A personal representative is someone authorized under applicable law to make relevant health-care decisions for another person.

Depending on the scope of that authority, the person may exercise access rights for:

  • An adult lacking decision-making capacity
  • A minor
  • A deceased client or the client’s estate

The representative’s access is limited to the health information relevant to the authority granted. A title such as spouse, parent, caregiver, or adult child does not automatically establish authority.

Inspection Versus Copies

California distinguishes between inspecting a record and receiving a copy.

Inspection

Inspection allows the client or representative to review the record.

The person may be accompanied by one person of their choosing during the inspection.

Copies

A request for copies should identify the records being requested.

The therapist should determine:

  • Whether the request covers the full record or only part
  • Whether paper or electronic copies are requested
  • Whether the requested format is available
  • Where the records should be sent
  • Whether the record contains information requiring additional review

HIPAA Timing

For HIPAA-covered entities, the federal Privacy Rule generally requires action on an access request within 30 calendar days. One additional 30-day extension may be used if the client receives a timely written explanation and completion date. California’s shorter applicable timeline should not be ignored merely because HIPAA allows more time.

High-Yield Rule

When both California and HIPAA apply, use the applicable rule that gives the client the stronger or faster access right.

Format of the Record

A client may request:

  • Paper copies
  • Electronic copies
  • Specific portions
  • A complete record
  • A summary, when agreed upon and legally appropriate

When records are maintained electronically, California requires the provider to supply them in the requested electronic form if readily producible. Otherwise, the provider and requester should agree on a readable format.

A therapist should not automatically print an electronic record when the client properly requests a usable electronic copy.

Fees

A provider may generally charge a reasonable, cost-based fee for producing copies or an agreed summary.

California permits fees based on:

  • Copying labor
  • Supplies
  • Portable electronic media when requested
  • Postage
  • Preparation of an agreed summary or explanation

Paper-copy fees generally may not exceed $0.25 per page, and microfilm copies generally may not exceed $0.50 per page.

Important Rule

A therapist may not withhold a client’s records because the client has an unpaid treatment bill.

Red Flag“The client owes me money” is not a lawful reason to hold the record hostage.

Summary of Records

In some situations, a summary may be useful or legally permitted instead of producing every document.

However, the therapist should not substitute a summary merely because:

  • The therapist is embarrassed by the record
  • The therapist dislikes the client’s reason
  • The therapist fears criticism
  • Producing the record is inconvenient
  • The therapist believes the client “does not need” the information

A summary should be used only when legally permitted and consistent with the client’s rights and agreement.

Minor Records and Parent Requests

Parental access depends on:

  • Who consented to treatment
  • Whether the minor had independent legal authority to consent
  • What services the record concerns
  • Whether a parent is the minor’s personal representative for those services
  • Whether California law limits access

Under HIPAA, parents are often personal representatives of minor children, but not when the minor legally consented independently, another person lawfully consented, or the parent agreed to a confidential provider relationship. HIPAA also defers to California law governing parental access.

California law provides that a representative of a minor is not entitled to inspect or copy certain records involving care to which the minor lawfully consented. Access may also be restricted when the provider determines that parental access would harm the professional relationship or the minor’s physical safety or psychological well-being.

Minor Record Decision Questions

  • Who consented to treatment?
  • Was the minor authorized to consent independently?
  • What portion of the record relates to that independently consented care?
  • Does the requester have legal authority?
  • Would access create a legally recognized concern?
  • Is the decision being made in good faith and documented?

Minor consent rules are taught in detail in Workbook #1.

Psychotherapy Notes

HIPAA excludes separately maintained psychotherapy notes from the ordinary federal right of access.

Psychotherapy notes are not the same as:

  • Progress notes
  • Diagnosis
  • Treatment plans
  • Symptoms
  • Prognosis
  • Clinical summaries
  • Medication information
  • Session dates

A client generally has a HIPAA right to access information in the designated record set, but not the therapist’s separately maintained psychotherapy notes.

Can Access Ever Be Denied?

Access may be limited only under specific legal conditions.

The therapist should not deny access merely because the client may:

  • Become upset
  • Disagree with the therapist
  • File a complaint
  • Question the diagnosis
  • Discover a documentation mistake
  • Use the record in litigation
  • End treatment

Under HIPAA, denials are permitted only in limited circumstances. General concern that a person may feel distressed or misunderstand the record is not sufficient. Certain denials require written notice and an opportunity for independent review.

California Mental Health Record Limitation

California permits a provider to decline direct inspection or copies of mental health records when the provider determines there is a substantial risk of significant adverse or detrimental consequences to the client.

When using this exception, the provider must:

  • Make a written record of the request
  • State the reason for refusal
  • Describe the specific anticipated consequences
  • Permit access by or provide copies to a licensed professional designated by the client

This is a narrow exception, not a general “therapist discretion” rule.

Written Denials

When HIPAA permits a denial, the covered entity generally must provide a written explanation that includes:

  • The reason for denial
  • Whether review is available
  • How to request review
  • How to file a complaint

Even when one portion may be withheld, the therapist should provide access to the remaining information when reasonably possible.

Records About More Than One Person

Couple, family, and group records create additional access concerns.

A record may include:

  • Statements from another client
  • Information about a spouse or child
  • Private communications
  • Group-member information
  • Third-party reports
  • Collateral information
  • Details concerning nonclients

One person’s access right does not automatically erase the confidentiality interests of everyone else named in the record.

Multi-Person Record Review

Before releasing, ask:

  • Who is requesting the record?
  • Who is legally considered a client?
  • Is the record joint or individual?
  • Whose information appears in it?
  • Would release expose another client’s confidential information?
  • Can portions be separated?
  • Would a narrower record or summary satisfy the request?
  • Is qualified legal consultation needed?

Couple Therapy

One partner should not automatically receive the entire joint record without analysis of:

  • Joint-client status
  • How the record was maintained
  • Separate communications
  • Information concerning the other partner
  • Existing treatment agreements
  • Applicable access and confidentiality law

Family Therapy

A parent’s attendance, payment, or family role does not alone establish unrestricted access to every family participant’s information.

Group Therapy

A group member’s record should not include unnecessary identifying information about other members. A request by one member should be reviewed to prevent disclosure of another participant’s private information.

Third-Party Information

Records may contain information supplied by:

  • Relatives
  • Teachers
  • Employers
  • Other providers
  • Partners
  • Caregivers
  • Group members

The therapist should not automatically remove every reference to another person. The question is whether disclosure would improperly expose protected third-party information and whether the record can be provided in a manner consistent with applicable law.

Good documentation reduces this problem by avoiding unnecessary, inflammatory, or irrelevant detail about nonclients.

Electronic Access

Electronic access should be handled with the same care as paper access.

Before transmitting records:

  • Verify the requester’s identity
  • Confirm the correct email address or portal account
  • Confirm the requested format
  • Protect files during transmission
  • Send only the correct client’s records
  • Check attachments carefully
  • Document the date and method of delivery

Convenience does not justify sending records to an unverified address.

Requests From Deceased Clients’ Representatives

Records do not automatically become public after a client’s death.

A person seeking access should have legal authority to act for the deceased client or the estate, and the information requested should relate to that authority. HIPAA continues to protect identifiable health information for 50 years after death.

Being a family member does not automatically establish access.

Documenting the Request and Response

The therapist should document:

  • Date the request was received
  • Identity of the requester
  • Authority verified
  • Records requested
  • Format requested
  • Applicable deadline
  • Fees discussed
  • Records inspected or supplied
  • Date and method of delivery
  • Any restriction or denial
  • Written explanation provided
  • Review rights offered
  • Consultation obtained
  • Follow-up communication

Clinical Applications

Scenario 1: Client Requests Copies

A client submits a written request for an electronic copy of the complete clinical record. The record is maintained electronically and can be produced in the requested format.

What should the therapist do?

A. Require the client to accept paper copiesB. Provide the electronic copy within the applicable California deadlineC. Refuse because electronic delivery is less secureD. Provide only a treatment summary

Correct Answer: B

California generally requires copies within 15 days and requires an electronic format when the record is electronically maintained and readily producible in the requested format.

Why the Other Answers Are Incorrect

  • A is incorrect because a paper-only requirement may conflict with the client’s format rights.
  • C is incorrect because security concerns should be addressed through proper transmission, not automatic refusal.
  • D is incorrect because the therapist cannot substitute a summary solely for convenience.

Scenario 2: Unpaid Balance

A former client requests copies of treatment records but owes the therapist for several sessions.

What should the therapist do?

A. Withhold the record until payment is receivedB. Provide the record according to access law and address the balance separatelyC. Release only records from paid sessionsD. Require the client to sign a payment plan first

Correct Answer: B

California prohibits withholding records because of an unpaid treatment bill.

Why the Other Answers Are Incorrect

  • A is incorrect because records cannot be used as leverage for payment.
  • C is incorrect because access does not depend on which sessions were paid.
  • D is incorrect because a payment agreement is not a lawful prerequisite to access.

Scenario 3: Client May Become Upset

A therapist believes a client will feel embarrassed and angry after reading the progress notes.

What is the strongest response?

A. Deny access because emotional discomfort is likelyB. Destroy the upsetting portionsC. Follow the access rules unless a specific legal ground for limitation existsD. Tell the client that therapists own all records

Correct Answer: C

Discomfort, embarrassment, or disagreement is not enough by itself to deny access.

Why the Other Answers Are Incorrect

  • A is incorrect because general emotional upset does not establish a lawful denial.
  • B is incorrect because altering or destroying the record would create serious legal and ethical concerns.
  • D is incorrect because physical ownership of a record does not eliminate client access rights.

Scenario 4: Parent Requests a Minor’s Record

A parent asks for the records of a minor who independently consented to legally authorized treatment.

What should the therapist do first?

A. Release the record because parents always control minor recordsB. Determine who consented, what services are involved, and whether California law permits parental accessC. Refuse every parental requestD. Ask the parent who paid for treatment

Correct Answer: B

Parental access depends on consent authority, the type of care, and applicable California law.

Why the Other Answers Are Incorrect

  • A is incorrect because parents do not always control records for independently consented treatment.
  • C is incorrect because some parents do have lawful access.
  • D is incorrect because payment does not decide access rights.

Scenario 5: Joint Record Request

One partner requests the complete record from couple therapy. The record contains separate disclosures and sensitive information about the other partner.

What should the therapist do?

A. Release the entire record immediately because the requester was a clientB. Refuse permanently because couple records can never be releasedC. Review client status, record structure, third-party information, and applicable access law before respondingD. Remove the other partner’s name and send everything else automatically

Correct Answer: C

The therapist must evaluate all affected rights before releasing a multi-person record.

Why the Other Answers Are Incorrect

  • A is incorrect because one client’s access request may affect another client’s confidential information.
  • B is incorrect because access is not categorically prohibited.
  • D is incorrect because deleting a name may not remove identifiable or protected information.

Common Exam Traps

Trap 1

Treating a Client Request Like a Third-Party Release

Clients may be exercising direct access rights rather than asking the therapist to disclose information to someone else.

Better reasoning: Identify whether the requester seeks their own record.

Trap 2

Using HIPAA’s 30 Days When California Requires Faster Action

Federal law may allow more time than California.

Better reasoning: Apply the shorter controlling California deadline when required.

Trap 3

Withholding Records for Nonpayment

A financial dispute does not erase access rights.

Better reasoning: Provide lawful access and handle collections separately.

Trap 4

Denying Access Because the Therapist Feels Uncomfortable

Fear of criticism, litigation, or embarrassment is not a legal basis for denial.

Better reasoning: Identify a specific statutory ground before restricting access.

Trap 5

Assuming Parents Always Control Minor Records

Minor-consent laws can change who controls access.

Better reasoning: Determine who lawfully consented to the treatment involved.

Trap 6

Calling Every Therapy Note a Psychotherapy Note

Ordinary progress notes remain part of the general clinical record.

Better reasoning: Apply the actual definition, not the label.

Trap 7

Releasing a Multi-Person Record Without Review

Couple, family, and group records may contain information protected for several people.

Better reasoning: Review client status, authority, and third-party information first.

Chapter Summary

  • Client access requests are different from third-party disclosure requests.
  • Adult clients, qualifying minors, and personal representatives may have access rights.
  • California generally requires inspection within five working days and copies within fifteen days.
  • Electronic records should be provided electronically when the requested format is readily producible.
  • HIPAA generally allows 30 calendar days, but California may require a faster response.
  • Records cannot be withheld because of an unpaid treatment balance.
  • Fees must remain reasonable and cost-based.
  • Parent access depends on who consented to treatment and what California law permits.
  • Psychotherapy notes are distinct from ordinary progress notes.
  • Access may be restricted only under narrow legal conditions.
  • General concern that a client may become upset is not enough to deny access.
  • Multi-person records require careful review of every affected person’s information.
  • The therapist should document the request, authority, deadline, response, and any lawful limitation.

15. Minimum Necessary Does Not Apply Identically Everywhere

The minimum necessary standard generally limits certain uses, disclosures, and internal access.

It does not operate identically for:

  • Treatment disclosures
  • Valid authorizations
  • Client access
  • Required disclosures
  • Psychotherapy notes

High-Yield Distinction

Authorization asks what the client permitted.

Minimum necessary asks whether the information is appropriately limited for the purpose.

19. Records Cannot Be Withheld for Nonpayment

The therapist may address an unpaid balance through lawful financial procedures.

The therapist may not deny access merely because the client owes money.

Trigger Phrase

“The former client owes for several sessions.”

Correct Reasoning

Handle:

  • Record access
  • Collections

20. Access May Be Restricted Only Under Narrow Legal Conditions

A therapist should not deny access because the client may:

  • Become angry
  • Feel embarrassed
  • Disagree
  • Complain
  • Sue
  • Question the diagnosis
  • Discover an error

High-Yield Rule

Therapist discomfort is not a legal ground for denial.

When a lawful restriction applies, the therapist must follow required documentation, notice, and review procedures.

21. Minor Record Access Depends on Who Consented

  • Did the minor independently consent?

  • Is the parent acting as a personal representative?
  • Does California law restrict access?

“The minor independently consented.”

Immediate Sequence

  • Identify the information.

  • Hacking or ransomware

Do not wait for confirmed harm before assessing the incident.
