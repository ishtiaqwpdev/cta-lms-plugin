Chapter 1

Confidentiality in Individual, Couple, Family, and Group Treatment

BIG IDEA | A person may attend a session, provide information, or support treatment without becoming a client. The therapist must determine what role was established and explained during informed consent. Because the spouse participated in one individual session, the spouse does not automatically gain access to the client’s treatment information, progress, or records. Disclosure still requires valid authorization or another lawful basis. Even confirming that a person attends therapy may reveal confidential information. The therapist should first determine whether the client authorized communication with the sister or whether another lawful basis permits disclosure. The sister’s concern may be sincere, but concern and family status do not create authority to receive treatment information.

  • Explain and apply Family-Treatment Roles.

Secrets Policies in Couple TherapySeparate communications in couple therapy can create divided loyalties and interfere with informed participation. The therapist should first review the policy established before treatment regarding individual contacts, private disclosures, and secrets. The policy may indicate that clinically relevant information cannot be kept indefinitely, may allow temporary confidentiality while disclosure is addressed, or may require case-by-case analysis. The therapist should apply the established policy consistently rather than improvise after the conflict arises.When one partner reveals a secret, use: Review the policy before promising, disclosing, or terminating. A strong sequence is: Review the established secrets policy Assess how the information affects treatment Explain the consequences of continued secrecy Encourage appropriate disclosure Consult, pause, or transfer treatment when necessary
Information From Collateral ContactsA therapist may sometimes receive clinically relevant information from a collateral contact without disclosing confidential information in return. However, the therapist should not automatically promise to conceal the communication from the client. The therapist should consider: Whether the information is relevant to treatment Whether it should be documented Whether the client should be informed Whether continued collateral contact could create confusion or divided loyalties Whether the information raises an immediate safety concern The therapist should clarify these limits before agreeing to receive extensive information.When someone says: “I need to tell you something, but you cannot tell the client,” do not promise secrecy automatically. First evaluate the information’s clinical relevance, documentation impact, and effect on treatment.
Family-Treatment RolesAttendance, payment, transportation, and close family involvement do not automatically create client status or authority to receive confidential information. The therapist must determine the grandmother’s actual role and whether the client authorized disclosure. If the grandmother claims legal authority, the therapist should verify that authority and its scope before sharing information.The following do not automatically create access: Paying Providing transportation Attending sessions Being closely related Expressing concern
Limits of Group ConfidentialityThe therapist is responsible for maintaining professional confidentiality, explaining group expectations, reinforcing those expectations, and addressing violations. However, the therapist cannot fully control what individual members disclose after leaving the group. Prospective members should understand this limitation before agreeing to participate.Remember: Group confidentiality may be expected and reinforced—but not guaranteed.
Multi-Person Treatment RecordsA multi-person record may contain protected information about more than one client or participant. One partner’s signature does not automatically resolve the other partner’s confidentiality interests. The therapist should evaluate: Who holds client status How the record was structured What policies governed separate communications Whether the authorization covers the proposed disclosure Whether the signer may authorize release of the entire record Whether information can be separated or disclosed more narrowlyFor joint records, ask: Who signed—and whose information would actually be released?
Individual Treatment With Family ParticipationA person may participate in treatment without becoming a client or joint client. The therapist must look at how the treatment relationship was established, who is receiving services, who consented, and what applicable minor-consent and parental-access rules permit. Occasional participation for support or coordination does not automatically transform individual treatment into family therapy or create unrestricted access to the teenager’s communications.Separate: Who participates Who receives treatment Who has legal authority Who may receive information These are not always the same person.
Secret That Disrupts Couple TreatmentThe therapist should apply the policy established during informed consent and explain how the secret affects the integrity of couple treatment. The therapist may encourage the partner to disclose the information, support a plan for disclosure, and clarify that treatment cannot continue normally if the therapist must mislead the other joint client. Consultation, pausing treatment, or arranging transfer may be appropriate when the role becomes unworkable.A no-secrets policy does not mean: “The therapist immediately reveals everything.” It means the therapist should not agree to preserve treatment-disrupting secrets indefinitely and may need to reconsider whether joint treatment can continue.
Integrated Multi-Person Confidentiality AnalysisThe therapist must slow down and analyze several separate issues: Who was identified as a client Whether the family or individual members were the treatment unit What policy governed separate emails Whose confidential information appears in the file Whether the parent has legal or client authority Whether the information can be separated Whether authorization or another lawful basis exists Payment and administrative involvement do not automatically determine access. The therapist must protect every affected client or participant from unnecessary disclosure.For complex family-treatment questions, use this sequence: SETTING → CLIENTS → INFORMATION → POLICY → AUTHORITY → DISCLOSURE Do not begin with the requester’s relationship, payment, or level of involvement.
Collateral ContactsA collateral contact provides information or support related to treatment without necessarily receiving therapy. The teacher’s contribution may be clinically useful, but it does not automatically create client status or a right to receive information in return. Before disclosing anything to the teacher, the therapist must identify a valid authorization or another lawful basis.Remember: Information may flow into treatment without automatically flowing back out.
Family Involvement and ConfidentialityFamily treatment may involve joint clients, individual clients, participants, and collateral contacts. Those roles do not automatically carry identical confidentiality or access rights. The therapist must determine who is receiving professional services, what policies were established, whose information is involved, and whether authorization or another lawful basis supports disclosure.Family participation does not erase confidentiality. Identify CLIENT STATUS → AGREEMENTS → AUTHORITY before sharing information.
First Step in Multi-Person TreatmentClient status determines whose rights, information, and professional interests may be involved. Before deciding whether information may be shared, the therapist must know whether treatment involves one client, joint clients, a family treatment unit, or a combination of clients and participants. Once client status is clear, the therapist can evaluate the treatment agreements, information involved, requester’s authority, and lawful basis for disclosure.In multi-person treatment, start with: Who is the client? Not: Who is the relative, payer, organizer, or most involved participant?
Group Confidentiality BreachThe therapist cannot undo the disclosure or guarantee another member’s future behavior. The therapist can investigate what occurred, address the violating conduct, reinforce group expectations, assess the impact on the affected member and the group, and consider corrective action. Depending on the seriousness and circumstances, the therapist may also need to modify participation, revisit informed consent, provide support, and document the incident.After a group breach, use: Assess → Address → Reinforce → Evaluate impact → Correct → Document
No Established Secrets PolicyThe therapist should not invent a retroactive policy and apply it as though both partners previously understood and accepted it. The therapist should also avoid promising secrecy that may make joint treatment deceptive or unworkable. The stronger response is to recognize the informed-consent gap, assess the information’s relevance to treatment, discuss the consequences of continued concealment with the disclosing partner, clarify how future communications will be handled, and obtain consultation when necessary. Joint treatment may need to be paused or transferred if the therapist cannot proceed without misleading another client.No established policy does not mean: Disclose automatically Promise secrecy automatically Ignore the secret It means the therapist must address the unclear expectations and determine whether treatment can continue ethically.
Conflicting Rights in a Family RecordThe record may implicate several people’s confidentiality and access interests. The therapist must determine whether the requesting parent is a client, joint client, participant, or representative and whether that status creates access to some or all of the record. The therapist should also evaluate the adult child’s separate information, the collateral contact’s statements, the treatment agreements, and whether a narrower disclosure can protect affected individuals while honoring any lawful access right.For multi-person records, review: Clients → Requester → Authority → Record structure → Other people’s information → Possible separation
Couple-Therapy Confidentiality and Records — Part 1 of 2The scenario presents two related but distinct confidentiality problems. First, the therapist must apply the limited-secrets policy to the private disclosure. The therapist should explain why indefinite concealment may undermine joint treatment, encourage appropriate disclosure, and consider whether treatment can continue if the issue remains unresolved. Second, the record request requires its own analysis.

Record request: Identify access or disclosure rights, authority, scope, and other protected information.

Chapter 2

Authorizations, Releases of Information, and Limits on Disclosure

BIG IDEA | An authorization is the client’s or authorized representative’s permission for a defined use or disclosure of confidential information. It should identify what may be disclosed, who may disclose it, who may receive it, the purpose, and when the authorization expires. The authorization creates permission only within its stated scope. The therapist must still confirm that the form is valid and that the planned disclosure matches what the client actually authorized. A signature is only one element of a valid authorization. The intended recipient must be meaningfully identified so the therapist knows who is permitted to receive the information. The therapist should not fill this gap through assumption. The client should clarify or complete the document before any disclosure occurs.

Purpose of an AuthorizationAn authorization is the client’s or authorized representative’s permission for a defined use or disclosure of confidential information. It should identify what may be disclosed, who may disclose it, who may receive it, the purpose, and when the authorization expires. The authorization creates permission only within its stated scope. The therapist must still confirm that the form is valid and that the planned disclosure matches what the client actually authorized.Think: Authorization = specific permission, not unlimited access.
Valid Authorization ElementsA signature is only one element of a valid authorization. The intended recipient must be meaningfully identified so the therapist knows who is permitted to receive the information. The therapist should not fill this gap through assumption. The client should clarify or complete the document before any disclosure occurs.When a question says, “The client signed the release,” keep reading. Check: Recipient Information Purpose Expiration Signer’s authority Revocation status
Scope of Authorized DisclosureThe authorization permits disclosure of appointment dates and attendance information. It does not authorize disclosure of the diagnosis, symptoms, treatment plan, or other clinical details. The therapist should match the disclosure precisely to the authorized scope. The employer’s request cannot expand the client’s permission.Use the scope-matching test: Information authorized = information released Do not expand the release merely because the requester asks for more.
Signer AuthorityBeing the client’s parent does not automatically give the mother authority to authorize disclosure for a competent adult. The therapist must determine whether she is a legally recognized personal representative and verify the scope of that authority. Possible authority might arise from a valid health-care decision-making document, conservatorship, court order, or another lawful arrangement. Without verified authority or the client’s authorization, the therapist should not release information.Do not assume automatic authority based on titles such as: Parent Spouse Adult child Caregiver Close relative Look for verified personal-representative authority.
Expired AuthorizationAn expired authorization no longer supports a new disclosure. The fact that the school lawfully received information in the past does not extend the authorization beyond its stated expiration date or event. The therapist should obtain a new authorization containing the necessary elements before releasing the current progress summary, unless another lawful basis independently permits disclosure.Pay close attention to dates hidden in the stem. Previously authorized does not mean currently authorized.
RevocationRevocation generally prevents future reliance on the authorization. The therapist should document the revocation, update the release-tracking process, notify relevant staff when necessary, and stop future disclosures covered by the revoked permission. The therapist should still consider whether another independent legal basis permits or requires disclosure, but the revoked authorization itself can no longer be used.Remember the distinction: Expiration: Permission ends according to a date or event. Revocation: The client actively withdraws permission. Both generally stop future reliance.
Electronic and Copied AuthorizationsElectronic or scanned authorizations may be valid when applicable requirements are satisfied, but the therapist must still review their completeness, legibility, authenticity, and apparent integrity. A reasonably suspected alteration should be clarified before information is released. The therapist should confirm the client’s intent and ensure that the identified recipient matches the client’s authorization.For electronic releases, check: Complete → Legible → Authentic → Unaltered → Correct recipient → Secure destination
Broad Authorization and Sensitive InformationA broad authorization should not be treated as an instruction to release every sensitive detail without review. The therapist should ensure that the client understands the scope, determine whether the requested information is appropriate for the stated disability purpose, and assess whether collateral or third-party information requires protection. Separately maintained psychotherapy notes require additional analysis and generally specific authorization. The therapist should also verify the recipient, expiration, revocation status, and secure destination before releasing anything.A broad release creates an outer boundary, not an automatic command to disclose every page. Ask: What does the client understand? What serves the stated purpose? Are third parties affected? Are psychotherapy notes involved? Is a narrower disclosure appropriate?
Authorization for a Multi-Person RecordThe authorization may be facially complete, but one partner’s signature does not automatically authorize disclosure of every other person’s information in a multi-person record. The therapist must examine: Joint-client status How the record was maintained Policies governing separate communications Whether the signer has authority over the entire file The other partner’s confidential information Information concerning the children Whether parts of the record can be separated Whether narrower disclosure or legal consultation is appropriate A valid signature is only one part of the analysis.For multi-person authorizations, ask: Who signed—and whose information would leave the file? Do not confuse a valid-looking form with valid authority over everyone’s information.
Authorization vs. Informed ConsentInformed consent explains the nature of treatment, relevant risks and benefits, policies, fees, confidentiality limits, and client choices. It supports the client’s decision to participate in services. An authorization serves a different purpose. It permits identified information to be used or disclosed to a particular recipient for a stated purpose and period.Do not confuse: Consent to treatmentwithPermission to disclose information
Attorney RequestsAn attorney’s letter is a request, not automatic authority to receive confidential information. The therapist should determine whether the client has provided a valid authorization or whether some other lawful process supports disclosure. The therapist must identify what legal document actually exists before deciding how to respond.Sort the document before acting: Attorney letter → request only Authorization → verify scope Subpoena → separate legal analysis Court order → review exact direction
Professional RequestersAnother provider’s involvement does not automatically create unrestricted access to the client’s entire record. The therapist should determine whether authorization is required, whether another legal basis applies, what information is clinically relevant, and whether specially protected material is involved. Care coordination may support disclosure in some circumstances, but it does not automatically justify sending the complete file.Professional requester does not mean automatic access. Ask: What authority applies, and what information is actually needed?
Verifying the DestinationA valid authorization does not prevent a confidentiality breach caused by sending information to the wrong destination. The therapist should verify the recipient and delivery information before transmitting records. The therapist should also confirm that the correct client document is attached, use an appropriate secure method, and document the disclosure.Before sending, verify: Recipient → Address → Attachment → Secure method
Purpose MismatchA valid authorization is limited not only by the recipient and information described but also by its stated purpose. Permission to use records for workplace accommodation may not authorize their use in unrelated custody litigation. The therapist should review the exact language, clarify the client’s intent, and obtain a new or revised authorization when the new use falls outside the original scope.Match all four: Recipient + Information + Purpose + Time period A match in only one or two areas is not enough.
Employer Requests and Professional RoleAn authorization permits disclosure but does not require the therapist to provide opinions beyond the therapist’s role, competence, or available clinical basis. A treating therapist may not be positioned to make a comprehensive occupational fitness determination. The therapist should clarify what limited information was authorized, explain the limits of the treating role, and avoid unsupported conclusions. A separate qualified evaluator may be needed for a formal fitness-for-duty opinion.A release answers: “May information be disclosed?” It does not automatically answer: “Is the therapist qualified to give the requested opinion?”
Release-of-Information AnalysisThe authorization cannot support the requested disclosure for several independent reasons: It has expired. It covered only attendance dates and a progress summary. It was signed by only one joint client. The requested file contains information about the other partner. It did not specifically address separately maintained psychotherapy notes. The therapist should not disclose based on the old form. The therapist must determine what current authorization or legal authority exists, examine whether both partners’ interests are affected, and separately evaluate the request for psychotherapy notes.For complex release questions, use this sequence: Identify the requester. Identify whose information is involved. Verify the signer’s authority. Check every authorization element. Check expiration and revocation. Match recipient, information, purpose, and time. Identify special or multi-person records. Verify the destination. Release only what is lawfully permitted. Document the response.

Chapter 3

Privacy, Protected Health Information, and HIPAA

BIG IDEA | Psychotherapy notes are narrowly defined notes documenting or analyzing counseling-session conversations that are maintained separately from the general clinical record. Their separate content and storage give them special protection under HIPAA. Privacy concerns the client’s interest in controlling access to personal information. Confidentiality concerns the therapist’s duty to protect information learned through the professional relationship. Information security refers to the administrative, physical, and technical safeguards used to prevent unauthorized access, loss, alteration, or disclosure.

What to Learn

  • Explain and apply Psychotherapy Notes.
  • Explain and apply Privacy vs. Confidentiality vs. Security.
  • Explain and apply Identifiable Health Information.
  • Explain and apply HIPAA Applicability.
  • Explain and apply Business Associates and BAAs.
  • Explain and apply HIPAA Security Rule.
  • Explain and apply Minimum Necessary.

Psychotherapy NotesPsychotherapy notes are narrowly defined notes documenting or analyzing counseling-session conversations that are maintained separately from the general clinical record. Their separate content and storage give them special protection under HIPAA.Use the clue: Separate storage + session analysis
Privacy vs. Confidentiality vs. SecurityPrivacy concerns the client’s interest in controlling access to personal information. Information security refers to the administrative, physical, and technical safeguards used to prevent unauthorized access, loss, alteration, or disclosure.Use the three-question formula: Privacy: Who should have access? Identifiable Health InformationProtected health information is not limited to diagnoses, symptoms, or progress notes. A client’s name combined with appointment information and the identity of a health-care provider may reveal that the person is receiving services. The therapist should treat the incident as a possible unauthorized exposure and promptly begin the appropriate containment and assessment process.Remember: No diagnosis does not mean no protected information. Names, appointments, billing details, insurance information, and treatment participation may all reveal a health-care relationship.
HIPAA
Applicability		HIPAA is not the source of every confidentiality obligation. A therapist who is not a HIPAA-covered entity may still be governed by California confidentiality and privacy laws, professional ethical standards, contractual duties, agency policies, and other specialized rules. The absence of HIPAA coverage does not make treatment information public or remove the therapist’s obligation to protect it.Avoid this exam trap: “HIPAA does not apply, so confidentiality does not apply.” The correct sequence is: Determine whether HIPAA applies. Identify applicable California law. Consider ethical and contractual duties. Apply the most protective controlling requirement.
Business Associates and BAAsA company that maintains electronic protected health information for a HIPAA-covered practice may qualify as a business associate. The practice should determine whether a Business Associate Agreement is required and evaluate the vendor’s safeguards, access practices, subcontractors, incident-reporting procedures, and data handling after the contract ends. A BAA generally identifies permitted uses and disclosures, required safeguards, reporting obligations, and responsibilities for protecting PHI.When a vendor handles client information, ask: Does the vendor create, receive, maintain, or transmit PHI? That question often triggers the business-associate and BAA analysis.
HIPAA
Security Rule		The Security Rule applies to electronic protected health information. It requires covered entities and applicable business associates to use reasonable and appropriate safeguards addressing the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of ePHI. Examples include staff training, access controls, device protections, passwords, encryption, secure backups, risk analysis, and incident-response procedures.Remember the three security goals: Confidentiality: No unauthorized access Integrity: No improper alteration or destruction Availability: Authorized users can access information when needed
Minimum NecessaryThe minimum necessary standard does not operate identically in every disclosure context. When disclosure is made under a valid authorization, the therapist should follow the actual scope of that authorization. Here, the client authorized a specified progress summary. The therapist should disclose that summary—not less merely because of minimum necessary and not more merely because a signature exists.Separate these questions: Authorization: What did the client permit?Minimum necessary: Is the information appropriately limited for this purpose? Do not assume minimum necessary applies identically to every disclosure.
Indirect IdentificationRemoving a name does not necessarily de-identify information. A rare diagnosis, small location, occupation, family structure, public event, photograph, voice, or combination of demographic and clinical facts may allow someone to connect the information to a particular person. The therapist should determine whether the details could reasonably identify the client and remove or alter enough information to protect privacy.Ask: Could someone familiar with the client reasonably connect these details to that person?
Confidential CommunicationsClients may have legitimate privacy needs concerning where and how a practice contacts them. The therapist should discuss the preferred method, document it, apply it consistently, and update the instructions when circumstances change. A neutral message and private number may reduce the risk of revealing the treatment relationship or sensitive information to unauthorized people.Communication preferences may involve: Alternative telephone numbers Neutral voicemail language Alternative mailing addresses Specific email accounts No contact at work
Privacy-Incident ResponseEvery suspected exposure requires prompt evaluation, although not every incident automatically becomes a legally reportable breach. The therapist should act quickly to contain the risk, report through the proper practice or compliance channel, identify the information and clients involved, assess possible access or acquisition, and evaluate federal and California notification duties. Immediate action may include remotely disabling or wiping the device, changing access credentials, preserving evidence, contacting appropriate leadership, obtaining privacy or legal consultation, and documenting the response.Use the incident sequence: Contain → Identify → Assess → Report → Correct → Document Do not wait for confirmed harm.
Integrated Electronic Privacy and Vendor RiskA BAA does not make the entire practice compliant. Privacy and security also depend on how systems are configured, who has access, whether credentials are shared, whether former workers are removed, whether devices are protected, and whether staff follow established procedures. The practice should promptly disable access, preserve evidence, determine which records were viewed, report the event, assess notification requirements, and correct the underlying failures. Corrective action may include unique credentials, role-based access, automatic account termination, device controls, encryption, workforce training, and improved monitoring.“HIPAA-compliant software” or a signed BAA is never the entire answer. Evaluate: Vendor contract + system configuration + workforce access + device safeguards + incident response
HIPAA
Privacy Rule		The HIPAA Privacy Rule addresses how protected health information may be used or disclosed and provides individuals with certain rights concerning their information. It includes subjects such as authorizations, access rights, confidential communications, personal representatives, treatment and payment disclosures, minimum necessary, and notices of privacy practices.Remember: Privacy Rule = uses, disclosures, and client rights
Electronic Protected Health InformationElectronic protected health information, commonly called ePHI, is identifiable health information that is created, received, maintained, or transmitted electronically within HIPAA’s regulated structure. A record containing the client’s name, diagnosis, and treatment plan directly connects identifiable information with health care.Look for both: Health-care information + identifiable client
HIPAA and California LawHIPAA often establishes a federal privacy floor rather than replacing every stronger state protection. A California therapist must consider whether state confidentiality or medical-information law places additional limits on the disclosure. When applicable law requires greater privacy protection, the therapist should follow the controlling, more protective requirement.Use this order: Does HIPAA apply? Does California law apply? Do the requirements differ? Which controlling rule provides greater protection?
Minimum Necessary and Treatment DisclosuresThe minimum necessary standard generally does not apply to provider-to-provider disclosures for treatment in the same way it applies to certain other uses and disclosures. That does not mean every treatment disclosure is unrestricted. The therapist must still determine whether HIPAA applies, whether California law permits the disclosure, whether the information supports the treatment purpose, and whether psychotherapy notes or another specially protected category is involved.Do not translate: “Minimum necessary does not apply” into: “Send everything.”
Misdirected Electronic DisclosureThe therapist should respond immediately rather than wait for confirmed harm. Containment may include contacting the unintended recipient, requesting deletion or secure return, disabling access when possible, and preserving relevant evidence. The therapist must then determine what information was involved, whether it was accessed, which client was affected, and what federal, California, organizational, or contractual notification duties apply.For a wrong attachment: Contain first—then assess notification duties.
Privacy, Security, PHI, and Incident Response — Part 1 of 2The signed BAA and software marketing do not establish overall compliance. The practice remains responsible for its own administrative, physical, and technical safeguards. The practice should promptly disable former-employee access, change compromised credentials, preserve and review access logs, determine which information was viewed, assess applicable notification obligations, and document its analysis. It must also correct the systemic problems through unique credentials, role-based access, automatic screen locking, secure devices, encryption, workforce training, and timely account termination. Both progress notes and psychotherapy notes require protection. 
Privacy, Security, PHI, and Incident Response — Part 2 of 2The psychotherapy notes may receive additional disclosure protections, but separate storage does not make unauthorized access unimportant.For integrated privacy questions, evaluate all layers: Information: Is it identifiable health information? Law: Do HIPAA and California law apply? Access: Was the person authorized? Security: Were administrative, physical, and technical safeguards adequate? Incident response: Was the exposure contained, assessed, reported, corrected, and documented?

  • When a vendor handles client information, ask: Does the vendor create, receive, maintain, or transmit PHI? That question often triggers the business-associate and BAA analysis.
  • Remember the three security goals: Confidentiality: No unauthorized access Integrity: No improper alteration or destruction Availability: Authorized users can access information when needed
  • Separate these questions: Authorization: What did the client permit?Minimum necessary: Is the information appropriately limited for this purpose? Do not assume minimum necessary applies identically to every disclosure.
  • Ask: Could someone familiar with the client reasonably connect these details to that person?

Chapter 4

Client Access to Records and Responding to Record Requests

BIG IDEA | An adult client requesting their own treatment record is exercising a potential right of access. The therapist should determine which records are requested, verify the client’s identity, identify the applicable California and federal requirements, and respond within the appropriate timeframe. This differs from a request to disclose information to another person or organization. California generally requires a patient or authorized representative to be allowed to inspect records during business hours within five working days after the request is received. Copies generally must be transmitted within 15 days. When California and HIPAA both apply, the therapist should not rely on HIPAA’s longer general federal timeframe when California provides the client with faster access rights.

An unpaid balance, complaint risk, or disagreement with the diagnosis does not justify withholding the record. California’s generally faster 15-day copy deadline controls when applicable, and a readily producible requested electronic format should be used. Ordinary progress notes, the treatment plan, and billing information are generally reviewed under the clinical-record access rules. Separately maintained psychotherapy notes require a distinct state-and-federal analysis, and confidential collateral information must be reviewed before disclosure.
