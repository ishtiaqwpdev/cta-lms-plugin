Chapter 1

Foundations of Informed Consent

BIG IDEA | Meaningful informed consent is a process involving information, sufficient understanding, voluntariness, and an actual treatment decision. Informed consent exists to support meaningful client decision-making and participation.

What to Learn

  • Explain and apply Informed Consent as a Process.
  • Explain and apply Purpose and Autonomy.
  • Explain and apply Informed Refusal.
  • Explain and apply Decision-Specific Capacity.
  • Explain and apply Questions After Intake.
  • Explain and apply Shared Decision-Making.
  • Explain and apply External Pressure and Voluntariness.

Core Study Rules

ConceptControlling Study RuleMemory Cue
Informed Consent as a ProcessMeaningful informed consent is a process involving information, sufficient understanding, voluntariness, and an actual treatment decision.When the choices contrast a process with paperwork, select the answer centered on meaningful decision-making.
Purpose and AutonomyInformed consent exists to support meaningful client decision-making and participation.The primary purpose is client decision-making—not guaranteed outcomes, defensive paperwork, or therapist protection.
Informed RefusalA competent adult in voluntary treatment may generally decline a proposed intervention after receiving and understanding relevant information.Look for the qualifiers competent adult, voluntary treatment, and understands the recommendation.
Decision-Specific CapacityCapacity is decision-specific and concerns the person’s ability to understand and participate meaningfully in that decision.Eliminate answers that equate diagnosis, disagreement, or a signature with capacity.
Questions After IntakeLater questions are part of the ongoing decision-making process and should be addressed directly.A prior signature never makes a later, reasonable treatment question irrelevant.
Shared Decision-MakingProfessional expertise permits a recommendation, but it does not justify withholding reasonable information or dismissing client questions.Distinguish a strong professional recommendation from professional domination.
External Pressure and VoluntarinessExternal pressure is relevant, but the therapist should assess the client’s own goals and choice before deciding whether participation is voluntary.The first response to external pressure is usually explore—not automatically validate or invalidate consent.
Understandable CommunicationThe therapist should use clear, accessible communication and verify comprehension.When the problem is understanding, improve communication rather than merely documenting the problem.
Client DeferenceThe therapist should confirm that the client understands the basic decision and knowingly chooses to rely on the recommendation.Client deference can be valid, but it should be informed—not assumed to be a waiver of all information.
Therapist PressureThe therapist should restore understandable, voluntary decision-making and evaluate any legitimate treatment or termination issues separately.A sudden ultimatum is a clue to evaluate therapist-created pressure and restore meaningful choice.
Identifying a Material Treatment ChangeMoving from individual to recurring conjoint treatment can materially change the nature of the service, who is considered a client, the therapist’s role, and confidentiality expectations. Those changes should be discussed before implementation.Identify whether the proposed change alters the service, roles, confidentiality, risks, costs, or expectations—not merely whether something in treatment is different.
Legal Representative and Client ParticipationThe therapist should rely on the lawful decision-maker while supporting the client’s meaningful participation to the extent possible.Separate legal consent authority from the ethical responsibility to involve the client whenever possible.
Mandated TreatmentThe therapist should clarify what is required, what information may be reported, and what meaningful choices remain.Mandated attendance does not mean unlimited therapist authority or zero client rights.
Temporary ImpairmentThe therapist should manage immediate safety concerns and defer a nonurgent major decision until meaningful participation is possible.Temporary impairment plus a nonurgent decision usually means address safety now and postpone the decision.
Autonomy and Professional ResponsibilityThe client may refuse the recommendation, but the therapist is not required to provide ineffective or incompetent treatment and should manage alternatives or transition appropriately.The strongest answer preserves both sides: client refusal and therapist professional responsibility.
Request to Avoid Material InformationThe therapist should avoid unnecessary detail while still providing information material to the decision.Choose the balance: do not overwhelm the client, but do not materially mislead.
Foundations of Informed ConsentPause the nonurgent intervention, address immediate safety, clarify the proposed change and reasonable options when the client can participate meaningfully, assess understanding and voluntariness, determine the client’s decision, and document the significant discussion. This response integrates decision-making ability, understanding, voluntariness, material change, safety, client choice, and documentation.In an integrated consent question, organize the facts: authority and ability, information and understanding, voluntariness, choice, safety, and documentation.

Chapter 2

Information Required for Informed Decision-Making

BIG IDEA | A client needs a basic, understandable explanation of the proposed service and its purpose before deciding whether to participate. The therapist should provide balanced, accurate information and acknowledge uncertainty before relying on the client’s decision.

What to Learn

  • Explain and apply Nature and Purpose of Services.
  • Explain and apply Balanced Benefits and Risks.
  • Explain and apply Reasonable Treatment Alternatives.
  • Explain and apply Confidentiality Framework.
  • Explain and apply Fees and Financial Policies.
  • Explain and apply Context-Specific Disclosure.
  • Explain and apply Developing or Unfamiliar Treatment.

Core Study Rules

ConceptControlling Study RuleMemory Cue
Nature and Purpose of ServicesA client needs a basic, understandable explanation of the proposed service and its purpose before deciding whether to participate.When the client knows when to attend and how to pay—but not what is being offered—look for the missing nature and purpose of treatment.
Balanced Benefits and RisksThe therapist should provide balanced, accurate information and acknowledge uncertainty before relying on the client’s decision.Guaranteed outcomes and intentional omission of meaningful risks are major red flags.
Reasonable Treatment AlternativesCoverage affects cost, but intentionally concealing a clinically reasonable alternative may improperly narrow the client’s decision.Distinguish discussing an alternative from being required to provide it.
Confidentiality FrameworkThe client should understand how information is generally protected and the significant limits relevant to participation.The client needs meaningful confidentiality information—not silence and not an exhaustive law lecture.
Fees and Financial PoliciesA substantial fee can affect whether a client enters or continues treatment and should be communicated before enforcement.Ask whether the undisclosed policy could reasonably affect the client’s decision to begin or continue treatment.
Context-Specific DisclosureInformed-consent content is context-specific and generally increases in depth as risk, novelty, or complexity increases.Avoid the trap that every informed-consent conversation must be identical.
Developing or Unfamiliar TreatmentNovelty and uncertainty are potentially material and should be communicated accurately before the client decides.New, developing, experimental, or unfamiliar methods require greater transparency—not less.
Accessible CommunicationThe therapist should identify material information, explain it clearly, and check comprehension.Do not confuse more pages with more understanding.
Alternatives Outside the Therapist’s PracticeThe therapist should accurately discuss clinically reasonable options and professional limitations.“Not offered here” does not mean “does not exist.”
Materiality and Client-Specific RiskA routine intervention may require additional discussion when a known individualized factor materially changes risk.Ask what information would realistically matter to this client’s decision.
Accurate Expected BenefitsThe statement explains a potential benefit and accurately acknowledges uncertainty.Language such as “may help” and “outcomes vary” is stronger than promises of cure.
Opportunity to Ask QuestionsMeaningful consent includes a genuine opportunity for questions and clarification.Information without an opportunity to respond is an incomplete process.
Relevant Financial InformationFinancial transparency combines accurate known costs, significant policies, and honest uncertainty.Choose accurate known costs plus significant policies—not silence or false certainty.
Material Risks vs. Every Conceivable RiskThe goal is accurate, proportionate, decision-relevant information.Avoid both extremes: no meaningful risk information and every imaginable remote risk.
Financial Interest and Treatment OptionsPersonal financial benefit appears to be distorting the information and narrowing the client’s meaningful choice.When the therapist has a personal interest, ask whether the client is receiving accurate information or only information that benefits the therapist.
Client Request to Avoid Material InformationThe therapist should pace and adapt the discussion while still providing material information.Do not overwhelm—but do not materially mislead.
Comprehensive Information for Informed Decision-MakingReopen the discussion, correct the inaccurate guarantee, explain the intervention accessibly, disclose material risks and financial information, discuss reasonable options, invite questions, and determine whether the client voluntarily wishes to proceed. This response restores balanced information, materiality, alternatives, financial transparency, understanding, questions, and voluntary choice.For an integrated disclosure question, check nature, benefits, risks, alternatives, costs, understanding, questions, and voluntary choice.

  • When the client knows when to attend and how to pay—but not what is being offered—look for the missing nature and purpose of treatment.
  • Guaranteed outcomes and intentional omission of meaningful risks are major red flags.
  • Distinguish discussing an alternative from being required to provide it.
  • The client needs meaningful confidentiality information—not silence and not an exhaustive law lecture.
  • Ask whether the undisclosed policy could reasonably affect the client’s decision to begin or continue treatment.
  • Avoid the trap that every informed-consent conversation must be identical.
  • New, developing, experimental, or unfamiliar methods require greater transparency—not less.
  • Do not confuse more pages with more understanding.

Chapter 3

Ongoing Informed Consent

BIG IDEA | The therapist should respond to significant changes, new information, questions, misunderstanding, and meaningful decision points. Clarification and reassessment of willingness support meaningful continued participation.

What to Learn

  • Explain and apply Meaning of Ongoing Informed Consent.
  • Explain and apply Client Questions After Intake.
  • Explain and apply Significant Treatment Change.
  • Explain and apply Newly Recognized Material Risk.
  • Explain and apply Withdrawal From a Specific Intervention.
  • Explain and apply Routine Adjustment vs. Material Change.
  • Explain and apply Individual to Conjoint Treatment.

Core Study Rules

ConceptControlling Study RuleMemory Cue
Meaning of Ongoing Informed ConsentThe therapist should respond to significant changes, new information, questions, misunderstanding, and meaningful decision points.Do not interpret “ongoing” as “repeat everything constantly.” Look for a meaningful trigger.
Client Questions After IntakeClarification and reassessment of willingness support meaningful continued participation.When a client asks a reasonable later question, choose explanation and continued choice—not “you already signed.”
Significant Treatment ChangeA material departure from the original plan is an appropriate point to revisit information and choice.“Substantially different from the original plan” is a strong renewed-discussion trigger.
Newly Recognized Material RiskNew material information should be placed in context and incorporated into the client’s continuing decision.New information that could affect the decision calls for communication—not silence or an extreme response.
Withdrawal From a Specific InterventionThe therapist should distinguish withdrawal from one intervention from withdrawal from the entire relationship and consider appropriate alternatives.Declining one technique does not automatically mean the client is ending all treatment.
Routine Adjustment vs. Material ChangeOngoing consent is responsive to material changes, not every small clinical judgment.Ask whether the change meaningfully alters what the client agreed to—not whether anything changed at all.
Individual to Conjoint TreatmentThe change may alter clients, roles, goals, confidentiality, records, and information handling.Individual to couple or family treatment should immediately trigger role, client, and confidentiality analysis.
Treatment Progress and New PlanMonitoring effectiveness and involving the client in a material change supports competent and collaborative care.Ongoing consent does not mean continuing the original plan forever; it means discussing meaningful changes.
Mandated Treatment and Remaining ChoiceMandated attendance limits voluntariness but does not eliminate accurate information, professional role clarification, confidentiality analysis, or all client choice.Mandated attendance is not the same as no autonomy, no confidentiality, and consent to everything.
Temporary Incapacity at a Major DecisionA major nonurgent decision should ordinarily wait until meaningful participation is possible.Temporary impairment plus a nonurgent decision favors postponement, not paperwork.
Reconsidering a Treatment DecisionThe therapist should explore the concern and consider an appropriate plan.An earlier “yes” is not permanent authorization.
Documentation of a Significant DiscussionThe record should show the material issue, discussion, and resulting plan.For a major change, remember: discuss it and document the discussion.
Routine ClarificationOngoing consent includes understandable clarification without unnecessary procedural overreaction.Not every later question is a formal re-consent event; respond proportionately.
Change in Professional RoleThe therapist must assess the role conflict and provide accurate information before relying on agreement.A professional-role change requires analysis of purpose, client expectations, confidentiality, and use of information.
Apparent Agreement Without UnderstandingThe therapist should correct the misunderstanding and assess the client’s actual decision.Look beyond compliance behavior. Ask whether the client actually understands.
Emergency Safety and ConsentImmediate safety may justify proportionate emergency action when ordinary participation is not possible, with explanation and documentation.In a genuine emergency, prioritize immediate safety, proportionate action, and documentation.
Comprehensive Ongoing Informed ConsentPause implementation, clarify the intervention and role changes, explain the new risk and reasonable options, assess understanding and voluntariness, determine whether the client agrees, and document the significant discussion. This response addresses the material intervention change, new risk, changed participants and roles, alternatives, pressure, understanding, and documentation.For an integrated ongoing-consent question, check change, new information, roles, questions, understanding, voluntariness, and documentation.

Exam Reasoning Checklist

  • Do not interpret “ongoing” as “repeat everything constantly.” Look for a meaningful trigger.
  • When a client asks a reasonable later question, choose explanation and continued choice—not “you already signed.”
  • “Substantially different from the original plan” is a strong renewed-discussion trigger.
  • New information that could affect the decision calls for communication—not silence or an extreme response.
  • Declining one technique does not automatically mean the client is ending all treatment.
  • Ask whether the change meaningfully alters what the client agreed to—not whether anything changed at all.
  • Individual to couple or family treatment should immediately trigger role, client, and confidentiality analysis.
  • Ongoing consent does not mean continuing the original plan forever; it means discussing meaningful changes.

Required Disclosures

BIG IDEA | The Notice is a written, documented client notice provided before psychotherapy or as soon as practicably possible afterward. For new clients after July 1, 2025 it includes the filed name, credential type and number, and expiration date. California requires accurate disclosure of the APCC's registered, unlicensed, employed, and supervised status.

  • Explain and apply Good Faith Estimate.
  • Explain and apply Notice Timing.
  • Explain and apply Clinical Counselor Trainee Disclosure.

Chapter 5

General Consent Requirements for Minors

BIG IDEA | The therapist should not assume that an accompanying relative has authority merely because the person arranged the appointment, provides transportation, or pays for treatment. The therapist should determine: Whether a parent, legal guardian, or another legally authorized person may consent Whether the minor qualifies to consent independently under California law Whether any urgent or emergency circumstance changes the immediate response California permits a minor who is at least 12 to consent to qualifying outpatient mental-health services when the attending professional determines that the minor is mature enough to participate intelligently. The existence of that exception must be evaluated; it should not be assumed merely because the minor requests care.

Verifying Authority to Consent — Part 1 of 2The therapist should not assume that an accompanying relative has authority merely because the person arranged the appointment, provides transportation, or pays for treatment. Verifying Authority to Consent — Part 2 of 2The existence of that exception must be evaluated; it should not be assumed merely because the minor requests care.Separate these facts: Who brought the minor?Who pays?Who has legal authority? Only the third question resolves consent authority.
Reviewing Custody Orders“Joint custody” is not sufficiently specific. California defines joint legal custody as both parents sharing the right and responsibility to make decisions concerning the child’s health, education, and welfare. The custody order must specify the circumstances in which both parents’ consent is required; in circumstances not requiring mutual consent, either parent may act alone. The therapist therefore needs to review the actual order instead of relying on a parent’s summary or a general assumption about joint custody.When the stem mentions: Divorce Separation Joint custody A custody dispute A court order look for: Review the actual current order. Do not guess from labels such as “joint,” “primary,” or “custodial parent.”
Court Order Requiring Mutual Consent — Part 1 of 2California Family Code section 3083 provides that a joint legal custody order specifies the circumstances in which both parents’ consent must be obtained. Either parent may act alone only in circumstances not designated for mutual consent. Because this order expressly requires both parents’ agreement for nonemergency mental-health treatment, the therapist should not proceed solely on one parent’s authorization. 
Court Order Requiring Mutual Consent — Part 2 of 2Before deciding how to proceed, the therapist may evaluate whether: Both parents can provide the required consent The minor independently qualifies to consent under applicable law A genuine emergency creates another lawful basis for immediate action Legal or professional consultation is needed Any authority analysis and resulting decision should be documented.Use this order of analysis: Read the custody order. Identify whether one or both parents must consent. Determine whether an independent minor-consent law applies. Consider emergencies separately. Document the legal and clinical basis for proceeding.
Sole Legal CustodySole legal custody gives one parent the right and responsibility to make decisions concerning the child’s health, education, and welfare. Parenting time or physical custody does not automatically transfer that legal decision-making authority to the other parent. The therapist should determine whether the mother has authorized treatment or whether another lawful basis permits services before beginning nonemergency care.Keep these concepts separate: Physical custody = where and with whom the child livesLegal custody = who makes health, education, and welfare decisions
One Parent Acting Under Joint Legal CustodyCalifornia requires a joint legal custody order to specify when both parents’ consent is required. In circumstances not designated for mutual consent, either parent may generally exercise legal control alone, provided the action is not inconsistent with the physical-custody order. The therapist should still review the actual order, verify that it is current, and document the authority analysis.Do not memorize: Joint legal custody = both signatures every time Instead, read what the order says requires mutual consent.
Caregiver’s Authorization AffidavitA qualifying relative caregiver who properly completes and signs the statutory affidavit may receive authority comparable to a guardian’s authority to authorize medical and dental care. The statute specifies that this authority may include mental-health treatment, subject to applicable Probate Code limitations. The therapist should review the actual affidavit, confirm the caregiver’s identity, evaluate its scope and current applicability, and retain appropriate documentation rather than either accepting or rejecting it automatically.When a nonparent relative brings a child to treatment, ask whether the person has: Guardianship authority A valid caregiver affidavit Written authorization Another recognized legal basis Do not decide from family relationship alone.
Physical Custody Does Not Override Legal CustodyJoint physical and joint legal custody are distinct concepts, and a court may grant joint legal custody without equal physical custody. When the controlling order expressly requires mutual consent for mental-health treatment, the child’s primary residence does not override that provision. The therapist should obtain the required authorization or identify another valid legal basis before beginning nonemergency treatment.Never use these facts as substitutes for legal authority: The child lives with this parent This parent scheduled the appointment This parent pays This parent communicates more often
Court-Appointed GuardianA guardian of the person may possess authority to consent to medical treatment for the ward, but the therapist should examine the actual court documents and applicable limitations rather than relying solely on the adult’s statement. California law also preserves situations in which a minor may consent independently under another statute. Verification should include whether the appointment remains effective, whether the guardianship concerns the person rather than only property, and whether the court imposed relevant restrictions.For guardianship questions, verify: Who was appointed + what type of guardianship + what powers were granted + whether limitations exist
Conflicting Custody DocumentsConsent authority depends on the current legal-custody arrangement, not on which parent communicates first or appears more persuasive. Sole legal custody and joint legal custody confer materially different decision-making rights. When apparently conflicting orders create genuine uncertainty and no emergency requires immediate intervention, the therapist should verify the controlling order, clarify authority, seek legal or professional consultation when needed, and document the process.When the documents conflict: Verify—do not vote, guess, or choose the more persuasive parent.
Parent Objection When One Parent May Act Alone — Part 1 of 2When a joint legal custody order specifies only certain matters requiring mutual consent, either parent may generally exercise legal control alone in other circumstances, subject to the order’s physical-custody limitations. The mother therefore may have possessed authority to authorize the treatment initially. The father’s later objection does not automatically erase that authority, but it is still significant. 
Parent Objection When One Parent May Act Alone — Part 2 of 2The therapist should review the order and objection, consider whether the family conflict affects treatment or client welfare, communicate within appropriate confidentiality limits, document the analysis, and obtain consultation when the situation becomes legally or clinically complex.Separate two questions: Did the consenting parent have legal authority? How should the therapist manage the later family conflict? A legally authorized start does not eliminate the need for thoughtful clinical management.
Payment Does Not Establish Consent AuthorityScheduling, transportation, financial responsibility, and close family involvement do not independently establish legal authority to consent for a minor. The therapist should determine whether the grandfather has guardianship, a valid caregiver authorization affidavit, authorization from a person with legal custody, or another recognized legal basis. The therapist should also assess separately whether the minor may consent independently under California law.Never equate: Pays + transports + attendswithlegally authorized to consent
Stepparent AuthorityA stepparent’s relationship or residence with the child does not, by itself, establish healthcare decision-making authority. The therapist should verify whether the person has guardianship, appropriate authorization, or a valid caregiver affidavit. California’s caregiver-affidavit law includes stepparents within its definition of a relative, meaning a qualifying stepparent may obtain authority by properly completing the applicable affidavit. That potential authority must be established rather than assumed.For a stepparent, ask: What legal document or authority supports consent? Do not answer solely from the family title.
Documentation of Consent AuthorityThe record should support the therapist’s decision to rely on a particular person’s consent. Appropriate documentation may identify: The court order, guardianship document, affidavit, or authorization reviewed The relevant provision or scope of authority The date of review Questions or limitations identified Consultation obtained when necessary The resulting treatment decision This supports continuity, accountability, and later verification without requiring the therapist to reproduce every page of a legal document in the progress note.Document enough to answer: What authority did the therapist rely upon, and why?
Emergency Safety Action vs. Routine Consent — Part 1 of 2Routine consent analysis should not delay a legally appropriate response to an immediate safety emergency. California law permits specified authorized professionals, upon probable cause, to initiate a 72-hour evaluation process for a minor who, because of a mental disorder, is dangerous to self or others or gravely disabled when voluntary authorization is unavailable. The facility must make every effort to notify the parent or guardian after detention. 
Emergency Safety Action vs. Routine Consent — Part 2 of 2The therapist should act within the therapist’s professional role and local authorization, arrange the appropriate emergency response, and document the risk assessment, consultation, actions, notifications, and disposition.Separate: Immediate crisis protectionfromauthorization for ongoing nonemergency treatment Emergency action does not erase future consent responsibilities.
Parent Decision and Caregiver AffidavitA qualifying relative caregiver with a properly completed affidavit may possess authority comparable to a guardian’s authority to authorize medical care, including certain mental-health treatment. However, California law provides that the caregiver’s decision is superseded by a contrary decision from a parent or another person with legal custody, provided that contrary decision does not jeopardize the minor’s life, health, or safety. The therapist should document the communications and evaluate whether another lawful treatment basis, including independent minor consent, may apply.For caregiver affidavits, remember: Valid authority—but not necessarily superior to the legal custodian’s contrary decision.
Guardianship and Independent Minor ConsentA guardian generally has the same right as a parent with legal custody to consent to medical treatment for a ward. California Probate Code section 2353 also expressly states that guardian consent is not required when another law allows the minor to consent independently. The therapist must therefore identify the particular service and determine whether an independent-consent statute applies rather than assuming guardianship answers every consent question.Ask two separate questions: What authority does the guardian possess? Does another law permit this minor to consent independently to this service?
Comprehensive Minor-Consent Authority Analysis — Part 1 of 2The scenario contains multiple possible consent pathways, none of which should be assumed: The current custody order must be identified. Sole and joint legal custody confer different decision-making authority. Stepparent status and payment do not automatically establish authority. A valid caregiver affidavit or other authorization may be relevant. The minor may have an independent right to consent if the statutory requirements are satisfied. No emergency exists to justify bypassing the nonurgent analysis. 
Comprehensive Minor-Consent Authority Analysis — Part 2 of 2California permits a minor aged 12 or older to consent to qualifying outpatient mental-health services when the attending professional determines that the minor is mature enough to participate intelligently. That possibility must be assessed separately from parental custody authority. The therapist should document the information reviewed, the professional determination, consultation, and the legal and clinical basis for proceeding or postponing treatment.For a complex minor-consent question, use this sequence: Is there an emergency? What is the current custody or guardianship authority? Does another adult possess documented authority? May the minor consent independently? What limitations apply to the specific service? Was the analysis documented and consultation obtained when needed?

Minor Consent Exceptions

BIG IDEA | California Family Code section 6924 and Health and Safety Code section 124260 permit a minor who is 12 years of age or older to consent independently to qualifying outpatient mental-health treatment or counseling when, in the attending professional’s opinion, the minor is mature enough to participate intelligently in the services. A request for confidentiality alone does not establish authority to consent. C. Emancipated minors may possess broader consent rights, but emancipation is not required under these particular minor-consent statutes. D. Independent consent does not depend on the parents being unavailable. The issue is whether the minor satisfies the statutory age and maturity requirements.

Family Code section 3025 protects a parent from denial based solely on noncustodial status. 
Records Created Under Different Consent Arrangements — Part 2 of 3For the independently consented treatment period, Health and Safety Code section 123115 provides that the minor’s representative is not entitled to inspect or copy records relating to qualifying services the minor was legally authorized and sufficiently capable to consent to independently. The therapist should also consider confidential third-party material, verify the scope of the request, document the analysis, and obtain legal consultation when the records cannot be separated confidently. A. The legal basis under which each service was provided directly affects record-access rights. B.

The therapist should protect the minor’s confidentiality and avoid confirming even the existence of the therapeutic relationship when no lawful basis for disclosure has been established. A. The therapist should separately evaluate whether information specifically concerning the sister may be addressed, but her participation does not provide access to the minor’s complete record. B. Dependency Court Order and Continuing Therapist Authority — Part 1 of 3When a minor has been removed through dependency proceedings, a parent ordinarily may not inspect or copy the minor’s mental-health records. A juvenile court may issue an order authorizing access after finding that access would not be detrimental. 
Dependency Court Order and Continuing Therapist Authority — Part 2 of 3However, Health and Safety Code section 123116 expressly preserves the therapist’s authority under section 123115 to deny the parent’s request when access would detrimentally affect: The therapist’s professional relationship with the minor The minor’s physical safety The minor’s psychological well-being The therapist should document the individualized good-faith basis for the decision and obtain legal consultation when necessary. A. The statute expressly preserves the therapist’s section 123115 authority even after the juvenile court authorizes access. B. Parent Request, Minor Request, and Protective Record Procedures — Part 1 of 4The parent’s and minor’s requests involve different statutory rights. The Parent’s Request Family Code section 3025 provides that records cannot be denied solely because a parent is noncustodial. However, Health and Safety Code section 123115 separately provides that a minor’s representative is not entitled to records relating to qualifying services the minor lawfully authorized independently. The independent-consent restriction—not the parent’s noncustodial status—is the basis for denying this request. 
Parent Request, Minor Request, and Protective Record Procedures — Part 2 of 4The Minor’s Request A minor lawfully authorized to consent to the service generally has a right to inspect and obtain the records relating to that type of care. Because the therapist has identified a substantial risk of significant adverse psychological consequences, the therapist may restrict direct access only by following the safeguards in section 123115. 
Parent Request, Minor Request, and Protective Record Procedures — Part 3 of 4These include: Documenting the request Explaining the specific anticipated consequences in the record Informing the minor of the refusal Informing the minor of the right to designate a qualifying licensed professional Providing the records to the licensed professional designated by the minor Documenting whether that option was requested The designated professional may review the records but may not permit the minor to inspect or copy them directly through that process. A. Section 3025 prevents denial based solely on noncustodial status, but it does not override the independent-minor-consent restriction in section 123115. B. 
Parent Request, Minor Request, and Protective Record Procedures — Part 4 of 4The therapist must provide the required explanation and professional-review option rather than imposing an undocumented blanket denial. C. Exam Reasoning Checklist

  • Keep these issues separate: Consent to treatment ≠ participation in treatment ≠ confidentiality ≠ record access A fact establishing one does not automatically decide the others.
  • When the minor consented independently, immediately examine: Who authorized the service—and who holds the corresponding record-access right? Do not assume that the parent’s relationship or payment role resolves the question.
  • Look for the statutory protection factors: Therapeutic relationshipPhysical safetyPsychological well-being The strongest answer uses an individualized, good-faith assessment, not an automatic rule.
  • Do not assume: Noncustodial parent = no access Also do not assume: Parent = automatic access to everything Verify authority and then apply the record-specific exceptions.
  • When records contain information from someone other than the client, ask: Who supplied the information, and was it given in confidence?
  • For parent-update questions, the strongest answer usually avoids both extremes: Tell the parent everythingandTell the parent nothing Clarify the boundaries and disclose only what is properly authorized, required, or clinically appropriate.
  • Connect the rights: Minor may lawfully consent to the service → minor may access records relating to that service
  • Distinguish two different rules: Parent requests minor’s records: Apply section 123115(a)Patient requests own mental-health records: Apply section 123115(b) and its safeguards
