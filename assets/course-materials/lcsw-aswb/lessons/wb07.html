Blueprint Note
For Clinical exams administered on or after August 3, 2026, the ASWB outline assigns 36% to Values and Ethics, 32% to Assessment and Planning, and 32% to Intervention and Practice. Planning also appears across ethical self-determination, referral, case management, advocacy, evaluation, and continuity.

Learning Objectives

  • Convert assessment findings into prioritized, collaborative clinical, functional, resource, and service-access goals that distinguish urgent safety or basic-needs concerns from lower-acuity needs.
  • Apply self-determination and informed choice when clients decline or modify recommendations while recognizing the limits created by immediate danger, capacity concerns, or a stated legal requirement.
  • Select interventions, modalities, community resources, and levels of care based on needs, strengths, development, culture, abilities, evidence, accessibility, feasibility, and client or client-system preferences.
  • Monitor symptoms, functioning, service access, participation, alliance, environmental barriers, and client-defined outcomes and revise the plan when progress is absent.
  • Plan ethical termination, discharge, transfer, and continuity when goals, client choice, eligibility, agency limits, attendance barriers, or program transitions affect care.

Why This Topic Matters

ASWB planning questions test whether the social worker can move from assessment to an ethical and feasible response. A recommendation may be clinically sound yet still be wrong if the client was not involved, the resource is inaccessible, an urgent housing or safety need was ignored, or the plan lacks a way to evaluate progress. The strongest answer usually protects priority, self-determination, access, and continuity at the same time.

2. Person-in-Environment Priority Setting

LCSW planning connects symptoms and functioning to housing, food, income, health care, education, employment, caregiving, discrimination, immigration, disability, transportation, legal systems, community resources, and organizational conditions. The exam often asks whether a basic need or system barrier must be addressed before a lower-acuity intervention can be effective.

Priority LevelExamplesPlanning Direction
ImmediateDanger, medical instability, abuse, severe intoxication or withdrawal, inability to remain safe.Stabilize and activate the appropriate protective or emergency response.
Basic and access needsImminent housing loss, food insecurity, medication interruption, unsafe placement, transportation or language barrier.Assess urgency and connect feasible resources while preserving client choice.
Clinical and functionalDepression, trauma, anxiety, substance use, parenting, relationship, school, or work impairment.Develop measurable clinical and functional goals with appropriate intervention.
Maintenance and transitionRelapse prevention, benefits continuity, aftercare, independent skill use, community support.Strengthen supports and prepare for tapering, transfer, or closure.

3. Self-Determination and Informed Choice

Clients may decline a recommended service, prefer another option, or hold goals different from those of family members, agencies, courts, or providers. The social worker explores the concern, provides understandable information, identifies risks and alternatives, and supports an informed decision unless a legal limitation or immediate safety duty applies. Refusal of one recommendation is not the same as refusal of all help.

Weak ResponseStronger Response
Document noncompliance and close the case.Explore the reason for refusal, identify barriers or prior harms, and collaborate on acceptable alternatives.
Insist that the agency’s standard service is the only option.Explain options, benefits, limits, and risks in language the client can use.
Contact family to persuade the client without authorization.Preserve confidentiality and involve supports with consent or another valid basis.
Assume the client lacks motivation.Assess readiness, stigma, culture, access, cost, transportation, disability, and service fit.

4. Measurable Clinical, Functional, and Service Objectives

Vague StatementMore Useful Planning Language
“Feel better.”Reduce depressive symptom severity and resume two valued weekly activities within eight weeks.
“Find housing.”Complete eligibility screening for two accessible housing programs and identify a transportation plan by the next review.
“Improve functioning.”Attend work at least four days per week and complete essential self-care on six of seven days.
“Use resources.”Contact the selected benefits office with the social worker’s assistance and verify application status within 10 business days.
“Stop using.”Track use, identify high-risk situations, and engage in the agreed treatment or harm-reduction plan with weekly review.
Measurement Rule
A useful plan states what change will count, who will take the action, when it will be reviewed, and what will happen if the plan is not working.

5. Selecting an Intervention or Modality

The best intervention is not automatically the most intensive, the most familiar to the social worker, or the agency default. Selection should fit the client or client system’s needs, abilities, developmental level, culture, life stage, preferences, risk, evidence, goals, and actual access.

Planning TaskQuestions
TriageWhich need is most urgent, and what level of response is required?
Clinical modalityDoes individual, family, couple, group, trauma-focused, substance-use, or another approach fit the formulation and goals?
Resource selectionIs the service available, eligible, accessible, culturally responsive, and acceptable?
CoordinationWho must communicate, what consent is needed, and who follows up?
AdvocacyWhat policy, organizational, financial, language, disability, or discrimination barrier undermines the plan?
TransitionWhat aftercare, transfer, benefits, placement, or community supports preserve continuity?

6. Resource Matching and Warm Handoffs

A referral is not complete when the client receives a phone number. The social worker evaluates fit and helps the client move from recommendation to connection. Depending on setting and consent, this may include confirming eligibility, making a joint call, coordinating records, arranging interpretation or transportation, clarifying costs, and checking whether the service was obtained.

Resource CheckQuestions
AvailabilityIs the program open, accepting referrals, and able to respond within the needed time?
EligibilityDoes the client meet age, diagnosis, income, insurance, location, legal, or functional criteria?
AccessibilityCan the client physically, linguistically, technologically, and cognitively use the service?
AcceptabilityDoes the option fit the client’s goals, culture, safety, prior experiences, and preferences?
LogisticsAre transportation, childcare, work schedule, phone, documentation, and cost barriers addressed?
Follow-throughWho will confirm connection, troubleshoot barriers, and revise the plan if the referral fails?

7. Outcome Monitoring

MonitorQuestions to Ask
Symptoms and riskAre severity, frequency, duration, or safety indicators changing?
FunctioningIs the client functioning differently at home, work, school, or in relationships?
Resource accessDid the client obtain housing, benefits, medication, transportation, placement, or other planned supports?
Service outcomesDid the intervention or referral produce the intended clinical or practical result?
Participation and allianceDoes the client feel understood, included, and able to use the plan?
Environmental barriersHave poverty, discrimination, caregiving, agency policy, or service-system limits changed?

8. When the Plan Is Not Working

No progress can reflect an inaccurate assessment, poor intervention fit, weak alliance, inaccessible resources, cultural mismatch, organizational limits, competing basic needs, side effects, inadequate intensity, or a change in risk. Reassessment should occur before the social worker blames motivation, continues unchanged, or terminates.

  • Review the client’s priorities, new information, risk, diagnosis, strengths, and person-in-environment formulation.
  • Ask whether the client feels understood and whether the goals remain meaningful.
  • Determine whether the intervention or referral was actually available and implemented.
  • Assess transportation, cost, language, disability, caregiving, schedule, stigma, and policy barriers.
  • Modify the plan, advocate, coordinate, consult, refer, or change level of care as indicated.
  • Set a new review point and document the client’s participation and informed choices.
Decision Rule
Reassess before repeating, intensifying, blaming, or ending.

9. Termination, Discharge, and Continuity

Termination decisions incorporate goal attainment, safety, functioning, client choice, eligibility, service limits, resource access, supports, and follow-up. When a program closes or a social worker leaves, the focus shifts to transparent notice, transfer options, consent for information sharing, and reasonable continuity. Missed appointments require attention to risk, barriers, policy, outreach, and documentation before absence is treated as a simple refusal of care.

Termination SituationCore Responsibilities
Goals achievedReview outcomes, consolidate skills, identify future risks, develop aftercare, and process the ending.
Client chooses to stopExplore reasons without coercion, discuss relevant risks and alternatives, and support informed choice.
Agency or program closesProvide timely notice, referrals, transfer options, consent-based information sharing, and continuity efforts.
Higher or different service neededExplain the rationale, coordinate a feasible handoff, and address urgent safety or access needs.
Eligibility or session limit endsDistinguish administrative limits from clinical readiness and develop a transition plan.
Repeated missed visitsUse reasonable outreach, assess barriers and risk when feasible, and provide closure or re-entry information.

Worked Example 1: Depression and Imminent Eviction

A client seeking treatment for depression has an eviction notice and no safe place to stay after the week ends. Cognitive intervention may eventually help, but the first planning task is to assess the immediacy of the housing need and available emergency resources because housing instability affects safety, functioning, and engagement.

Best First Step
Prioritize the urgent housing need while integrating depression treatment into the broader service plan.

Worked Example 2: Client Declines a Group

A client declines a substance-use group because prior groups felt stigmatizing. The strongest response is not to label the client noncompliant or insist on the recommendation. The social worker explores the experience and collaborates on a clinically appropriate alternative the client can use.

Best Next Step
Preserve self-determination, assess the barrier, explain options, and revise the plan collaboratively.

Chapter Summary

  • Planning begins by prioritizing safety, medical stability, basic needs, and the client or client system’s goals before selecting a technique or service.
  • Self-determination requires understandable information, meaningful alternatives, and collaboration; declining one recommendation is not the same as refusing all help.
  • A referral becomes a workable plan only when eligibility, accessibility, acceptability, cost, transportation, language, disability access, and follow-through are addressed.
  • Outcome monitoring should include symptoms, functioning, resource access, participation, alliance, environmental barriers, and client-defined progress.
  • When services are ending, provide transparent notice, assess risk and barriers, coordinate reasonable transfer or referral, obtain consent for information sharing, and document continuity efforts.

My Planning Review Checklist

  • Did I identify the highest-priority need before selecting a technique?
  • Did I connect the intervention to a formulation and a collaborative, observable goal?
  • Did I assess basic needs, social determinants, service-system barriers, and the feasibility of the resource plan?
  • Did I account for readiness, culture, development, resources, access, and level of care?
  • Did I define how symptoms, functioning, relationships, resources, or client-defined outcomes will be monitored?
