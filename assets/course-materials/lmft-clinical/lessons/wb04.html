How to Use This Workbook

This workbook focuses on Clinical Evaluation Tasks T21-T35: social and interpersonal relationships, intimacy and sexuality, family or primary-support dynamics, trauma, loss, aggression, strengths, standardized instruments, consultation, and referral. Work through the instruction first, complete the worksheet, answer all 10 questions without the key, and then study every rationale.

Core Exam Rule
Assessment comes before interpretation or treatment. The best answer usually gathers the missing information, clarifies current safety, maps the relationship pattern, or obtains the specific consultation needed to answer an unresolved clinical question.

Learning Objectives

  • Assess social connection, intimacy, family organization, and recurring interactional patterns without relying on assumptions or taking sides.
  • Use neutral, inclusive, trauma-informed inquiry to evaluate trauma, loss, aggression, coercion, regulation, and present safety.
  • Identify demonstrated coping, resilience, resources, and protective relationships and connect them to the next clinical decision.
  • Distinguish screening, assessment, diagnosis, and specialized testing and select consultation or referral that answers the unresolved question.
  • Apply the RELATE sequence to select the best FIRST or NEXT assessment action in LMFT examination vignettes.

Why This Topic Matters

Relational symptoms rarely make sense in isolation. A partner’s withdrawal, a child’s acting out, a parent’s control, or a client’s social isolation may be a response within a larger cycle. The examination rewards candidates who assess the system, the individual, the context, and current safety before choosing a theory, diagnosis, or intervention.

Memory Aid: RELATE
R — Relationships and social connection. E — Emotional injuries, trauma, and loss. L — Life-support system and family dynamics. A — Aggression, impulsivity, and regulation. T — Tools, testing, consultation, and referral. E — Existing strengths, coping, resilience, and protective factors.

1. Social Connection and Interpersonal Functioning

Assess both the structure and quality of the client’s social world. Ask who is available, how support is requested and received, whether relationships are reciprocal, where conflict occurs, and whether isolation is chosen, imposed, protective, or distressing. A small network is not automatically deficient, and a large network is not automatically supportive.

Assessment DimensionUseful QuestionsExam Significance
Connection and belongingWho feels safe or dependable? Where does the client feel included or excluded?Clarifies support, isolation, minority stress, and protective factors.
Reciprocity and boundariesHow are help, privacy, limits, and responsibility negotiated?Reveals overfunctioning, underfunctioning, enmeshment, distance, or exploitation.
Conflict sequenceWhat happens immediately before, during, and after the conflict?Identifies the interactional cycle rather than a single blamed person.
Past and present patternsWhere else has this pattern occurred, and what is different now?Distinguishes enduring patterns from context-specific reactions.

2. Intimacy and Sexuality Assessment

Ask about intimacy and sexual functioning only when clinically relevant and with clear permission. Use inclusive language and avoid assumptions about identity, relationship structure, sexual practices, reproductive goals, disability, or cultural meaning. Consider consent, desire, arousal, pain, satisfaction, avoidance, conflict, trauma history, medical conditions, medication effects, and changes across the relationship or life cycle.

Trauma-Informed Pacing
Do not press for detailed disclosure to satisfy curiosity. Explain why the question matters, offer choice, ask only what is needed for the current decision, observe distress, and return to stabilization when necessary.

3. Family and Primary-Support System Assessment

A systemic assessment asks how the family or primary support group is organized and how the pattern is maintained. Examine roles, hierarchy, boundaries, alliances, coalitions, triangles, caregiving responsibilities, loyalties, communication rules, developmental transitions, migration or separation, cultural expectations, and patterns across generations. A genogram can organize history, but the clinical task is to understand the living pattern, not merely draw a diagram.

System FeatureWhat to AssessCommon Trap
Roles and hierarchyWho decides, protects, disciplines, mediates, or carries symptoms?Assuming the loudest person has the most power.
BoundariesWhere are limits too diffuse, rigid, or inconsistent?Calling cultural closeness enmeshment without context.
Alliances and trianglesWho joins with whom under stress, and who is left out?Treating an alliance as proof that one person caused the problem.
Life-cycle transitionWhat change is the system trying to absorb?Focusing only on symptoms and missing the transition.
Meaning and cultureWhat does the pattern mean within the family’s identities and context?Using one family norm as the universal standard.

4. Trauma, Abuse, Loss, and Grief

Assessment should establish present safety, the relationship between past experiences and current functioning, trauma reminders, avoidance, arousal, dissociation, relational effects, supports, and coping. With loss, assess the timing and meaning of the loss, cultural and spiritual practices, functioning, support, guilt, trauma exposure, and safety. Do not automatically pathologize grief or assume that disclosure requires immediate detailed processing.

First DetermineThen ExploreAvoid
Current safety and urgent needsImpact on sleep, emotion, relationships, body, functioning, and meaningSeeking a complete trauma narrative before stabilization
Whether abuse or coercion is currentControl, secrecy, threats, dependence, and supportDefaulting to conjoint disclosure when it may increase danger
Nature and timing of lossGrief response, role changes, rituals, support, and impairmentEquating intense grief with a disorder without context

5. Aggression, Impulsivity, Violence, and Regulation

Anger is not the same as violence, and conflict is not the same as coercive control. Assess behavior, pattern, intent, triggers, escalation, access to means, substance involvement, past incidents, injuries, legal involvement, remorse, accountability, protective factors, and the safety of each participant. When risk or coercion is present, safety assessment takes priority over communication training or conjoint confrontation.

Exam Trap
Do not assume that a calm presentation rules out risk, that a history of aggression proves current intent, or that a couple should discuss violence together before individual safety and suitability are assessed.

6. Strengths, Coping, Resilience, and Protective Factors

Strengths assessment is not generic encouragement. Identify what the client or system has actually done, which relationships or values support coping, where exceptions occur, what resources are available, and what has helped during prior stress. Protective factors can reduce risk but do not erase it. Connect strengths to the next clinical task.

Strength DomainExamplesHow It Informs Care
Internal capacitiesInsight, persistence, humor, regulation, problem solvingSupports realistic goals and skill selection.
Relational resourcesSafe adults, partner support, peer community, spiritual communityStrengthens safety and follow-through.
Prior copingHelp-seeking, routines, recovery efforts, successful boundary settingIdentifies usable strategies and exceptions.
Contextual resourcesHousing, transportation, school services, health care, community programsShows what is feasible and where referral is needed.

7. Standardized Instruments and Clinical Judgment

A screening measure estimates whether additional assessment may be needed. It does not automatically establish a diagnosis, level of care, or treatment plan. Before using an instrument, know its purpose, intended population, administration and scoring requirements, limitations, cultural and language fit, and how the result will affect a clinical decision. Integrate results with interview data, observation, collateral information, and context.

QuestionBest Practice
Why use this instrument?To answer a defined assessment question, not because the measure is available.
Is it appropriate?Consider age, language, literacy, disability, culture, setting, and validation population.
What does the score mean?Interpret within the measure’s limits and alongside other data.
What happens next?Clarify whether the result changes assessment, safety planning, consultation, referral, or monitoring.

8. Consultation and Referral for Further Evaluation

Choose the professional whose expertise answers the unresolved question. Continue appropriate LMFT assessment and support while arranging the consultation; do not use referral to avoid clinical responsibility. Explain the purpose, obtain necessary authorization, share relevant information, and follow up on the result.

Unanswered QuestionLikely ResourceExamples
Are symptoms psychiatric, and is medication evaluation indicated?Psychiatrist or qualified psychiatric prescriberSevere mood symptoms, psychosis, complex medication questions.
Could a medical condition explain or worsen symptoms?Physician or medical specialistFainting, endocrine symptoms, neurological change, sleep or pain concerns.
Is specialized psychological or neurocognitive testing needed?Psychologist or qualified specialistCognitive profile, neuropsychological function, complex diagnostic clarification.
What educational or behavioral supports are needed?School-based assessment or servicesLearning, attention, school behavior, accommodations, IEP or related evaluation.
Do multiple systems need coordinated assessment?Multidisciplinary teamCombined medical, developmental, educational, psychiatric, and social needs.

Common Examination Traps

  • Treating one participant’s report as the complete family truth.
  • Interpreting a pattern before clarifying what happens in sequence.
  • Pressing for trauma or sexual detail before explaining relevance and assessing safety.
  • Pathologizing cultural family closeness, nontraditional relationships, disability, or a small social network.
  • Using a screening score as a diagnosis.
  • Referring immediately when the therapist can first gather basic information within competence.
  • Failing to refer when medical, psychiatric, psychological, educational, or multidisciplinary expertise is clearly needed.
  • Listing strengths without connecting them to risk reduction, engagement, or a treatment decision.

Worked Example

A 16-year-old is brought to therapy after school refusal and escalating arguments. One parent describes the adolescent as manipulative; the other privately reports fear of the first parent’s temper. The adolescent says school feels unsafe but refuses to explain in the joint meeting.

1. Assess immediate safety and suitability for joint discussion rather than pressing for disclosure in front of the family.

2. Meet with participants within the established treatment frame to clarify perspectives, coercion, aggression, school context, and the interactional sequence.

3. Assess trauma, loss, social connection, coping, and protective relationships with developmentally appropriate pacing.

4. Determine whether school-based, medical, psychiatric, or multidisciplinary consultation is needed.

5. Formulate the family pattern only after distinguishing current danger, individual concerns, and system-maintained responses.

Why This Sequence Wins
It protects safety, preserves alliance, avoids taking sides, gathers relational and contextual data, and uses consultation only for the question that requires additional expertise.

Chapter Summary

  • Relational assessment examines support, reciprocity, boundaries, roles, hierarchy, alliances, transitions, and the sequence that maintains a problem.
  • Intimacy, sexuality, trauma, loss, and aggression require clinically relevant, consent-based, inclusive, and safety-focused inquiry.
  • Protective factors are observable coping resources and relationships that inform risk assessment, engagement, and planning; they do not erase danger.
  • Screening instruments supplement clinical judgment and must be interpreted within their purpose, population, limitations, and cultural context.
  • When essential information is missing, use RELATE to assess the system, protect safety, and obtain the consultation that answers the specific unresolved question.

Question 1 (LMFT-WB04-Q001)

Correct Answer: A

A. Correct. The question assesses dependability, help-seeking, and the actual function of support.

B. A count does not establish safety, reciprocity, availability, or quality.

C. The wording is potentially judgmental and assumes the preference is problematic.

D. This is leading and prescribes a conclusion before assessment.

Exam Strategy: Assess the pattern, quality, reciprocity, and context of relationships; do not equate living alone or a small network with unhealthy isolation.

Question 2 (LMFT-WB04-Q002)

Correct Answer: B

A. Screening complements rather than replaces interviewing and clinical judgment.

B. Correct. Structured results should be integrated with interview, observation, history, and context.

C. A screening score alone does not establish a final diagnosis.

D. Appropriateness and interpretation require attention to culture, language, disability, and context.

Exam Strategy: Use a measure only when its purpose, population, administration, scoring, limitations, and effect on the clinical decision are clear.

Question 3 (LMFT-WB04-Q003)

Correct Answer: C

A. Minimization can interfere with assessment and is not inherently protective.

B. The absence of reported conflict is not a demonstrated coping resource.

C. Correct. A dependable help-seeking relationship is a concrete protective factor.

D. A therapist’s general impression is less useful than observable behavior and resources.

Exam Strategy: Identify demonstrated coping, relationships, values, resources, and exceptions, then connect them to the current clinical task.

Question 4 (LMFT-WB04-Q004)

Correct Answer: D

A. Accuracy contests and labels do not explain the maintaining cycle.

B. Separation is a decision for the clients and is premature before assessment.

C. Selecting who apologizes first takes sides and bypasses formulation.

D. Correct. Mapping the sequence clarifies how each response influences the next.

Exam Strategy: Ask what happens before, during, and after the conflict and how each response influences the next response.
