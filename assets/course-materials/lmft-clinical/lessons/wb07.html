How to Use This Workbook

Read the workbook in sequence and practice the PLAN-ER and CLOSE memory aids. For each vignette, identify the unit of treatment, the maintaining relational process, the highest-priority target, the collaborative goal, the intervention mechanism, and the outcome indicator. Complete the worksheet and all 10 questions before reviewing the rationales.

Core Exam PrincipleAssessment drives the plan. The plan drives the intervention. Progress data drive revision or termination.
A good treatment plan is not a list of techniques. It is a defensible chain from assessment and systemic formulation to priority, goal, intervention, outcome measure, review, revision, and termination.

Learning Objectives

  • Convert assessment and systemic formulation into prioritized, collaborative short- and long-term goals with observable relational and functional outcomes.
  • Select the appropriate unit of treatment, modality, theoretical mechanism, adjunctive service, referral, or level of care based on formulation, risk, culture, development, resources, and client-system fit.
  • Monitor symptoms, functioning, relational patterns, alliance, implementation, and barriers and use lack of progress as data for reassessment and plan revision.
  • Clarify confidentiality, authorization, role, and information boundaries when payers, employers, courts, schools, or other third parties influence treatment planning.
  • Evaluate readiness for termination and choose an ethical response to goal attainment, dependency, premature ending, transfer, financial barriers, or administrative limits while preserving continuity.

Why This Topic Matters

Planning items often contain several clinically reasonable choices. The best answer is usually the one that occurs at the correct stage and links directly to the assessment. The exam may test whether the therapist clarifies the goal before choosing a technique, repairs the alliance before assigning more work, revises the unit of treatment when new relational data emerge, or evaluates readiness before continuing or ending treatment.

2. Priority and Goal Hierarchy

The treatment plan may contain several goals, but the next action depends on acuity and sequence. Safety and medical stability precede routine relational work. Severe impairment or destabilizing contextual pressures may need early attention because they prevent participation. Once urgent needs are addressed, the therapist organizes symptom, functioning, relationship, developmental, and maintenance goals.

Priority LevelExamplesPlanning Direction
ImmediateDanger, abuse, medical instability, severe intoxication or withdrawal, inability to remain safe.Stabilize and activate the required protective or emergency response.
FoundationalEngagement rupture, unclear unit of treatment, conflicting expectations, housing or access barrier affecting attendance.Clarify frame, repair alliance, or address the barrier before complex intervention.
Primary clinicalPanic avoidance, depressive withdrawal, repeated couple escalation, parenting conflict, trauma symptoms.Define the target and select a formulation-linked intervention.
Maintenance and growthRelapse prevention, communication consolidation, autonomy, future developmental transitions.Strengthen independent use of gains and prepare for tapering or termination.

3. Collaborative and Measurable Goals

Goals are strongest when they use the client or family’s language, identify observable or reportable change, and describe how progress will be reviewed. Collaboration does not require accepting unsafe or clinically unsupported requests. It requires transparency, meaningful participation, and a plan the client system understands.

Vague StatementMore Useful Planning Language
“Communicate more.”Use a 10-minute structured check-in twice weekly without interruption, criticism, or withdrawal.
“Stop fighting.”Recognize the escalation sequence and use an agreed pause-and-return process in four of five conflicts.
“Be less anxious.”Reduce panic-related avoidance enough to drive to work three days per week within eight weeks.
“Improve parenting.”Parents maintain a coordinated response to limit-setting in at least four of five identified situations.
“Feel closer.”Each partner directly expresses one primary emotion and one attachment need during weekly check-ins.
Goal Rule
The plan should make the desired change clear enough that the client system and therapist can recognize progress without guessing.

4. Unit of Treatment and Systemic Formulation

LMFT planning identifies who must participate for change to occur and what interactional process maintains the problem. The initial referral may name one person, but assessment may show that couple, family, parent, or individual work is more appropriate. New information can justify revisiting the unit of treatment rather than treating the original arrangement as fixed.

Planning QuestionSystemic Focus
Who is the client or client system?Clarify the treatment agreement, participants, confidentiality frame, and who shares the goals.
What pattern maintains the problem?Track sequences, roles, boundaries, coalitions, hierarchy, attachment responses, reinforcement, and feedback loops.
What observable change is expected?Name the interaction, boundary, role, communication, or independent functioning that should change.
Who must practice the change?Identify each participant’s action rather than assigning one person responsibility for the whole system.

Match the plan to the theory’s mechanism of change, not merely to a technique whose name you recognize.

6. Modality, Level of Care, and Third-Party Limits

Intervention selection reflects the formulation, evidence, development, culture, abilities, preferences, risks, resources, setting, and availability. The therapist also considers whether individual, couple, family, group, psychiatric, medical, school, residential, or other services are needed. When a payer, employer, court, school, or referral source is involved, role and information boundaries must be clear before the plan is finalized.

QuestionExam Use
Does conjoint treatment remain safe and clinically appropriate?Do not preserve a relational format when coercion, violence, intimidation, or acute instability makes it unsafe.
Is the intervention within competence and scope?Consult, obtain training, or refer when the needed service exceeds competence.
Does the client system understand third-party limits?Clarify purpose, confidentiality, authorization, reports, and who receives information.
Is a higher or different level of care required?Explain the clinical rationale, facilitate the referral, and support continuity rather than simply ending.

7. Outcome Measures and Progress Monitoring

MonitorQuestions to Ask
Symptoms and riskAre severity, frequency, duration, or safety indicators changing?
FunctioningIs the client functioning differently at home, work, school, or in relationships?
Relational patternAre escalation, withdrawal, coalition, boundary, attachment, or parenting patterns changing?
AllianceDo all clients feel understood, included, safe, and aligned with goals and method?
ImplementationAre interventions being attempted, and what barriers affect follow-through?
FitDoes the formulation still explain the problem, and is the modality producing useful change?
Decision Rule
Outcome monitoring begins when the plan is written, not when treatment is ending.

8. When Treatment Is Not Working

Lack of progress is information, not proof of resistance or failure. Reassess diagnosis or formulation, risk, goals, readiness, alliance, cultural fit, external barriers, intervention implementation, frequency, level of care, and the need for consultation or referral. Continuing unchanged and terminating immediately are opposite errors when reassessment has not occurred.

  • Review whether the original assessment and systemic formulation still fit.
  • Ask each client how the alliance, goals, pace, and method are working.
  • Identify practical, cultural, developmental, or relational barriers to implementation.
  • Determine whether the intervention was delivered adequately and at an appropriate intensity.
  • Modify the plan, repair the alliance, consult, refer, or change level of care as indicated.
  • Document the new information, collaborative decision, and next review point.

9. Termination as a Clinical Process

Termination may occur because goals are achieved, the client chooses to stop, the therapist cannot continue, a higher or different service is needed, treatment is not beneficial, or an administrative limit ends care. The therapist evaluates readiness, discusses the meaning of ending, reviews gains and remaining concerns, anticipates relapse or future stress, identifies supports and referrals, clarifies emergency or re-entry options, and documents the process.

Termination SituationCore Responsibilities
Goals achievedReview outcomes, consolidate skills, identify future risks, develop aftercare, and process the ending.
Client chooses to stopExplore reasons without coercion, explain relevant risks, offer options and referrals, and document.
Therapist or agency ends servicesProvide reasonable notice when possible, support transfer or referral, and protect continuity.
Higher or different care neededExplain the rationale, support a warm handoff, and address urgent safety needs.
Dependency concernsExplore the meaning of ending, reinforce autonomous coping, taper when appropriate, and avoid unnecessary continuation.
Repeated missed contactUse reasonable outreach consistent with risk and policy, assess barriers when possible, and provide closure information.

Worked Example 1: Child Referral, Parent Conflict

Parents bring a child for school refusal. Assessment shows escalating parental conflict about discipline, and the child becomes symptomatic after these disputes. A weak plan treats only the child’s anxiety. A stronger systemic plan revisits the unit of treatment, clarifies parental goals, and targets the conflict and boundary pattern maintaining the symptom.

Best Planning Move
Revise the formulation and include parent or family work as clinically indicated before selecting a child-only intervention.

Worked Example 2: No Progress After Eight Sessions

A family attends consistently but reports no improvement and rarely completes between-session tasks. The therapist should not automatically continue, intensify homework, or terminate. The next step is to reassess the formulation, alliance, barriers, readiness, and fit of the interventions with the family.

Best Next Step
Use PLAN-ER: evaluate what the lack of progress means, then collaboratively revise the plan.

Chapter Summary

  • Begin with assessment and systemic formulation, then translate the highest-priority need into collaborative and measurable goals.
  • The unit of treatment may change when new relational information shows that couple, family, parent, or individual participation is needed for change.
  • Match interventions to the theory, maintaining process, developmental and cultural context, client-system preferences, evidence, resources, and level of care.
  • When progress is absent, reassess formulation, alliance, barriers, implementation, and treatment fit before repeating the same plan or terminating.
  • Termination is a clinical process that reviews gains, risk, supports, relapse prevention, referrals, transfer, and continuity rather than a single final session.

My Planning Review Checklist
