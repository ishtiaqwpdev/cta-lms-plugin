CASE 1 — MAYA R.
Controlled metadata: 10 scored-style + 3 field-test-style | Final key: CADBCBBAABBCA
SECTION 1 — INITIAL PRESENTATION
Maya is a 29-year-old bilingual Latina elementary-school teacher. During the past eight weeks, she has experienced six sudden episodes of intense fear accompanied by racing heart, chest tightness, trembling, shortness of breath, dizziness, and a fear that she might faint or lose control. The symptoms reach peak intensity within several minutes and then gradually subside.
Maya has twice gone to an emergency department because she feared she was having a medical emergency. Those evaluations did not identify an acute cardiac or respiratory condition. She has been advised to obtain follow-up medical care if symptoms change or new warning signs occur.
Maya now avoids a section of freeway where one episode occurred. On some mornings she takes surface streets instead, making her late for work. She has also asked her sister to drive her on several occasions.
Maya usually sleeps about five hours on work nights. She drinks energy drinks most mornings and sometimes again in the afternoon. She says they help her “push through” fatigue.
Maya worries that relatives may think counseling means she is weak. She also asks, “Can my principal or the school district find out that I'm seeing a therapist?” The counseling practice is independent of Maya's employer, and Maya has not authorized disclosure of counseling information to her employer.
Q1. Which follow-up inquiry would MOST directly clarify the diagnostic significance of Maya's episodes?
A. Review the emergency evaluations, family cardiac history, medications, sleep, exercise, and any physical symptoms occurring between episodes.
B. Explore freeway avoidance, work lateness, sister involvement, other avoided settings, and what Maya does to prevent another episode.
C. Clarify whether episodes are unexpected or consistently cued, what happens during them, their duration, recurrence concerns, behavioral changes, and substance timing.
D. Assess generalized worry, social-evaluative fear, trauma symptoms, depressive symptoms, prior counseling, and family history of anxiety conditions.
Why the keyed answer is best: The most discriminating information is the phenomenology and chronology of the attacks—whether they are unexpected or consistently cued, what occurs during them, whether concern persists afterward, what behavior changes follow, and how substance exposure relates temporally. Panic disorder is characterized by recurrent unexpected panic attacks with persistent worry or behavior change; the presence of panic symptoms by itself is not sufficient.
Why A is less appropriate: is clinically appropriate and would become more important if new medical red flags appeared, but it does not resolve the central panic-versus-cued/substance diagnostic question as directly.
Why B is less appropriate: is important for impairment and avoidance but presumes the attacks have already been sufficiently characterized.
Why D is less appropriate: is a useful broader differential assessment but is less direct than establishing the attack pattern itself.
Transfer rule: First characterize the episodes themselves; then decide what diagnostic category best explains them.
Q2. How should the counselor respond to Maya's concern that her employer may learn she is in counseling?
A. Explain the counselor's confidentiality obligations and limits, clarify that no employer disclosure is currently authorized, and review any relevant record or payment arrangements with Maya.
B. Explain that counseling records are private health information and therefore cannot be shared with an employer unless Maya personally requests the disclosure.
C. Clarify that the practice is separate from Maya's employer and defer detailed confidentiality discussion unless the employer later requests documentation or workplace accommodation.
D. Explain that employer access depends primarily on Maya's insurance plan, so she should first ask the insurer whether counseling information appears in employment records.
Why the keyed answer is best: The counselor should explain confidentiality and its limits based on the actual counseling relationship and clarify relevant record/payment arrangements rather than make an absolute promise. The ACA Code requires counselors to discuss confidentiality and its limitations with clients and recognizes additional privacy/security considerations when technology or records are involved.
Why B is less appropriate: is too absolute; confidentiality has recognized ethical and legal limitations.
Why C is less appropriate: unnecessarily delays a question that Maya is asking now.
Why D is less appropriate: may identify one relevant administrative issue in some cases, but it makes insurance the primary determinant of confidentiality without case evidence supporting that assumption.
Transfer rule: Answer confidentiality questions from the actual consent, record, and disclosure structure—not from blanket promises.
Q3. Maya says, “In my family, going to counseling can look like you can't handle your own problems.” Which response BEST demonstrates empathic responding?
A. “Family attitudes toward counseling can be powerful. We can identify ways to keep treatment private while you become more comfortable with receiving help.”
B. “Many families value self-reliance, so counseling can feel inconsistent with those expectations even when it is clinically useful.”
C. “You can respect your family and still decide that counseling is right for you. We can work on separating their beliefs from your needs.”
D. “It sounds like seeking help may carry shame or conflict for you. What does counseling mean in your family, and what does it mean to you?”
Why the keyed answer is best: Maya is expressing possible shame and conflict between receiving help and family meanings around self-reliance. D reflects that experience and invites Maya's own meaning rather than immediately normalizing, correcting, or solving it. Empathic responding is a distinct current NCMHCE Core Counseling Attribute.
Why A is less appropriate: recognizes the possible family influence but moves quickly toward privacy strategy.
Why B is less appropriate: offers plausible normalization but generalizes about families rather than staying with Maya's experience.
Why C is less appropriate: supports autonomy but begins separating Maya from her family's beliefs before understanding what those beliefs mean to her.
Transfer rule: Empathy starts by understanding meaning before trying to modify it.
Q4. Which inquiry would provide the MOST useful assessment of Maya's energy-drink use?
A. Determine whether Maya exceeds recommended daily caffeine amounts, then ask whether she is willing to stop energy drinks during the diagnostic assessment.
B. Clarify products, caffeine and other stimulant content, amount, timing, purpose, sleep effects, attack timing, lower-use periods, and previous attempts to reduce.
C. Compare panic symptoms on workdays and weekends, using days without energy drinks to determine whether caffeine is producing the episodes.
D. Focus on the afternoon energy drink because stimulant use later in the day is most likely to explain Maya's short sleep and morning vulnerability.
Why the keyed answer is best: Caffeine is a psychoactive stimulant and can contribute to anxiety, palpitations, sleep problems, and related symptoms in susceptible individuals. A clinically useful assessment therefore needs product content, amount, timing, function, sleep effects, symptom chronology, other stimulant exposure, and lower-use periods rather than simply counting beverages.
Why A is less appropriate: could be part of shared decision-making after the pattern is known, but it moves from assessment to a specific change before establishing Maya's actual exposure.
Why C is less appropriate: offers useful naturalistic comparison but risks treating workday/weekend differences as proof of causation.
Why D is less appropriate: appropriately considers sleep effects but prematurely identifies afternoon use as the primary mechanism.
Transfer rule: Assess dose, timing, function, consequences, and symptom chronology before assigning causation to a substance.
SECTION 2 — ADDITIONAL INFORMATION
Further assessment shows that Maya's first three attacks were not consistently tied to one setting. One occurred while grading papers at home, one while shopping, and one while driving. She reports that the attacks seemed to occur “out of nowhere.” Later attacks became more likely when she anticipated driving on the freeway or noticed bodily sensations resembling an earlier episode.
For more than six weeks, Maya has worried about having another attack and has altered her commuting behavior. She says her greatest fear on the freeway is, “What if it happens and I can't get off the road?”
Maya reports no persistent fear of scrutiny in ordinary social or professional situations. She teaches in front of groups, attends social gatherings, shops alone, and speaks at school meetings without a general fear of embarrassment or judgment. She does not report trauma-related intrusions, compulsions, mania, or pervasive excessive worry across multiple areas of life.
A primary-care clinician reviews the prior emergency evaluations and completes additional follow-up without finding a current medical explanation for the episodes.
Maya had been drinking two energy drinks containing approximately 180 mg of caffeine each on most work mornings and occasionally a third caffeinated drink in the afternoon. Under medical guidance, she substantially reduces her caffeine intake for three weeks. Her sleep improves and she feels less physically “jittery,” but she experiences another unexpected panic episode during a weekend when she had consumed little caffeine.
Maya prefers English for counseling but says that when talking about family expectations she sometimes finds Spanish words easier for expressing exactly what she means.
A baseline standardized panic-severity measure shows substantial symptoms and interference. Maya says her priorities are to drive to work normally, stop organizing her day around fear of another episode, and sleep more consistently.
Q5. Which measurement plan would BEST support ongoing assessment?
A. Use a general anxiety scale at each visit and add a panic-specific measure only when Maya reports an attack between sessions.
B. Alternate panic, anxiety, and functioning measures across sessions so each major component of Maya's presentation receives equal measurement attention.
C. Repeat the same appropriate panic-severity measure at planned intervals and pair it with avoidance, commuting, work-function, sleep, and attack data.
D. Track attack frequency and freeway use weekly, reserving formal measures for the end of treatment so repeated testing does not influence Maya's responses.
Why the keyed answer is best: Repeated use of an appropriate comparable measure supports longitudinal interpretation, while behavioral and functional data keep the score from becoming the entire formulation. The current NCMHCE explicitly distinguishes selecting/interpreting assessment instruments from using outcome data more broadly.
Why A is less appropriate: changes what is measured depending on whether Maya reports an attack and can weaken comparability.
Why B is less appropriate: samples several domains but alternates measures in a way that makes longitudinal interpretation harder.
Why D is less appropriate: provides valuable weekly behavioral data but unnecessarily delays formal repeated measurement.
Transfer rule: Use standardized measurement consistently, but interpret it alongside functioning and behavior.
Q6. Which diagnosis is BEST supported by the information now available?
A. Caffeine-induced anxiety disorder because Maya's high stimulant intake preceded the attacks and contributed to sleep disruption and autonomic symptoms.
B. Panic disorder because Maya has recurrent unexpected attacks followed by persistent concern and behavior change despite symptoms occurring independently of heavy caffeine use.
C. Generalized anxiety disorder because Maya experiences recurrent worry, physical tension, sleep problems, and occupational interference over several weeks.
D. Social anxiety disorder because fear of having visible symptoms in public has led Maya to alter commuting and work-related behavior.
Why the keyed answer is best: Maya now has recurrent unexpected panic attacks plus more than a month of persistent concern and meaningful behavioral change. NIMH describes that pattern as characteristic of panic disorder. Her attack during a low-caffeine period and persistence of the syndrome after substantial caffeine reduction make caffeine a plausible exacerbating factor but not the most supported sole explanation.
Why A is less appropriate: is competitive because caffeine can contribute to anxiety and sleep disturbance, but the chronology does not adequately support caffeine as the sole cause.
Why C is less appropriate: is less supported because Maya does not show pervasive difficult-to-control worry across multiple life domains.
Why D is less appropriate: is less supported because Maya functions in numerous social and professional evaluative settings without the broader scrutiny-centered fear characteristic of social anxiety.
Transfer rule: A panic attack is a symptom; panic disorder requires the broader longitudinal syndrome.
Q7. Which CBT plan BEST fits Maya's current presentation?
A. Begin with cognitive restructuring and controlled breathing until Maya can reliably reduce panic sensations, then introduce exposure to feared situations.
B. Combine panic psychoeducation, interoceptive practice, graded in-vivo exposure, and reduction of safety behaviors while monitoring functional progress collaboratively.
C. Focus first on sleep and caffeine reduction, adding panic-focused exposure only if attacks and avoidance remain after those factors have stabilized.
D. Begin in-vivo freeway practice while Maya uses breathing and reassurance from her sister to remain in the situation long enough for anxiety to decrease.
Why the keyed answer is best: Panic-focused CBT commonly targets catastrophic interpretation of bodily sensations and avoidance through cognitive/behavioral work and exposure. In Maya's case, both internal sensations and freeway avoidance are active, while pulse checking and reassurance can function as safety behaviors that limit corrective learning. The treatment plan therefore needs to address both the sensations and the avoided context rather than making symptom elimination a prerequisite for exposure.
Why A is less appropriate: includes legitimate CBT tools but makes reliable reduction of panic sensations a condition for exposure.
Why C is less appropriate: appropriately addresses modifiable sleep/caffeine factors but postpones the active panic-avoidance cycle despite evidence that it persists independently.
Why D is less appropriate: begins useful in-vivo practice but retains reassurance and breathing as conditions for tolerating the exposure.
Transfer rule: Match the intervention to the mechanisms maintaining the disorder, not merely to the symptom that feels easiest to change first.
Q8. Which inquiry BEST demonstrates multicultural sensitivity to Maya's concern about family attitudes toward counseling?
A. Ask Maya how language, family expectations, self-reliance, help-seeking, and her own cultural experiences shape what counseling means to her.
B. Ask whether Maya's relatives hold traditional views about mental health and whether those views create enough stigma to interfere with treatment.
C. Explore whether speaking Spanish about family issues would make counseling feel more culturally congruent, then use Spanish whenever those subjects arise.
D. Ask which family members are most supportive of counseling so treatment can rely on culturally familiar relationships when stress increases.
Why the keyed answer is best: A opens several potentially relevant cultural and linguistic domains while allowing Maya to decide what each means to her. It avoids assuming either that her family is stigmatizing or that Spanish is automatically the preferred therapeutic language. The current NCMHCE explicitly tests multicultural sensitivity.
Why B is less appropriate: raises a reasonable hypothesis but frames “traditional views” and treatment interference before Maya has defined the family meaning.
Why C is less appropriate: appropriately notices Maya's language experience but turns occasional Spanish expression into an assumed treatment-language rule.
Why D is less appropriate: may support later resource planning but starts by identifying “supportive” family members before clarifying whether Maya wants family involvement at all.
Transfer rule: Cultural responsiveness asks what language, family, and identity mean to the client rather than predicting their meaning from demographic membership.
Q9. Maya asks what her baseline panic score means for treatment. Which response BEST uses the result to facilitate decision-making?
A. Explain what the score measures, compare it with Maya's reported impairment, and use both to decide collaboratively which symptoms and functional goals to prioritize.
B. Explain that the score confirms panic disorder and can now be used to determine how quickly Maya should progress through exposure exercises.
C. Explain that the score indicates substantial severity and therefore supports beginning the full standard panic-treatment protocol without further target selection.
D. Explain that the score is mainly useful for comparing treatment outcomes later and should not influence current decisions because Maya's goals already determine treatment.
Why the keyed answer is best: An assessment result contributes information about current severity; it does not independently make the diagnosis, set treatment intensity, or determine the exposure schedule. Using the result alongside Maya's own impairment and goals turns measurement into shared treatment planning rather than a command. NBCC explicitly includes using assessment results to facilitate client decision-making.
Why B is less appropriate: treats a symptom score as diagnostic confirmation and a dosing rule for exposure.
Why C is less appropriate: uses the result too prescriptively.
Why D is less appropriate: goes too far the other direction by claiming current treatment decisions should not be influenced by useful severity data.
Transfer rule: Assessment data inform collaborative decisions; they do not make those decisions by themselves.
SECTION 3 — COURSE OF CARE
Eight weeks later, Maya has continued the reduced-caffeine pattern and usually sleeps about seven hours per night. She reports one panic attack during the past three weeks compared with two to three attacks per week before treatment.
Maya now drives to work regularly and has stopped missing work because of panic. She has completed several interoceptive and in-vivo exercises.
One freeway segment remains difficult. Maya can drive it but usually exits before crossing a long elevated interchange. When she attempts the interchange, she keeps a bottle of cold water in her lap, repeatedly checks her pulse at her smartwatch, and sometimes calls her sister on Bluetooth “just in case.”
With Maya's written permission, her sister attended one session to learn how to support practice without repeatedly reassuring Maya that nothing bad will happen.
Maya says, “I'm doing much better, but that interchange still feels like the final test.”
She also asks whether some evening sessions can occur by video from a private room in her home. Maya and the counselor will be located in the same jurisdiction for those sessions. She has reliable internet and a private device.
Q10. Which next-phase treatment objective is BEST matched to Maya's current progress?
A. Eliminate panic symptoms for four consecutive weeks before Maya attempts the elevated interchange without her sister available for support.
B. Complete agreed graded interchange practices while progressively reducing pulse checking and reassurance behaviors and maintaining normal work attendance.
C. Reduce panic-severity scores into the minimal range while maintaining normal work attendance before decreasing smartwatch and reassurance use.
D. Drive the interchange independently on every workday for two weeks, using coping tools as needed and recording peak anxiety after each attempt.
Why the keyed answer is best: The remaining impairment is behavioral and specific: Maya still avoids the interchange and relies on safety behaviors. D creates a collaborative, observable next-phase objective without requiring complete symptom elimination or using a score threshold as a prerequisite.
Why A is less appropriate: makes absence of panic a gate for behavioral progress.
Why C is less appropriate: makes a score threshold control when functional work proceeds.
Why D is less appropriate: is behavioral but imposes a rigid daily schedule and allows unspecified coping tools that may include maintaining safety behaviors.
Transfer rule: A good objective targets the actual remaining impairment in observable terms without making symptom perfection the prerequisite.
Q11. Which plan BEST addresses Maya's request for evening telehealth?
A. Convert evening sessions to video because Maya has stable internet, a private room, and improving symptoms, documenting the modality change in the treatment plan.
B. Review telehealth consent, privacy/security, Maya's session location, backup communication and emergency procedures, clinical appropriateness, and how effectiveness will be monitored.
C. Use video only for psychoeducation and review sessions while continuing all exposure-related counseling in person so treatment quality is not reduced.
D. Alternate video and in-person sessions for a trial period and add telehealth-specific consent only if Maya prefers to continue using video after the trial.
Why the keyed answer is best: The ACA Code's distance-counseling standards address informed consent, risks and benefits, technology failure, response times, emergency procedures, privacy/security, client location/identity, appropriateness of technology-assisted services, and ongoing effectiveness.
Why A is less appropriate: identifies several positive feasibility factors but treats them as sufficient without updating the telehealth-specific consent and emergency/privacy framework.
Why C is less appropriate: assumes exposure-related counseling inherently requires in-person care, which the case does not establish.
Why D is less appropriate: permits the modality before completing the applicable informed-consent process.
Transfer rule: Telehealth appropriateness is a clinical, ethical, privacy, technology, and emergency-planning decision—not a convenience decision alone.
Q12. Which strategy BEST addresses Maya's remaining difficulty with the freeway interchange?
A. Increase the length and intensity of interchange exposure until Maya can remain on the route long enough for anxiety to fall substantially each time.
B. Practice the interchange with her sister in the passenger seat while eliminating smartwatch checking, then gradually remove her sister once anxiety decreases.
C. Use a graded plan targeting the interchange while systematically reducing pulse checking, reassurance calls, and other safety behaviors that may limit new learning.
D. Continue easier freeway routes until Maya experiences no unexpected panic attacks for several weeks, then return to the elevated interchange.
Why the keyed answer is best: Maya can already enter the freeway environment; the remaining pattern is concentrated at one interchange and includes repeated pulse checking and reassurance. A graded strategy that targets the specific route while reducing those safety behaviors most directly addresses the residual learning problem.
Why A is less appropriate: assumes that longer or more intense exposure is the necessary correction and makes anxiety reduction during each exposure the marker of success.
Why B is less appropriate: removes one safety behavior while preserving another substantial reassurance/support condition.
Why D is less appropriate: returns to symptom-absence logic and postpones the remaining functional target.
Transfer rule: When progress plateaus, examine what the client is still doing to prevent the feared outcome before simply increasing treatment intensity.
Q13. Which pre/post outcome approach provides the STRONGEST evidence of meaningful treatment change?
A. Repeat the same panic measure and compare it with baseline attack frequency, freeway behavior, work functioning, sleep, caffeine pattern, and remaining safety behaviors.
B. Compare the final panic score with the baseline score and use a clinically meaningful score reduction as the primary indicator that treatment succeeded.
C. Compare attack frequency and work attendance before and after treatment, using Maya's satisfaction with counseling to interpret whether residual avoidance remains important.
D. Use a new comprehensive anxiety measure at the end of treatment so the final assessment captures symptoms that may not have appeared on the original panic measure.
Why the keyed answer is best: A uses the same measure for actual pre/post comparison and integrates it with behavioral, functional, sleep, substance, and residual safety-behavior data. That provides stronger evidence than either symptom scoring alone or substituting a new instrument at the end. The NCMHCE explicitly includes use of pre-test/post-test measures to assess outcomes.
Why B is less appropriate: preserves comparability but reduces success mainly to the scale score.
Why C is less appropriate: includes meaningful behavior and functioning but omits standardized pre/post comparison.
Why D is less appropriate: broadens the final assessment at the cost of direct comparability with baseline.
Transfer rule: Pre/post measurement is strongest when the same measure is interpreted alongside real-world functioning.

CASE 2 — JORDAN K.
Controlled metadata: 9 scored-style + 4 field-test-style | Final key: CCDBABACCABDC
SECTION 1 — INITIAL PRESENTATION
Jordan is a 22-year-old nonbinary college student. For approximately six weeks, they have felt depressed most days, lost interest in activities they previously enjoyed, slept poorly, experienced persistent guilt, and had increasing difficulty concentrating on coursework. Their grades have declined, and they have missed several classes.
Jordan recently came out to family members as nonbinary. Some relatives responded critically and have reduced contact. Jordan says the rejection has been painful and has contributed to feeling isolated.
Jordan reports drinking alcohol on some weekends but has not yet described the quantity, pattern, or relationship between drinking and mood.
Near the end of the intake, Jordan says quietly, “Sometimes I think everyone would be better off without me.” When the counselor asks what Jordan means, they look down and say, “I don't really want to get into it.”
Jordan reports no known history of a manic or psychotic episode.
Q14. Which inquiry would MOST directly resolve the immediate safety uncertainty?
A. Clarify the severity of depressed mood, anhedonia, guilt, sleep disruption, concentration problems, appetite changes, and academic impairment over the six-week period.
B. Explore the family rejection, current housing and financial dependence, available affirming relationships, isolation, and whether family contact is improving or worsening.
C. Ask directly about suicidal thoughts, possible methods, intent, planning or preparation, access to means, prior suicidal behavior, protective factors, and usable supports.
D. Clarify alcohol quantity, intoxication, blackouts, mood changes while drinking, other substance use, and whether weekend drinking increases impulsivity or hopelessness.
Why the keyed answer is best: Jordan's statement raises immediate uncertainty about suicidal thinking, so direct inquiry must clarify ideation, possible method, intent, planning or preparation, means access, previous behavior, protective factors, and available supports. SAFE-T similarly separates identification of risk/protective factors, direct suicide inquiry, risk formulation/intervention, and documentation rather than allowing one passive statement or one denial to determine the whole picture.
Why A is less appropriate: is necessary for the depressive formulation but does not directly resolve the suicide-related statement.
Why B is less appropriate: addresses an important potential driver and may materially affect risk, but it still leaves current suicidal thinking unknown.
Why D is less appropriate: is important because intoxication can affect safety and judgment, but the alcohol history does not replace direct suicide inquiry.
Transfer rule: When a client makes an ambiguous death-related statement, clarify the suicidal content directly before relying on explanations for why the statement occurred.
Q15. Jordan says, “Maybe they're right. Maybe everyone would be better off if they didn't have to deal with me.” Which response BEST demonstrates empathic attunement?
A. “You deserve support regardless of your family's response. Let's identify people who can remind you that you are valued when thoughts like this occur.”
B. “Family rejection can create minority stress, and it may help to separate their reaction from the way you understand your own identity and worth.”
C. “Being rejected by people whose acceptance matters to you seems to have intensified the sense that you're a burden. I want to understand that with you.”
D. “That sounds painful, and because you're describing yourself as a burden, I want to return immediately to the suicide assessment before exploring the family situation.”
Why the keyed answer is best: Jordan has linked interpersonal rejection with a growing sense of burdensomeness. A reflects both dimensions without assuming that identity alone causes the depression or shifting prematurely to education, resource planning, or further assessment.
Why A is less appropriate: is supportive and may later strengthen safety, but it answers the pain with reassurance and resource activation before fully understanding it.
Why B is less appropriate: contains a plausible formulation, but it moves from Jordan's personal experience to the counselor's conceptual language about minority stress.
Why D is less appropriate: appropriately recognizes the safety significance of burdensomeness but leaves the relational meaning unaddressed.
Transfer rule: Empathic attunement can recognize the meaning of a risk-related statement without confusing empathy with the full safety assessment.
Q16. Which inquiry would provide the MOST clinically useful assessment of Jordan's weekend alcohol use?
A. Determine whether Jordan's drinking meets a binge-drinking threshold, then use that result to decide whether alcohol treatment should precede depression treatment.
B. Compare Jordan's mood and suicidal thinking on drinking versus nondrinking weekends, then determine whether alcohol is causing the depressive presentation.
C. Explore what Jordan likes and dislikes about drinking, how friends respond to it, and whether Jordan would be willing to reduce use during treatment.
D. Clarify quantity, frequency, pace, intoxication, consequences, coping function, other substances, change attempts, and whether drinking coincides with worsening mood or suicidal thinking.
Why the keyed answer is best: Current alcohol assessment should establish pattern, quantity, frequency, intoxication, consequences, function, other substance exposure, change attempts, and temporal relationship to psychiatric symptoms. Current NIAAA guidance likewise emphasizes quantity/frequency screening and further assessment of alcohol-related health and safety effects rather than inferring an alcohol-use disorder from the phrase “drinks on weekends.”
Why A is less appropriate: could be useful as one screening step, but crossing a drinking threshold would not by itself determine diagnostic priority.
Why B is less appropriate: examines an important relationship between alcohol and mood but prematurely asks whether alcohol is *causing* the depressive syndrome.
Why C is less appropriate: appropriately explores function and motivation but does not adequately characterize severity or safety.
Transfer rule: Assess drinking pattern, consequences, function, and psychiatric timing before deciding whether alcohol is causal, co-occurring, or incidental.
Q17. Which conclusion BEST reflects Jordan's current level of mental health functioning?
A. Functioning remains largely intact because Jordan continues attending college, has not withdrawn completely, and can participate coherently in the counseling intake.
B. Jordan shows meaningful impairment in academic performance, sleep, concentration, interest, and social connection while retaining some daily functioning and treatment engagement.
C. Functioning is severely compromised because declining grades and a death-related statement indicate that Jordan can no longer safely manage ordinary college responsibilities.
D. The level of functioning cannot be evaluated until a definitive depressive diagnosis and complete suicide-risk formulation have both been established.
Why the keyed answer is best: Jordan retains meaningful capacities—college enrollment, treatment participation, communication, and some social connection—but also shows clear deterioration in grades, attendance, sleep, concentration, interest, and connection. The most defensible conclusion therefore recognizes substantial impairment without treating continued participation in college as proof of intact functioning or suicidal language as proof of global incapacity.
Why A is less appropriate: underestimates the breadth of the deterioration.
Why C is less appropriate: overstates the functional implications of the current evidence and collapses safety concern into a conclusion about all ordinary functioning.
Why D is less appropriate: incorrectly assumes functioning cannot be assessed until diagnostic and risk labels are finalized.
Transfer rule: Evaluate functioning across domains and against baseline; do not use either one preserved role or one serious symptom as the entire functional assessment.
SECTION 2 — ADDITIONAL INFORMATION
With direct inquiry, Jordan reports intermittent thoughts of overdosing on an old prescription approximately two or three times during the past two weeks. They deny current intent, rehearsal, or preparatory behavior and say they have not taken the medication out during these episodes. The unused medication remains accessible in a desk drawer.
Jordan reports no previous suicide attempt. Two years ago, after a relationship breakup, Jordan made several superficial cuts to an arm. Jordan states that the purpose was to reduce emotional pressure rather than to die and reports no recurrence since that time.
Jordan identifies a close friend as someone they can contact and says the friend has responded reliably in past crises. Jordan also attends activities at the campus LGBTQ+ center but says, “It's a good place for me, but I don't want people assuming it's automatically my whole support system.”
Further assessment shows depressed mood and loss of interest most days, insomnia, low energy, reduced appetite, difficulty concentrating, excessive guilt and worthlessness, and suicidal thoughts. The symptoms have been present across settings for approximately six weeks and impair school and social functioning.
Jordan describes no history of sustained elevated or expansive mood, reduced need for sleep accompanied by increased energy, grandiosity, or distinct periods of markedly increased goal-directed behavior. Their depressive symptoms occur on days when they do not drink alcohol as well as on drinking days.
Jordan reports drinking three to five alcoholic drinks on about one evening per weekend. They have occasionally become intoxicated but report no withdrawal, tolerance, daily use, or repeated alcohol-related academic or legal problems. Jordan notes that hopelessness sometimes feels stronger when intoxicated.
Jordan agrees to participate in safety planning and to make the unused medication less accessible. They say they prefer structured counseling with concrete skills and between-session practice.
Jordan tells the counselor, “I need you to understand that I'm nonbinary, but I don't want every problem I have explained by my gender.”
Q18. Which safety plan is BEST matched to Jordan's current presentation?
A. Identify warning signs, internal coping steps, usable people and settings, professional/crisis resources, medication-access steps, and clear thresholds for urgent reassessment or emergency care.
B. Have the close friend hold Jordan's medications and check in each evening, with Jordan agreeing to contact the counselor whenever suicidal thoughts increase.
C. Increase counseling frequency and provide crisis contacts, adding a formal safety plan only if Jordan develops active intent or begins preparing for an overdose.
D. Focus the plan on removing medication access and avoiding alcohol because those are the two modifiable factors most directly associated with overdose risk.
Why the keyed answer is best: A complete safety plan links warning signs with internal coping, usable supports or settings, professional/crisis resources, means-safety actions, and escalation thresholds. SAMHSA's current safety-planning framework similarly organizes intervention around warning signs, coping strategies, supportive contacts, professional resources, and making the environment safer.
Why B is less appropriate: appropriately activates a reliable support and reduces medication access, but it makes the friend and counselor disproportionately responsible for ongoing safety.
Why C is less appropriate: includes monitoring and crisis access but postpones formal planning until risk becomes more acute.
Why D is less appropriate: targets two important modifiable factors but is too narrow to function as Jordan's complete response pathway.
Transfer rule: Safety planning is a layered response system, not one promise, one support person, or one means-restriction action.
Q19. Which diagnosis is BEST supported by the information now available?
A. Adjustment disorder with depressed mood because Jordan's symptoms began after family rejection and remain closely linked to an identifiable psychosocial stressor.
B. Major depressive disorder, single episode, because a sustained depressive syndrome with anhedonia, neurovegetative and cognitive symptoms, suicidality, and impairment is present across contexts.
C. Alcohol-induced depressive disorder because alcohol sometimes increases hopelessness and therefore may account for the severity of the depressive and suicidal symptoms.
D. Bipolar II disorder, current depressive episode, because Jordan's age and depressive severity require continued consideration of bipolar-spectrum illness despite no established hypomanic episode.
Why the keyed answer is best: Jordan now has a sustained syndrome involving depressed mood, anhedonia, insomnia, low energy, appetite change, concentration difficulty, guilt/worthlessness, suicidal thinking, and meaningful impairment across more than two weeks. NIMH describes major depression as depressed mood or loss of interest occurring most of the time for at least two weeks with interference in daily activities.
Why A is less appropriate: remains plausible because family rejection is a clear precipitating stressor. However, the case now establishes a broad depressive syndrome extending beyond individual family encounters rather than merely subthreshold stress-related symptoms.
Why C is less appropriate: is weakened because depressive symptoms occur during nondrinking periods and the alcohol pattern does not account for the full six-week syndrome; substance and mental-health symptoms can overlap and require comprehensive differential assessment.
Why D is less appropriate: requires evidence of hypomania that the case specifically does not supply.
Transfer rule: An identifiable stressor does not automatically make a depressive presentation an adjustment disorder when the evidence supports a full depressive syndrome.
Q20. Jordan says, “I don't want every problem blamed on being nonbinary.” Which counselor response BEST demonstrates sensitivity to gender issues?
A. “We can use the name and pronouns you want and explore how identity or rejection matters when you say it matters, without assuming it explains the depression.”
B. “Because the family rejection occurred before the depression, minority stress should remain part of the formulation even if you would rather focus on other issues.”
C. “We can keep gender identity outside the clinical formulation unless you bring it up, so treatment does not unintentionally pathologize your identity.”
D. “The campus LGBTQ+ center may help us determine which parts of your experience are identity-related and which are better understood as depression.”
Why the keyed answer is best: Jordan is explicitly asking the counselor not to make gender identity the universal explanation for their distress. A respects Jordan's self-defined identity and language while leaving room to examine family rejection and other gender-related context when Jordan identifies it as relevant.
