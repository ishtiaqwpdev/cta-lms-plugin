CASE 1 — MAYA R.
SECTION 1 — INITIAL PRESENTATION

During the past eight weeks, she has experienced six sudden episodes of intense fear accompanied by racing heart, chest tightness, trembling, shortness of breath, dizziness, and a fear that she might faint or lose control. The symptoms reach peak intensity within several minutes and then gradually subside.
Maya has twice gone to an emergency department because she feared she was having a medical emergency. Those evaluations did not identify an acute cardiac or respiratory condition. She has been advised to obtain follow-up medical care if symptoms change or new warning signs occur.

Maya usually sleeps about five hours on work nights. She drinks energy drinks most mornings and sometimes again in the afternoon. She says they help her “push through” fatigue.
Maya worries that relatives may think counseling means she is weak. She also asks, “Can my principal or the school district find out that I'm seeing a therapist?” The counseling practice is independent of Maya's employer, and Maya has not authorized disclosure of counseling information to her employer.

CASE 3 — ELI P.
SECTION 1 — INITIAL PRESENTATION
Eli is a 19-year-old community-college student. His aunt accompanies him to counseling because she has noticed a marked change during the past two weeks.
Before the recent change, Eli regularly attended classes, worked approximately 12 hours per week at a bookstore, slept about seven hours most nights, and spent time with several friends.
During the past two weeks, Eli has become increasingly suspicious. He says videos and comments he sees online sometimes contain messages intended specifically for him and worries that people online may know where he lives. He has stopped attending several classes and spends long periods reviewing posts and videos for clues.
Eli appears guarded and distracted during the interview. He has recently been sleeping three to four hours per night because he stays awake monitoring online activity. He says he feels tired during the day rather than unusually energetic.
Eli reports using a high-potency THC vape or concentrate several times per day. He has used cannabis daily for approximately nine months and says his use has increased during the past few months. He also reports taking stimulant pills obtained from a friend twice during the past week “to study,” but the exact timing of those doses relative to the behavioral change has not yet been established.
Eli denies current suicidal thoughts. When discussing his fear about people coming to the house, he says, “If they show up, I might have to defend myself.”
His aunt reports that since becoming fearful, Eli moved a baseball bat from a closet and has been keeping it near the front door. Eli says he has not gone looking for anyone. When asked whether he is afraid of a specific known person, he says, “I don't know who they are.”
Eli is reluctant to seek additional care but agrees that his aunt can transport him for a same-day psychiatric and medical evaluation if the counselor recommends it. His aunt is available to remain with him.
Direct safety assessment clarifies that Eli believes anonymous people online may eventually find him, but he cannot identify a specific person. He denies having a plan to locate or confront anyone. He says the baseball bat is intended for protection “if somebody actually comes in.”
His aunt reports that Eli carried the bat to her car on one occasion because he was afraid someone might approach them while they were traveling. Eli has no known history of assault, weapon threats, or serious violence.
During the mental status examination, Eli is casually dressed with adequate hygiene. He is alert and oriented to person, place, time, and situation. He remains vigilant and guarded. His speech is understandable. His thought process is mildly tangential, and his thought content includes ideas of reference and persecutory beliefs concerning online material. He denies auditory or visual hallucinations and reports no command experiences. Attention is impaired. His insight into the possibility that his interpretations may be inaccurate is limited, and his judgment regarding the perceived threat and the baseball bat appears impaired.
Further substance assessment shows that Eli has been using high-potency THC concentrate multiple times daily for approximately nine months. His use increased during the past three months. There has not yet been a sustained period of abstinence during the current episode from which symptom persistence can be evaluated.
The stimulant pills were a friend's prescription amphetamine. Eli took the first pill four days after the suspiciousness had already begun and took another several days later. He reports feeling more keyed-up and sleeping even less after the second dose, but the referential beliefs clearly preceded stimulant use.
Eli denies other nonprescribed substances, heavy alcohol use, new supplements, or other medications likely to explain the presentation. Initial medical and neurological screening identifies no acute medical explanation.
Further mood assessment does not show a sustained elevated or expansive mood, grandiosity, unusually increased goal-directed activity, or reduced need for sleep without fatigue. Eli says he wants more sleep and feels exhausted.
There is no known prior psychotic episode. Because heavy cannabis exposure is ongoing and the episode is still recent, the treatment team does not yet have enough longitudinal evidence to determine with confidence whether the presentation represents a primary psychotic disorder or a substance-related psychotic condition.
Eli signs separate authorizations. His aunt may receive information needed to help with transportation, appointments, and agreed home-safety supports. The psychiatric and early-psychosis treatment team may receive clinically relevant assessment and treatment information.
Eli says, “I feel like everybody is lining up against me to prove I'm crazy.”
The same-day psychiatric and medical evaluation concludes that Eli requires brief inpatient stabilization because of the overall severity of the acute presentation and impaired judgment. He is subsequently discharged to a coordinated early-psychosis program.
Six weeks later, Eli has not used nonprescribed stimulants. He has chosen to remain abstinent from cannabis for the past four weeks while the treatment team continues the longitudinal assessment. His psychiatric medication is managed by the prescribing clinician.
Eli now sleeps approximately seven hours most nights. His suspiciousness and referential thinking have decreased substantially. He occasionally notices an online post that feels unusually significant to him, but he can now consider that the feeling may not mean the post is actually directed at him.
He no longer keeps the baseball bat by the door or carries it outside the home, and he reports no violent intent or plan.
Eli has returned to one college class and attends consistently. He hopes to add a second class next term and eventually return to part-time employment.
His aunt continues to help with transportation and appointments when Eli requests it. Eli wants to reconnect with one friend and is interested in using the college's academic-support office. He says he does not want his aunt involved in every treatment or school decision.
Eli tells the counselor:
> “I want to get back to school, know what signs to watch for, and not have my whole life become about this diagnosis.”
